The short version (2026): For deaths after June 30, 2022, the Indiana small estate affidavit covers a probate estate worth up to $100,000, after subtracting liens, encumbrances and reasonable funeral expenses. A distributee can use it 45 days after the death, with no court filing, to collect personal property. Vehicles and watercraft can be retitled through the BMV after just 5 days. Indiana real estate needs a separate affidavit recorded with the county recorder.

The Indiana small estate affidavit, often called the Distributee’s Affidavit, lets heirs collect bank accounts, investments and other personal property without opening a probate estate. It lives in Indiana Code 29-1-8-1, and the state doubled its limit to $100,000 for deaths after June 30, 2022.

Indiana’s version has two useful features: the limit is measured after funeral expenses and debts secured by the property, and cars and boats can move through the BMV almost immediately. This guide covers who qualifies, what the affidavit must say, and the separate path for real estate.

The Indiana limit depends on the date of death

IC 29-1-8-1 ties the limit to when the person died:

Date of deathAffidavit limit
Before July 1, 2006$25,000
July 1, 2006 – June 30, 2022$50,000
After June 30, 2022$100,000

The value measured is the decedent’s gross probate estate, less liens, encumbrances and reasonable funeral expenses. That net approach is more forgiving than states that use gross value: a $30,000 car loan or an $11,000 funeral bill both come off before the estate is compared with $100,000.

What the Indiana affidavit must state

Forty-five days after the death, anyone holding the decedent’s personal property must pay or deliver it to a distributee who presents an affidavit stating:

  1. The value of the gross probate estate, less liens, encumbrances and reasonable funeral expenses, does not exceed the applicable limit ($100,000 for deaths after June 30, 2022);
  2. Forty-five days have passed since the death;
  3. No application or petition to appoint a personal representative is pending or has been granted in any jurisdiction;
  4. The names and addresses of each distributee and the share each is entitled to;
  5. That the affiant has notified each distributee of the affiant’s intent to present the affidavit; and
  6. That the affiant is entitled to receive the property on behalf of the distributees.

Which state’s law applies matters too. If the decedent lived in Indiana, Indiana rules govern their personal property wherever it is held, so an Indiana affidavit can be presented to an out-of-state bank. If they lived in another state, that state’s law generally decides who inherits their personal property, so start with the home state’s small estate procedure and ask the Indiana holder what it will accept. Real estate always follows the law of the state where the land sits.

The Indiana courts’ Coalition for Court Access publishes a standard fillable version, and the state forms site carries the Distributee’s Affidavit for Disposition of Small Estates. Sign before a notary and attach a certified death certificate.

What counts toward $100,000

Counted: the probate estate, meaning property in the decedent’s sole name with no built-in transfer at death: bank and credit union accounts without a beneficiary, brokerage accounts, stocks, money owed to the decedent, valuables, and real estate in their sole name.

Subtracted: liens and encumbrances on the property (such as a car loan or mortgage) and reasonable funeral expenses.

Left out: jointly held property with survivorship, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, trust assets, and real estate covered by a recorded Indiana transfer on death deed.

Cars and boats: 5 days through the BMV

Indiana treats vehicles and watercraft more quickly than other property. Under IC 29-1-8-1, a motor vehicle or watercraft can be transferred through the Bureau of Motor Vehicles once five days have passed since the death, on an affidavit meeting the statute’s conditions. That lets a family retitle a car long before the 45-day affidavit for bank accounts is available.

Real estate: the separate recorded affidavit

The 29-1-8-1 affidavit reaches personal property only; it cannot transfer Indiana land in the decedent’s sole name. For real estate in a small estate, IC 29-1-8-3 provides a separate affidavit that is recorded with the county recorder where the property sits. It uses the same value test ($100,000 for deaths after June 30, 2022, less liens, encumbrances and funeral expenses) and must include:

  • the legal description of the real estate;
  • a statement that the estate does not exceed the applicable value;
  • the names of the people entitled to an interest and the share each receives; and
  • an explanation of how those shares were determined.

Because title companies must rely on this document later, many Indiana families use an attorney or a title company for the real estate affidavit even when they handle the bank accounts themselves. When real estate is involved, check with the recorder’s office and a title company about what they will accept.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Order certified death certificates.
  2. Retitle vehicles through the BMV if you want to (available after 5 days).
  3. List all assets and mark which are probate assets.
  4. Subtract liens and funeral expenses and confirm the result is $100,000 or less.
  5. Identify every distributee under the will or Indiana intestacy law, and notify each that you will present the affidavit.
  6. Wait 45 days after the death, then sign the affidavit before a notary.
  7. Present it with a death certificate to each bank or holder.
  8. Distribute the property to the distributees in the shares stated.
  9. Record a real property affidavit separately, if there is Indiana land.

A worked example

An Indianapolis man died in 2026 without a will, survived by two daughters. He left a checking account ($24,000), a brokerage account ($61,000), a truck worth $28,000 with a $19,000 loan, and a funeral bill of $12,000 that one daughter paid.

  • Gross probate estate: $24,000 + $61,000 + $28,000 = $113,000.
  • Less the truck loan: $113,000 − $19,000 = $94,000.
  • Less reasonable funeral expenses: $94,000 − $12,000 = $82,000, under $100,000.

After five days, the daughters can retitle the truck through the BMV. After 45 days, one daughter signs the affidavit, having notified her sister, and collects both accounts. She reimburses herself for the funeral, then splits the rest with her sister according to Indiana intestacy law.

After you collect: debts and responsibility

The affidavit moves property without a court, but it does not erase the decedent’s debts. Banks and other holders that pay in good faith on a proper affidavit are protected. The people who receive the property are the ones who remain answerable if a creditor, an overlooked heir, or a later-appointed personal representative has a better claim to it.

In practice, that means three habits protect the affiant:

  • Pay known debts before distributing. Final medical bills, the last credit card statement and utilities are the usual items. Keep receipts.
  • Keep the money traceable. Deposit collected funds into one account rather than mixing them with your own until distribution is done.
  • Document the notice. The affidavit says you notified every distributee. Keep copies of the letters, emails or texts you sent and the dates.

If a creditor or relative raises a claim after distribution, those records show what came in, what was paid and who received what, which is exactly what a court would ask for.

Common mistakes with Indiana small estates

  • Using the wrong limit for the date of death. A death in May 2022 is measured against $50,000, not $100,000.
  • Forgetting the deductions. Families sometimes reject the affidavit because the gross total is over $100,000, when liens and funeral expenses would bring it under.
  • Signing before day 45. The affidavit must state that 45 days have passed; banks will reject one signed early.
  • Skipping the notice to other distributees. It is a required statement, not a courtesy.
  • Trying to move land with the personal property affidavit. Indiana real estate needs its own recorded affidavit or probate.
  • Waiting 45 days for the car. Vehicles and watercraft can go through the BMV after 5 days.

When the affidavit does not fit

  • Over $100,000 after deductions: Indiana probate, often as an unsupervised administration, which is less court-intensive. Our probate cost estimator helps you plan costs.
  • Disputes among heirs: the affiant must notify all distributees and state their shares. A disagreement belongs in court.
  • Planning ahead: Indiana’s transfer on death deeds and beneficiary designations keep most assets out of probate. See our transfer on death deed guide.

How Indiana compares

Indiana’s $100,000 limit is above the national middle value of $75,000, and measuring it after liens and funeral costs makes it one of the more generous in the Midwest. Its 45-day wait is longer than the usual 30. Neighbors: Illinois allows $150,000 with no wait; Ohio uses a court release at $35,000; Michigan sits at $53,000 for 2026. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Indiana small estate affidavit limit?

$100,000 for deaths after June 30, 2022, measured as the gross probate estate less liens, encumbrances and reasonable funeral expenses. For deaths from July 1, 2006 through June 30, 2022, the limit is $50,000; before that, $25,000.

How long do I have to wait in Indiana?

45 days after the death for the small estate affidavit. Vehicles and watercraft can be transferred through the BMV after 5 days.

Does the Indiana small estate affidavit have to be filed with the court?

No. It goes directly to the banks and other holders. A separate real property affidavit, if needed, is recorded with the county recorder.

Can I transfer a house with the Indiana small estate affidavit?

Not with the IC 29-1-8-1 affidavit, which covers personal property. Real estate in a qualifying small estate uses a separate affidavit under IC 29-1-8-3 that is recorded in the county where the property is located.

Do funeral costs count toward the $100,000?

No. Reasonable funeral expenses are subtracted, along with liens and encumbrances, before the estate is compared with the limit.

Do I have to notify the other heirs?

Yes. The affidavit must state that you notified each distributee that you intend to present it, and it must list every distributee and their share.
