The short version (2026): For deaths on or after August 15, 2025, the Illinois small estate affidavit covers personal property worth up to $150,000 (it was $100,000), and motor vehicles no longer count toward that limit. There is no court filing and no statutory waiting period, but the person cannot have owned Illinois real estate in their own name, and the affiant personally promises to pay the estate’s debts in the order Illinois law sets. Illinois courts must accept the Supreme Court’s statewide form.

The Illinois small estate affidavit lets a family collect bank accounts, investments and personal belongings without opening a probate case. You fill out a sworn form, attach the death certificate, and hand copies to each bank or company holding the property. Nothing goes to a judge.

It became much more useful in 2025. A change to 755 ILCS 5/25-1 by the 104th General Assembly raised the limit by half and took vehicles out of the count. This guide covers the new rules, what counts toward $150,000, the promises you make when you sign, and what to do when the affidavit does not fit.

Who can use the Illinois small estate affidavit

Under 755 ILCS 5/25-1, the affidavit works when all of the following are true:

  1. The gross value of the personal estate, not counting motor vehicles, is $150,000 or less (for deaths on or after August 15, 2025; $100,000 for earlier deaths).
  2. No letters of office are outstanding: no executor or administrator has been appointed.
  3. No petition for letters is pending or planned.
  4. The decedent did not own Illinois real estate in their own name at death. Real estate that already passed by joint tenancy or a transfer on death instrument does not block the affidavit.

Anyone who knows the facts can serve as the affiant, but in practice it is usually the surviving spouse or an adult child. The affiant does not need to be an Illinois resident; a nonresident affiant submits to the jurisdiction of Illinois courts and names an agent for service.

Unlike many states, Illinois sets no minimum waiting period after the death. You can use the affidavit as soon as you have the death certificate and have gathered the facts. The practical limit is the debt-payment promise below, which usually means waiting until the major bills are known.

What counts toward $150,000 in Illinois

The limit measures the gross value of the personal estate: property other than real estate that the decedent owned alone and that has no built-in transfer at death. Gross means loans are not subtracted.

Counted: checking, savings and investment accounts in the decedent’s sole name without a beneficiary; certificates of deposit; stocks and bonds held in their name; money owed to them; jewelry, collectibles and other valuables; final wages and refunds.

Not counted:

  • Motor vehicles, under the 2025 change. Vehicles can still be transferred with the affidavit, and the Secretary of State has its own heirship process for titles.
  • Accounts and policies with a living beneficiary: life insurance, 401(k)s and IRAs, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts.
  • Joint accounts with right of survivorship.
  • Trust assets.

If the decedent owned real estate in their own name, it is not simply left out of the count; it rules the affidavit out, because real property in sole name must pass through probate or another court procedure.

What the affidavit must say

Illinois requires specific content, which is why the statewide standardized form is the safest choice. The affidavit sets out, under oath:

  • The affiant’s name, address and relationship to the decedent, and the decedent’s name, address and date of death;
  • That no letters of office are outstanding and no petition is pending or contemplated;
  • That the gross value of the personal estate, excluding motor vehicles, does not exceed the limit;
  • Whether the decedent left a will, with a copy attached if so (the original must be filed with the circuit clerk of the county where the decedent lived);
  • A list of all assets and their values, all known debts including funeral and medical bills, and the names and addresses of the heirs or legatees and their shares;
  • That the affiant will pay the debts, in the order of priority Illinois law sets, before distributing anything; and
  • That the affiant will distribute the remaining property to the people entitled to it.

Sign before a notary, attach a copy of the death certificate (and the will, if any), and give copies to each holder. A bank or company that pays or delivers in good faith under a proper affidavit is released from liability.

The debt promise: paying claims in Illinois order

The most important part of the Illinois affidavit is the promise to pay the estate’s debts before distributing, and to pay them in the statutory order. Illinois ranks claims in classes. Class 1 includes funeral and burial expenses and administration expenses; later classes cover items such as certain surviving-spouse and child awards, debts owed to the government, and ordinary unsecured debts like credit cards and medical bills.

If there is not enough money to pay everyone, higher classes are paid in full before lower classes see anything. The affiant who distributes to family while higher-priority claims go unpaid can be personally liable for them. For that reason, most families using the affidavit first pay the funeral home, gather every bill, and only then distribute.

The affiant is also personally liable to anyone who is harmed by a false statement in the affidavit, and to any heir or legatee who is left out. Take time to confirm who inherits, especially in blended families where Illinois intestacy rules can surprise people.

How to use the affidavit, step by step

  1. Order certified death certificates.
  2. File the original will, if there is one, with the circuit clerk in the county where the decedent lived.
  3. List every asset and mark which are in the decedent’s sole name without a beneficiary. Confirm there is no Illinois real estate in their sole name.
  4. Total the personal estate, leaving out vehicles and beneficiary assets. Check it against $150,000 (or $100,000 for deaths before August 15, 2025).
  5. List all debts and sort them into the statutory classes.
  6. Complete the statewide small estate affidavit and sign it before a notary.
  7. Present copies to each bank, brokerage, employer or other holder with the death certificate.
  8. Pay debts in class order, then distribute what remains to the heirs or legatees named in the affidavit. Keep records of every payment.

A worked example under the 2025 rules

Suppose a widower in Peoria died in March 2026. He left a checking account of $38,000 and a brokerage account of $97,000, both in his name alone with no beneficiary, a 2021 pickup worth $31,000, a life insurance policy naming his daughter, and a house he had already deeded to his two children with a transfer on death instrument.

  • The house passed under the transfer on death instrument, so it is not part of the estate and does not block the affidavit.
  • The life insurance goes straight to the daughter and is not counted.
  • The pickup is a motor vehicle, so it no longer counts toward the limit, though it can still be transferred with the affidavit.
  • The counted personal estate is $38,000 + $97,000 = $135,000, under the $150,000 limit.

Under the pre-2025 rules, the same estate would have failed twice: $135,000 is over the old $100,000 limit, and the truck would have pushed the total to $166,000. The 2025 change is the difference between a notarized form and a probate case.

Before distributing, the affiant in this example would pay the funeral bill and any administration costs first, then the remaining debts in class order, and only then split what is left between the two children as the intestacy rules or will direct.

When the Illinois affidavit does not fit

  • The decedent owned real estate in their own name. The property needs probate unless it passed by joint tenancy or a transfer on death instrument. Our transfer on death deed guide explains how Illinois families avoid this next time.
  • The personal estate is over $150,000. Recount first; beneficiary accounts and vehicles are the most common overcount. If it is still over, Illinois probate follows, usually as an independent administration with limited court supervision.
  • Heirs disagree or cannot be located. The affidavit depends on the affiant’s sworn statement of who inherits. A contested or uncertain family tree belongs in court.

How Illinois compares

At $150,000, Illinois now has one of the higher affidavit limits, double the national middle value of $75,000 and ahead of neighbors Wisconsin ($50,000), Indiana ($100,000), Iowa ($100,000 from July 2026) and Missouri ($40,000). Its lack of a waiting period is unusual; most states require 30 days or more. Its exclusion of real estate is the norm. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Illinois small estate affidavit limit in 2026?

$150,000 in personal property, not counting motor vehicles, for deaths on or after August 15, 2025. For earlier deaths, the limit is $100,000 and vehicles count toward it.

Do I have to file the Illinois small estate affidavit with the court?

No. The affidavit goes directly to the banks and companies holding the property. The only court filing involved is the original will, if there is one, which must be filed with the circuit clerk of the county where the decedent lived.

How long do I have to wait to use a small estate affidavit in Illinois?

Illinois sets no minimum waiting period. Because the affiant promises to pay the estate’s debts in legal priority order before distributing, most families wait until the funeral home and major bills are known.

Can I use an Illinois small estate affidavit if there is a house?

Not if the house was in the decedent’s sole name at death. Real estate in sole name must go through probate. If the house passed to a joint tenant or through a transfer on death instrument, it is no longer part of the estate and the affidavit can still be used for the personal property.

Does the Illinois small estate affidavit need to be notarized?

Yes. It is a sworn statement and must be signed under oath before a notary. Attach a copy of the death certificate, and a copy of the will if there is one.

What happens if I distribute the money and a debt turns up later?

The affiant who signed is personally responsible for paying valid claims in the statutory order, up to the property handled under the affidavit. That is why it is safer to identify and pay debts before distributing to heirs.
