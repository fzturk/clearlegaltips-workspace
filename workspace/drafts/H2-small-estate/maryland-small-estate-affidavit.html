The short version (2026): Maryland does not use a small estate affidavit. It uses small estate administration through the county Register of Wills, for probate assets with a gross value of $50,000 or less, or $100,000 or less when the surviving spouse is the sole heir or legatee. A personal representative is appointed, one newspaper notice is published, creditors generally have six months from the death (or 30 days after written notice) to claim, and for estates opened after October 1, 2022 there is no Register of Wills fee. Real estate in the decedent’s name counts and can be handled in the estate.

If you are looking for a Maryland small estate affidavit, the process you need is called small estate administration. It is administrative rather than judicial: the Register of Wills in the county where the person lived opens the estate, appoints a personal representative and supervises a short, simplified process. Maryland’s registers describe it as having fewer requirements than a regular estate and very rarely involving a judge.

This guide covers who qualifies, what counts toward the $50,000 or $100,000 limit, how the notice and creditor rules work, and what happens to real estate.

Who qualifies for small estate administration

Under Estates and Trusts §5-601, an estate is opened as a small estate when the decedent’s probate assets have a gross value of $50,000 or less. If the surviving spouse is the sole heir or legatee, the limit is $100,000.

For small estate purposes, Maryland values property at fair market value less debts of record secured by the property at the date of death, to the extent insurance does not pay the secured debt. A house worth $180,000 with a $150,000 mortgage therefore counts as $30,000.

The threshold has risen over time. For deaths from July 1, 2000 through September 30, 2012, it was $30,000 ($50,000 for a spouse who was sole heir), and earlier periods used lower amounts, so older estates may be measured differently.

What counts: probate assets only

Probate assets are property in the decedent’s name alone with no built-in transfer at death: bank and investment accounts without a beneficiary, vehicles titled only to the decedent, personal belongings, money owed to them, and real estate in their sole name.

Not probate assets: jointly owned property with survivorship (including real estate held as tenants by the entirety), payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, and trust assets.

Maryland also requires anyone holding the decedent’s will to file it promptly with the Register of Wills in the county of domicile, even if no one plans to probate it. Filing the will is a separate duty from opening an estate, and it applies even when every asset passed outside probate.

How small estate administration works

  1. File the will (if any) with the Register of Wills in the county where the decedent lived.
  2. Petition to open a small estate. The Register’s sample packet includes the petition for administration of a small estate, a list of interested persons, and the small estate notice of appointment. You list the probate assets and their values.
  3. Appointment. The Register appoints a personal representative, the person named in the will or the person with priority under Maryland law.
  4. Notice. A small estate notice of appointment, notice to creditors and notice to unknown heirs is published once. Anyone who objects has 30 days from publication.
  5. Creditor period. Claims must be presented by the earlier of six months after the death or 30 days after the personal representative mails or delivers written notice to a creditor.
  6. Collect, pay and distribute. The personal representative collects the assets, pays valid claims and any inheritance tax, and distributes to the heirs or legatees.
  7. Close the estate with the Register’s required paperwork.

Fee: for small estates opened on or after October 1, 2022, there is no required fee to the Register of Wills. Estates opened earlier may owe a fee based on value. Newspaper publication is a separate cost.

Who becomes the personal representative

Every Maryland small estate has a personal representative, even though the process is simplified. The Register of Wills appoints the person with priority under Maryland law. When there is a will, that is the person the will names. When there is no will, or the named person cannot serve, priority generally runs to the surviving spouse and children, then grandchildren, then parents, then brothers and sisters, then other relatives, and after them creditors or other suitable persons.

People with equal priority, such as several adult children, can agree among themselves who will serve, and those with priority can consent to someone else being appointed. The Register’s packet includes the consent forms. Collecting those consents before your first visit usually means the estate can be opened in a single appointment rather than two or three trips.

What the personal representative does in practice

The small estate personal representative has the same core duties as in a regular estate, with less paperwork:

  • Take control of the probate assets using the letters issued by the Register of Wills: retitle or close accounts, retitle the car through the MVA, and secure any real estate.
  • Keep estate money separate, usually in an estate checking account opened with the letters.
  • Pay valid claims, funeral expenses and any inheritance tax before distributing.
  • Distribute to the legatees under the will or the heirs under Maryland intestacy law.
  • File the closing paperwork the Register requires so the estate can be closed.

Registers of Wills staff can explain forms and deadlines but cannot give legal advice. When heirs disagree, a creditor disputes a claim, or the estate owns unusual assets, a short consultation with a Maryland estates lawyer can prevent mistakes that are hard to undo after distribution.

Real estate in a Maryland small estate

Unlike many states’ affidavits, Maryland’s small estate administration includes real estate in the decedent’s sole name. The equity counts toward the limit, and the personal representative has authority to deal with the property as part of the estate, including transferring it to the heirs or legatees or selling it if needed. Real estate held as tenants by the entirety passes to the surviving spouse outside the estate and is not counted.

Maryland inheritance tax

Maryland also has an inheritance tax on property passing to more distant relatives and non-relatives. Transfers to close family, including the spouse, children and other lineal descendants, parents, and siblings, are generally exempt. The Register of Wills computes the tax as part of administration, so ask about it at the start if any beneficiary is a niece, nephew, friend or other non-exempt person.

A worked example

An Annapolis widow died in 2026 with a will leaving everything to her niece. Her probate assets: a checking account with $12,000, a car worth $9,000, and a small condo worth $140,000 with a $118,000 mortgage.

  • Condo: $140,000 − $118,000 secured debt = $22,000.
  • Total probate assets: $12,000 + $9,000 + $22,000 = $43,000, under $50,000.
  • Niece is not the spouse: the $50,000 limit applies, and the estate qualifies.

The niece files the will and a small estate petition with the Register of Wills, is appointed personal representative, and the notice is published once. She pays the final bills, the Register computes Maryland inheritance tax on her share (a niece is not an exempt relative), and she takes title to the condo subject to its mortgage before closing the estate.

Common mistakes in Maryland

  • Looking for an affidavit form. Maryland’s small estates run through the Register of Wills, with a personal representative appointed.
  • Using gross value for real estate. The small estate test subtracts debts of record secured by the property.
  • Forgetting the spouse limit. $100,000 applies only when the surviving spouse is the sole heir or legatee.
  • Distributing before the creditor period. Claims can be filed up to six months after death unless shortened by written notice.
  • Ignoring inheritance tax for nieces, nephews, friends and other non-exempt beneficiaries.

When a regular estate is needed

If probate assets exceed the limits, Maryland regular administration applies, with a probate fee based on the estate’s value and a longer creditor process. How Maryland compares

Maryland’s $50,000 limit is below the national middle value of $75,000, but its $100,000 spouse limit, its subtraction of secured debt and its inclusion of real estate make the process broader than the headline figure. Its administrative, registrar-led model is similar in spirit to New Jersey’s surrogate system. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Maryland have a small estate affidavit?

No. Maryland handles small estates through small estate administration with the county Register of Wills, which appoints a personal representative and supervises a simplified process.

What is the Maryland small estate limit?

$50,000 in probate assets, or $100,000 when the surviving spouse is the sole heir or legatee. Property is valued at fair market value less debts of record secured by it.

Is there a fee for a small estate in Maryland?

For small estates opened on or after October 1, 2022, there is no required fee to the Register of Wills. You still pay for newspaper publication of the notice and any inheritance tax that applies.

How long do creditors have to file claims in a Maryland small estate?

The earlier of six months after the death or 30 days after the personal representative mails or delivers written notice to the creditor.

Can a Maryland small estate include a house?

Yes. Real estate in the decedent’s sole name is a probate asset, its equity counts toward the limit, and the personal representative can transfer or sell it within the small estate.

Do I need a lawyer for a Maryland small estate?

Many families handle small estates without one, because the Register of Wills provides forms and guidance. The Register cannot give legal advice, so consider a lawyer if heirs disagree or the estate has unusual assets.
