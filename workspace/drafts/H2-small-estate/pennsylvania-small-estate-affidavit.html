The short version (2026): Pennsylvania has no small estate affidavit you hand to a bank. It has two shortcuts instead. First, a bank must pay accounts totaling $10,000 or less to the spouse, a child, a parent or a sibling who presents a receipted funeral bill (20 Pa.C.S. §3101). Second, estates with $50,000 or less in personal property, not counting real estate, can be settled by a petition to the Orphans’ Court (§3102), whose decree works like a court-approved distribution. Pennsylvania inheritance tax still applies to both.

Families who search for a Pennsylvania small estate affidavit usually find that the state does not have one in the usual sense. There is no sworn form you can sign and present to collect $40,000. What Pennsylvania offers is a direct-payment rule for small bank accounts and a short court petition for estates up to $50,000. Used together, they settle a large share of modest estates without a personal representative, letters, or a formal accounting.

This guide explains both routes, what counts toward the $50,000 limit, what the petition needs, and the one step no Pennsylvania family can skip: the inheritance tax.

Route 1: The $10,000 bank payment (§3101)

Section 3101(b) says a bank, credit union or savings institution shall pay the balance of a deceased customer’s accounts, when the total at that institution is $10,000 or less, to a family member in this order of preference:

  1. the spouse;
  2. any child;
  3. the father or mother;
  4. any sister or brother.

The family member must present either a receipted funeral bill or an affidavit from a licensed funeral director confirming that satisfactory arrangements have been made to pay for the funeral. There is no court filing, no waiting period in the statute, and no notary requirement for the family member in the statute itself, though banks commonly ask for identification and a death certificate.

The $10,000 applies per institution. A person with $8,000 at one credit union and $6,000 at a bank has two separate accounts under the limit. The bank is released as if it had paid a court-appointed representative. The family member who receives the money is answerable to anyone harmed by an improper distribution, so it must still go where the will or intestacy law directs.

The limit rose from $3,500 to $10,000 under Act 35 of 2013, and it has not changed since.

Route 2: The $50,000 small estate petition (§3102)

For estates beyond the bank rule, section 3102 lets the Orphans’ Court Division of the county where the decedent lived direct distribution without a formal administration when:

  • The decedent was domiciled in Pennsylvania; and
  • The gross value of their property is $50,000 or less, excluding real estate and anything paid under section 3101, but including personal property claimed as the family exemption.

Two features make this route more flexible than it first appears:

  1. Owning real estate does not disqualify the estate. The statute says the court’s authority to distribute personal property “shall not be restricted because of the decedent’s ownership of real estate, regardless of its value.” The house is simply outside the petition and passes another way.
  2. It works whether or not letters have been issued or a will probated. A family that already opened an estate can still switch to the petition if the personal property is small.

The court’s decree of distribution tells banks, transfer agents and registrars to recognize the people named in it, and it has the same effect as a decree after a formal accounting. For one year after the decree, an interested party can petition to revoke it, for example because of a missed creditor or heir.

What goes in the petition (Rule 5.50)

Pennsylvania Orphans’ Court Rule 5.50 governs the petition. It must include, among other items:

  • Each petitioner’s name, address and relationship to the decedent;
  • The decedent’s name, date of death and domicile;
  • A list of all assets other than real estate and section 3101 payments, with values;
  • All debts, including funeral and administration costs, and whether they were paid;
  • The proposed distribution, with names, addresses and relationships; and
  • A statement about Pennsylvania inheritance tax.

Required exhibits include an original death certificate, a copy of the will with proof it was lodged or probated with the Register of Wills, proof that inheritance tax was paid or is not due, original consents or joinders from interested parties, and, when relevant, a letter from the Department of Human Services about any Medicaid claim. Petitioners must give written notice to interested parties under Rule 3.5(b). Many counties publish their own local petition form and fee schedule; Bucks County is one example.

The family exemption: $3,500 off the top

Pennsylvania’s family exemption lets a surviving spouse, or in some cases a child or parent who lived with the decedent, keep $3,500 of the decedent’s property free of creditors’ claims (other than administration costs). The exemption is claimed on top of any inheritance the spouse receives. In a small estate petition, property claimed as the family exemption is included in the $50,000 count but can be awarded to the claimant in the decree.

Do not skip the inheritance tax

Pennsylvania is one of the few states with an inheritance tax, and neither shortcut avoids it. The tax is reported on Form REV-1500 and is generally due nine months after death, with a discount for paying within three months. Rates depend on who inherits: 0% for a surviving spouse and for transfers from a child 21 or younger to a parent, 4.5% for children and other lineal descendants, 12% for siblings, and 15% for others. Rule 5.50 requires proof that the tax was paid or is not due before the court will sign a small estate decree.

Many assets that skip the petition, such as joint accounts, can still be subject to inheritance tax, so include them when you work out what is owed. See our overview of estate and inheritance taxes by state.

A worked example using both routes

A widowed Allentown retiree died in 2026 without a will, leaving two adult children. At death he had $7,200 in a credit union savings account, $31,000 in a checking account at a bank, a $12,000 car, a brokerage account worth $15,000, and a paid-off rowhouse. Nothing had a named beneficiary.

  1. Credit union ($7,200). The total at that institution is under $10,000, so one of the children presents the receipted funeral bill and collects it under section 3101. It drops out of the petition.
  2. Remaining personal property. Checking ($31,000) + car ($12,000) + brokerage ($15,000) = $58,000. That is over $50,000, so the petition does not fit, and the family opens a regular estate with the Register of Wills for the personal property.

Now change one fact: the checking account holds $21,000. The total is $48,000, and the children can file a small estate petition for the checking account, car and brokerage account, pay the funeral and debts, and let the court distribute the rest. No personal representative is appointed and no formal accounting is filed.

In both versions the rowhouse sits outside the petition. It still needs its own route to clear title, and title companies generally want letters from the Register of Wills before they insure a sale. Inheritance tax at 4.5% applies to what the children receive, including the house, and the return is due nine months after the death.

What if the estate is over $50,000?

  • Recount. Real estate and section 3101 bank payments are already out. Joint accounts, payable-on-death accounts and beneficiary assets also pass outside the estate.
  • Use both routes. Paying small accounts under section 3101 first reduces the value that must go through the petition.
  • Open a regular estate. Pennsylvania probate starts with the Register of Wills, which can often issue letters the same day the petition is filed. Our probate cost estimator helps you budget.

How Pennsylvania compares

Pennsylvania’s $50,000 petition limit is below the national middle value of $75,000, and it requires a court decree rather than an affidavit. Its two advantages are the mandatory $10,000 bank payment, which many states lack, and the rule that real estate does not block the petition. Its biggest difference is the inheritance tax, which applies regardless of the route. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Pennsylvania have a small estate affidavit?

Not a general one. Pennsylvania uses two other shortcuts: banks must pay accounts totaling $10,000 or less to a close family member who presents a receipted funeral bill, and estates with $50,000 or less in personal property can be settled by a small estate petition to the Orphans’ Court.

What is the Pennsylvania small estate limit in 2026?

$50,000 in gross value for the small estate petition, excluding real estate and bank payments made under section 3101, but including property claimed as the family exemption. The limit has been $50,000 since Act 35 of 2013.

Can a Pennsylvania bank release money to family without probate?

Yes, when the decedent’s total at that institution is $10,000 or less. The bank must pay the spouse, a child, a parent or a sibling (in that order of preference) who presents a receipted funeral bill or a funeral director’s affidavit that payment arrangements were made.

Can I use the small estate petition if the decedent owned a house?

Yes. Real estate is excluded from the $50,000 count, and the statute says owning real estate of any value does not restrict the court from distributing the personal property. The house itself must pass by another method, such as the will through probate, joint ownership, or a deed.

Do I still owe Pennsylvania inheritance tax on a small estate?

Yes, if the heirs are taxable. The rate is 0% for a surviving spouse, 4.5% for children and other lineal descendants, 12% for siblings and 15% for others. The court requires proof that the tax was paid or not due before approving a small estate petition.

How long does a small estate petition take in Pennsylvania?

It depends on the county and on how quickly heirs sign consents and the inheritance tax is resolved. The petition itself is short, and the court may decide it with or without an appraisal and with whatever notice it directs, which is why it is usually much faster than a full administration.
