The short version (2026): The Washington small estate affidavit (RCW 11.62.010) covers a probate estate worth up to $100,000 after liens, not counting the surviving spouse’s community property half. It can be used 40 days after the death by a successor who has given the other successors 10 days’ written notice and paid or provided for the decedent’s debts. There is no court filing, but a copy goes to DSHS. It collects personal property only, and it is available only if the decedent lived in Washington.

Washington’s small estate affidavit is a straightforward tool with a few steps that are easy to miss. The limit of $100,000 is fairly generous, the process stays out of court, and banks, transfer agents and the Department of Licensing must honor a proper affidavit. But the statute also requires written notice to every other successor, payment of the decedent’s debts, and a copy mailed to the state’s Office of Financial Recovery.

This guide explains each requirement, what counts toward the $100,000, how community property changes the math, and what to do about real estate.

Who can use the Washington affidavit

A “successor” under RCW 11.62.005, meaning a person entitled to the property under the will or the intestacy laws, can use the affidavit when:

  • the decedent was a Washington resident at death;
  • at least 40 days have passed since the death;
  • no personal representative has been appointed or applied for anywhere; and
  • the estate subject to probate is within the $100,000 limit.

What the affidavit must state (all nine items)

  1. The successor’s name and address, and that they are a successor as defined in RCW 11.62.005;
  2. That the decedent was a Washington resident at death;
  3. That the decedent’s entire estate subject to probate, wherever located, less liens and encumbrances and not including the surviving spouse’s or domestic partner’s community property interest, does not exceed $100,000;
  4. That 40 days have passed since the death;
  5. That no application or petition for a personal representative is pending or has been granted;
  6. That all debts of the decedent, including funeral and burial expenses, have been paid or provided for;
  7. A description of the personal property claimed and a statement that it is subject to probate;
  8. That the successor gave written notice, by personal service or mail, to all other successors, identifying the claim and the property, and that at least 10 days have passed since the notice; and
  9. That the successor is personally entitled to the property, or is claiming it on behalf of and with the written authority of all other successors with an interest.

Present the signed affidavit with proof of death. Banks and holders must pay or deliver; transfer agents must re-register securities; and any government agency that issues certificates of ownership, such as for vehicles or boats, must issue new ones to the successor. No tax release is required first.

The DSHS copy. RCW 11.62.010(5) requires that a copy of the affidavit, including the decedent’s Social Security number, be mailed to the Department of Social and Health Services, Office of Financial Recovery. The state uses it to identify estate recovery claims, for example for long-term care paid by Medicaid.

What counts toward $100,000

The test is the entire estate subject to probate, wherever located, less liens and encumbrances.

Counted: bank and investment accounts in the decedent’s sole name without a beneficiary, vehicles, household goods and valuables, money owed to the decedent, and the equity in real estate in the decedent’s name. Real estate cannot be transferred by the affidavit, but its equity still counts toward the limit.

Not counted: non-probate assets such as joint accounts with survivorship, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, trust assets, real estate passing by a recorded transfer on death deed, and the surviving spouse’s or domestic partner’s own community property half.

Community property changes the math

Washington is a community property state. Most property acquired during a marriage belongs half to each spouse. When one spouse dies, only the decedent’s half is part of their estate, and the statute expressly excludes the survivor’s half from the $100,000 test. A married couple with $150,000 in community savings has a decedent’s share of $75,000, which fits.

Many Washington couples also sign a community property agreement that passes all community property to the survivor at death without probate. If one exists and was properly executed, the survivor may not need the affidavit at all for community assets.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Order certified death certificates.
  2. Separate non-probate assets and the surviving spouse’s community half.
  3. Total the probate estate less liens, including real estate equity. Confirm it is $100,000 or less.
  4. Pay or provide for all debts, including the funeral.
  5. Send written notice to every other successor, by personal service or mail, describing your claim and the property.
  6. Wait until at least 40 days after the death and 10 days after the notice.
  7. Sign the affidavit before a notary with all nine statements.
  8. Mail a copy to DSHS Office of Financial Recovery, with the decedent’s Social Security number.
  9. Present it with proof of death to each holder, and to the Department of Licensing for vehicle titles.
  10. Distribute to the other successors as the will or intestacy law requires.

A worked example

A Spokane woman died in 2026 without a will, survived by her husband and two adult children from their marriage. During the marriage they acquired a savings account ($90,000), a car ($16,000) and a paid-off house held with a recorded transfer on death deed naming the husband. She also owned a brokerage account worth $22,000 that she had inherited before the marriage (her separate property).

  • House: passes by the transfer on death deed. Not counted.
  • Community savings and car: her half is $45,000 + $8,000 = $53,000; her husband’s half is excluded.
  • Her separate brokerage account: $22,000, counted in full.
  • Estate subject to probate: $75,000, under $100,000.

Her husband pays the funeral and final bills, mails written notice to both children, waits 40 days from the death and 10 days from the notice, signs the affidavit with the children’s written authority, mails a copy to DSHS, and collects the accounts. Washington intestacy law then decides how her share is divided between him and the children.

Who inherits in a Washington small estate without a will

The affidavit moves property to the successors, but the intestacy statute, RCW 11.04.015, decides who they are and in what shares. The rules for a surviving spouse or registered domestic partner are what most families need:

  • Community property: the survivor receives all of the decedent’s share of the net community estate.
  • Separate property, with children or other descendants: the survivor receives one-half; the descendants share the other half.
  • Separate property, no descendants but a parent or a parent’s descendants: the survivor receives three-quarters.
  • Separate property, none of those relatives: the survivor receives all of it.

In the Spokane example above, the husband receives all of his wife’s half of the community savings and car, and one-half of her separate brokerage account; the two children share the other half of the brokerage account. When the affidavit is signed on behalf of all successors, their written authority should be attached or kept with the file.

When there is a will, the will controls, but the affidavit still requires written notice to the other successors and the same 10-day wait. If the will names someone as executor, that person does not have to be appointed for the affidavit to work; the successors simply need to agree on who presents it and receives the property on everyone’s behalf.

Real estate: what to do instead

The affidavit collects personal property only. For real estate in the decedent’s sole name, options include a community property agreement (for spouses), a transfer on death deed recorded before death, or a probate with nonintervention powers, which in Washington lets the personal representative act with little court supervision. Common mistakes in Washington

  • Skipping the 10-day notice. The affidavit must state that notice was given and 10 days have passed.
  • Not paying debts first. The statute requires that all debts, including the funeral, be paid or provided for.
  • Forgetting DSHS. A copy with the decedent’s Social Security number must be mailed to the Office of Financial Recovery.
  • Counting the spouse’s half. The survivor’s community property interest is excluded from the $100,000 test.
  • Using it for a non-resident. The affidavit is only for decedents who lived in Washington.

How Washington compares

Washington’s $100,000 limit is above the national middle value of $75,000, and its exclusion of the survivor’s community half makes it more generous for married couples than the headline number suggests. Its 40-day wait is longer than the common 30. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Washington small estate affidavit limit?

$100,000 for the decedent’s estate subject to probate, less liens and encumbrances, not counting the surviving spouse’s or domestic partner’s community property interest.

How long do I have to wait in Washington?

At least 40 days after the death, and at least 10 days after you give written notice of your claim to the other successors.

Does the Washington small estate affidavit have to be filed with the court?

No. It is presented directly to banks and other holders. A copy must be mailed to DSHS, Office of Financial Recovery.

Can the Washington small estate affidavit transfer a house?

No. It collects personal property only, although real estate equity in the decedent’s name counts toward the $100,000 limit. Real estate usually passes by a community property agreement, a transfer on death deed, joint ownership or probate.

Do debts have to be paid before using the affidavit?

Yes. The affidavit must state that all of the decedent’s debts, including funeral and burial expenses, have been paid or provided for.

Why does DSHS get a copy?

Washington law requires a copy, including the decedent’s Social Security number, so the state can identify any estate recovery claim, such as for Medicaid-funded long-term care.
