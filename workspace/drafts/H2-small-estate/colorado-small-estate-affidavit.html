The short version (2026): The Colorado small estate affidavit (Form JDF 999, C.R.S. 15-12-1201) covers an estate worth up to $88,000 for 2026 deaths, measured at fair market value less liens. It can be used just 10 days after the death, the fastest of any state, with no court filing. It cannot transfer real estate. A holder who refuses a proper affidavit without reasonable cause can be liable for the family’s attorney fees.

Colorado’s small estate affidavit is one of the most practical in the country. The waiting period is only ten days, the form is short, the limit adjusts for inflation every year, and the statute gives families leverage when a bank drags its feet. The Colorado Judicial Branch publishes the official form, JDF 999, most recently revised on April 28, 2026.

The key detail is that Colorado sets its limit by the year of death. This guide shows each year’s figure, what counts toward it, how to fill out and use JDF 999, and what to do about real estate, which the affidavit cannot touch.

The Colorado limit by year of death

The value of all property the decedent owned and that passes by will or intestacy, wherever located, less liens and encumbrances, must not exceed the figure for the year the person died. JDF 999 lists:

Year of deathMaximum value
2026$88,000
2025$86,000
2024$82,000
2023$80,000
2022$74,000
2020 and 2021$70,000

Because the figure depends on the year of death, not the year you sign, an estate of someone who died in December 2025 is measured against $86,000 even if you use the affidavit in 2026.

What counts toward the limit

The test measures the total fair market value of all property subject to disposition by will or intestate succession, wherever located, less liens and encumbrances. That includes the decedent’s real estate equity, even though the affidavit cannot transfer it.

Counted: bank and brokerage accounts in the decedent’s sole name without a beneficiary, vehicles (net of loans), household goods and valuables, contents of a safe deposit box, money owed to the decedent, and the equity in real estate in their sole name.

Not counted: joint accounts and property with survivorship, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, trust assets, and real estate covered by a recorded Colorado beneficiary deed.

What JDF 999 asks you to swear

The affiant, who must be 18 or older and either a successor or acting for one or more successors, affirms that:

  1. at least ten days have passed since the death;
  2. the value of the estate, less liens and encumbrances, does not exceed the limit for the year of death;
  3. the affidavit is not being used to transfer real estate;
  4. no application or petition to appoint a personal representative is pending or has been granted anywhere;
  5. the listed successors are entitled to the property, with the amount or share each receives;
  6. how the property should be paid or delivered, before it is distributed to the successors;
  7. that anyone collecting on behalf of successors is their agent with an agent’s duties under Colorado law; and
  8. that anyone who receives property is answerable to a later personal representative or anyone with a superior right.

The affiant signs under oath before a notary. The form covers funds on deposit, safe deposit box contents, tangible personal property, and instruments such as stock certificates, debts owed to the decedent and livestock brands.

Why banks usually cooperate in Colorado

Two protections in C.R.S. 15-12-1202, printed at the top of JDF 999, make Colorado affidavits effective in practice:

  • Release: a holder that pays or delivers under the affidavit is discharged as if it had dealt with a court-appointed personal representative.
  • Fee-shifting: a holder that refuses to honor a proper affidavit without reasonable cause is liable for the costs, including reasonable attorney fees, incurred by the people entitled to the property.

If a bank refuses, a polite letter that points to these provisions often resolves the problem.

How to use JDF 999, step by step

  1. Order certified death certificates.
  2. List all property the decedent owned and mark non-probate assets.
  3. Value the probate property at fair market value, subtract liens and loans, and include real estate equity in the total.
  4. Compare with the limit for the year of death.
  5. Identify the successors and their shares under the will or Colorado intestacy law.
  6. Wait 10 days after the death.
  7. Complete JDF 999, listing each successor, the property and amounts, and how each item should be paid or delivered.
  8. Sign before a notary and present the affidavit with a death certificate to each holder.
  9. Distribute to the successors as the affidavit states, keeping records as their agent.

Nothing is filed with the court. The Colorado Judicial Branch also publishes JDF 998, a guide to collecting a decedent’s personal property, which walks through the same steps.

A worked example

A Colorado Springs man died in March 2026 without a will, survived by two adult children and no spouse. He left a checking account ($31,000), a brokerage account ($27,000), a truck worth $22,000 with a $9,000 loan, and a paid-off mountain cabin lot worth $15,000.

  • Truck equity: $22,000 − $9,000 = $13,000.
  • Total estate, less liens: $31,000 + $27,000 + $13,000 + $15,000 (lot) = $86,000, under the 2026 figure of $88,000.
  • The lot: counts toward the total, but JDF 999 cannot transfer it.

Ten days after the death, one child signs JDF 999 listing both children and their equal shares and collects the accounts and truck title. For the lot, the family needs a separate route, typically an informal probate or, if they qualify, another Colorado procedure for real property.

Who the successors are when there is no will

JDF 999 asks you to list every successor and the share each receives, so you need to know who inherits. When there is a will, it controls. When there is not, Colorado follows the Uniform Probate Code’s intestacy rules. The most common situations for small estates:

  • Spouse, and all of the decedent’s children are also the spouse’s children (and the spouse has no other children): the surviving spouse takes the entire estate.
  • Spouse, with children from another relationship on either side: the spouse takes a set dollar amount plus a fraction of the rest, and the decedent’s descendants share the remainder. The exact figures depend on the family facts, so check the statute or ask the court’s self-help center before filling in shares.
  • No spouse: the decedent’s descendants take everything, split by generation; if there are none, parents, then siblings and their descendants.

Getting shares right matters because the affiant acts as the successors’ agent. Paying one sibling too much and another too little is exactly the kind of error that makes the affiant answerable later.

When the family needs more than the affidavit

JDF 999 handles collection and delivery of personal property. It does not give anyone authority to sign tax returns for the estate, pursue a lawsuit, sell real estate, or negotiate with creditors on the estate’s behalf. If any of those are needed, an informal probate with a personal representative is the better tool, even for a small estate. Colorado’s informal process is handled by the court registrar without a hearing in most uncontested cases, so the gap between the affidavit and probate is smaller than in many states.

Real estate: what to do instead

JDF 999 expressly cannot transfer real estate. The statute has a narrow provision, C.R.S. 15-12-1201(3.5), for personal property that affects real estate, but title to land itself needs another route:

  • Beneficiary deed: Colorado allows a recorded beneficiary deed that transfers real estate at death outside probate. See our transfer on death deed guide for how these work.
  • Joint tenancy: real estate held as joint tenants passes to the survivor.
  • Informal probate: Colorado’s informal (unsupervised) probate is relatively quick for uncontested estates, and a personal representative can deed the property to the heirs. Our probate cost estimator helps you budget.

Common mistakes in Colorado

  • Using the wrong year’s limit. The figure is set by the year of death, not the year you sign.
  • Leaving real estate equity out of the total. It counts, even though the affidavit cannot move it.
  • Trying to transfer land with JDF 999. The form says in plain terms that it is not valid for real estate.
  • Using an outdated form. JDF 999 was revised on April 28, 2026; use the current version from the Judicial Branch.
  • Keeping money in your own account. The affiant acts as agent for the successors and should keep collected funds separate.

How Colorado compares

At $88,000 for 2026 deaths, Colorado sits above the national middle value of $75,000, and its 10-day wait is the shortest in the country; most states use 30 days and Virginia uses 60. Its annual inflation adjustment keeps the limit current, unlike states such as Missouri, whose $40,000 figure is fixed. See every state in our small estate affidavit limits by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Colorado small estate affidavit limit for 2026?

$88,000 for deaths in 2026, measured at fair market value less liens and encumbrances. The figure was $86,000 for 2025 deaths and $82,000 for 2024 deaths; use the amount for the year the person died.

How long do I have to wait to use a small estate affidavit in Colorado?

Ten days after the death, the shortest waiting period of any state.

Does the Colorado small estate affidavit need to be filed with the court?

No. JDF 999 is signed before a notary and presented directly to banks and other holders.

Can I use JDF 999 to transfer a house in Colorado?

No. The affidavit is not valid for real estate, although real estate equity counts toward the limit. Real estate passes by beneficiary deed, joint tenancy or probate.

What if a bank refuses to accept my Colorado small estate affidavit?

Under C.R.S. 15-12-1202(3), a holder that refuses to honor a proper affidavit without reasonable cause is liable for the costs, including reasonable attorney fees, incurred by the people entitled to the property.

Where do I get the Colorado small estate affidavit form?

From the Colorado Judicial Branch website: Form JDF 999, Collection of Personal Property by Affidavit, revised April 28, 2026. The companion guide is JDF 998.
