The short version (2026): Since July 1, 2025, the Virginia small estate affidavit covers a personal probate estate worth up to $75,000 (it was $50,000). It can be used 60 days after the death, must be signed by all known successors on the Supreme Court of Virginia’s form, and needs no court filing, although a will, if there is one, must first be probated. For any single asset worth $35,000 or less, the holder may pay a successor after 60 days without any affidavit. Real estate is never covered.

The Virginia Small Estate Act, in sections 64.2-600 through 64.2-603 of the Code of Virginia, lets heirs collect bank accounts, investments and personal property without qualifying as an executor or administrator. The 2025 General Assembly raised the limit by half, from $50,000 to $75,000, in Chapter 148 of the 2025 Acts, effective July 1, 2025.

Virginia’s version has two features that trip people up: it is signed by every known heir, not just one, and it has the longest waiting period of any state. This guide walks through both, plus the no-affidavit rule for assets of $35,000 or less.

Who can use the Virginia small estate affidavit

Under section 64.2-601, anyone holding a “small asset” must pay or deliver it to the designated successor on receiving an affidavit, made by all of the known successors, stating:

  1. The decedent’s entire personal probate estate, wherever located, was worth $75,000 or less on the date of death;
  2. At least 60 days have passed since the death;
  3. No application to appoint a personal representative is pending or granted anywhere;
  4. The decedent’s will, if any, was duly probated;
  5. The claiming successor is entitled to the asset, and on what basis;
  6. The names and addresses of all successors, to the extent known;
  7. The name of the designated successor who will receive the asset on behalf of everyone; and
  8. That the designated successor has a fiduciary duty to safeguard the asset and promptly pay or deliver it as Virginia law requires.

The affidavit must be on the form prepared by the Office of the Executive Secretary of the Supreme Court of Virginia. Several circuit court clerks post fillable versions.

What counts toward $75,000

The test is the decedent’s personal probate estate: personal property in their sole name that does not pass by beneficiary designation, survivorship or trust. The statute defines a small asset as anything owed to or owned by the decedent other than real property, and lists bank, credit union and brokerage accounts, securities, deposits, tax refunds and overpayments, tangible personal property, and instruments evidencing a debt.

Left out of the count: real estate (which the Act cannot transfer), joint accounts with survivorship, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, and trust property.

Unlike some states, Virginia measures the estate as of the date of death, so later growth in an investment account does not push an estate over the limit.

The $35,000 rule: no affidavit at all

Section 64.2-602 goes a step further for individual assets. Anyone holding a small asset worth $35,000 or less may pay or deliver it to any successor once 60 days have passed and no personal representative has been appointed or applied for. No affidavit is required.

Two points to note:

  • This is permissive. A bank may pay under section 64.2-602, but it can still insist on the full affidavit.
  • The successor who receives the asset has the same fiduciary duty to pass it on promptly to the other successors.

The 60-day wait and the will

At 60 days, Virginia’s waiting period is the longest in the country; most states use 30. Use the time to gather statements, identify every heir and, if there is a will, get it probated with the circuit court clerk. The Act requires that the will be duly probated, even though no executor qualifies and no estate is administered. Probating the will without qualifying an executor is a routine clerk’s office step.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Wait at least 60 days after the death.
  2. Probate the will, if there is one, with the clerk of the circuit court.
  3. Total the personal probate estate as of the date of death. Confirm it is $75,000 or less (for deaths on or after July 1, 2025).
  4. Identify all successors under the will or intestacy law, and choose a designated successor.
  5. Complete the Supreme Court of Virginia form and have every known successor sign it before a notary.
  6. Present it with a death certificate to each holder. Holders are discharged when they pay under it and need not verify its statements.
  7. Distribute promptly to the other successors. For a successor who is a minor or incapacitated, the Act allows payment to a conservator or guardian, a Uniform Transfers to Minors Act custodian, or another listed arrangement.

If a holder refuses a proper affidavit, the successor can bring a proceeding to compel payment and recover damages. Anyone who receives an asset remains accountable to a later personal representative or to a successor with an equal or better right.

A worked example

A Richmond widow died in August 2025 with a will leaving everything equally to her three children. She held a savings account ($28,000), a brokerage account ($39,000), a car ($8,000), and a townhouse in her own name. Her IRA named the children as beneficiaries.

  • IRA: passes by beneficiary designation. Not counted.
  • Townhouse: real estate is outside the Small Estate Act. It passes under the probated will; the children can record the will and later sell or retitle the townhouse, subject to Virginia’s rules on creditor claims against real estate.
  • Personal probate estate: $28,000 + $39,000 + $8,000 = $75,000. Because she died after July 1, 2025, that is exactly at the new limit and qualifies. Under the old $50,000 limit it would not have.

The children probate the will, wait out the 60 days, sign the affidavit together naming the eldest as designated successor, and collect the brokerage account. The bank may release the $28,000 savings account under the $35,000 rule even without an affidavit, and the car can be retitled through the DMV.

What the designated successor must do

Naming one designated successor is what makes the Virginia affidavit practical for banks: they deal with one person instead of every heir. That convenience comes with legal duties the statute spells out.

  • Safeguard the assets. Keep collected funds separate from your own money, ideally in a dedicated account, until they are distributed.
  • Pay promptly. The statute requires the designated successor to pay or deliver each successor’s share promptly as Virginia law requires. Holding funds indefinitely or using them personally breaches that duty.
  • Handle minors and incapacitated heirs correctly. Pay a conservator or guardian, a Uniform Transfers to Minors Act custodian or a custodial trustee, or, if none is known, an adult with care of the successor, or manage the share as a separate fund the successor can withdraw.
  • Keep records. Anyone who received an asset stays answerable to a later personal representative or to a successor with an equal or better right, so receipts and a simple ledger protect you.

Common mistakes with the Virginia affidavit

  • Signing before day 60. An affidavit dated earlier than 60 days after the death does not meet the statute, and holders will reject it.
  • Missing a successor’s signature. All known successors must sign. Leaving out a sibling who lives out of state is a common reason for delay.
  • Skipping probate of the will. When there is a will, the affidavit must state it was duly probated.
  • Using the old $50,000 figure. For deaths on or after July 1, 2025, the limit is $75,000; older forms and websites still show $50,000.
  • Counting non-probate assets. Joint and payable-on-death accounts, beneficiary designations and trust assets are not part of the personal probate estate.

When the Virginia shortcut does not fit

  • Over $75,000: a personal representative qualifies before the circuit court clerk. Our probate cost estimator helps with budgeting.
  • A successor will not sign: the affidavit needs all known successors. A disagreement usually means a formal administration.
  • Real estate only: the Act never covers real property. It passes under the will or by intestacy, and Virginia also allows transfer on death deeds for future planning; see our transfer on death deed guide.

How Virginia compares

At $75,000, Virginia now sits exactly at the national middle value, up from the bottom third before 2025. Its 60-day wait is the longest, and its all-successors signature rule is stricter than most states, where one heir can sign. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Virginia small estate affidavit limit in 2026?

$75,000 for the decedent’s entire personal probate estate, measured on the date of death. The limit rose from $50,000 on July 1, 2025, under Chapter 148 of the 2025 Acts of Assembly.

How long do I have to wait to use a small estate affidavit in Virginia?

At least 60 days after the death. The same 60-day wait applies to the separate rule letting a holder pay a single asset of $35,000 or less without any affidavit.

Who has to sign the Virginia small estate affidavit?

All of the known successors: everyone entitled to the property under the will or intestacy law. They designate one successor to receive the assets on behalf of all, and that person has a fiduciary duty to pass the shares on promptly.

Do I need to probate the will to use the Virginia small estate affidavit?

Yes, if there is a will. The affidavit must state that the will was duly probated. That means filing it with the circuit court clerk; it does not require qualifying an executor or opening a full administration.

Can the Virginia small estate affidavit transfer real estate?

No. The Act covers small assets other than real property. Real estate passes under the probated will or by intestacy, and heirs usually record documents in the land records to show the transfer.

Is there a Virginia small estate form?

Yes. The affidavit must be on the form prepared by the Office of the Executive Secretary of the Supreme Court of Virginia, which circuit court clerks make available.
