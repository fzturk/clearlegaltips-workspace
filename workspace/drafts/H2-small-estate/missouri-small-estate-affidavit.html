The short version (2026): The Missouri small estate affidavit (RSMo 473.097) covers an estate worth up to $40,000 after liens, debts and encumbrances, and it can include real estate. After 30 days, a distributee files it with the probate division, usually with a bond equal to the personal property’s value unless the court waives it, and pays a filing fee. If the listed property is worth more than $15,000, the clerk publishes a notice to creditors for two weeks. The clerk’s certificate then works like letters for banks and, once recorded, for real estate.

Missouri’s small estate affidavit has a low dollar limit, but it measures that limit generously and reaches further than most states. It subtracts not only liens but also the decedent’s debts before comparing the estate with $40,000, and it can transfer real estate through a recorded copy. Once the process is complete, the property is protected from later execution by the decedent’s unsecured creditors.

The trade-off is paperwork: a court filing, a probable bond, a filing fee, and sometimes a newspaper notice. This guide explains each piece so you can decide whether the Missouri affidavit or a regular administration fits better.

Who can use the Missouri small estate affidavit

Under RSMo 473.097, distributees can take the estate without letters when all of these are true:

  1. The value of the entire estate, less liens, debt and encumbrances, is $40,000 or less;
  2. At least 30 days have passed since the death, and no application for letters (or for a refusal of letters) is pending or granted;
  3. A bond is filed, in an amount not less than the value of the personal property, unless the court finds it unnecessary and dispenses with it; and
  4. The filing fee and, when required, the cost of publishing the notice to creditors are paid.

Who signs: if there is a will that was presented for probate within Missouri’s deadlines, the personal representative named in the will; otherwise, any distributee entitled to receive property, any time after 30 days from the death.

What the affidavit must state

  • That the decedent left no will, or that the will was presented for probate within the time limits of RSMo 473.050;
  • That all unpaid debts, claims and any estate taxes have been or will be paid, with the affiant’s liability for unpaid claims limited to the value of property received;
  • An itemized description and valuation of the decedent’s property (excluding property held as tenants by the entirety or joint tenants);
  • The names and addresses of the people who hold the property;
  • The names, addresses and relationships of the people entitled to receive each item after debts are paid; and
  • The facts establishing their right to it.

The clerk attaches a certificate naming the people entitled to the property and reciting that the will was probated (or none was presented) and that any estate taxes have been paid. The affidavit and certificate are filed with the probate division, and the clerk provides copies.

What counts toward $40,000

Missouri’s test is broader and more forgiving than most:

  • Included: both personal and real property in the decedent’s name.
  • Subtracted: liens, debts and encumbrances. A $60,000 house with a $35,000 mortgage counts as $25,000, and unsecured debts also reduce the total.
  • Excluded: property held as tenants by the entirety or as joint tenants; payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts; life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary; trust assets; and real estate passing by a recorded Missouri beneficiary deed.

The bond, the fee and the notice to creditors

Bond

The bond must be at least equal to the value of the personal property and is conditioned on paying the decedent’s debts (including debts to the State of Missouri), funeral and burial expenses, obeying future court orders, and delivering property to anyone who turns out to be entitled to it. The sureties’ liability ends unless proceedings are started within two years after the bond is filed. The court can dispense with the bond if it finds it unnecessary, so ask the clerk how your county handles waivers.

Fee

The filing fee is set by RSMo 483.580. Ask the probate clerk for the current amount in your county.

Notice to creditors

When the property listed in the affidavit is worth more than $15,000, the clerk publishes a notice to creditors once a week for two consecutive weeks in a qualifying county newspaper, at the affiant’s cost. The notice reminds creditors that Missouri bars claims one year after the death (RSMo 473.444) and that a creditor may ask to open a full administration.

Real estate: record the affidavit

Missouri’s affidavit can pass real estate. The distributees establish their right to the decedent’s land by recording a copy of the affidavit and the clerk’s certificate with the recorder of deeds in each county where the property sits. Once the procedure is complete, the property cannot later be taken in execution for the decedent’s debts, and the distributees’ right to it has the same effect as if a full administration had occurred. Secured creditors, such as a mortgage lender, keep their rights.

How to use the Missouri affidavit, step by step

  1. Order certified death certificates.
  2. If there is a will, present it to the probate division within Missouri’s deadlines.
  3. List all property with values, including real estate, and leave out survivorship, beneficiary and trust assets.
  4. Subtract liens, debts and encumbrances, and confirm the result is $40,000 or less.
  5. Wait 30 days from the death.
  6. Complete the affidavit, sign it before a notary, and file it with the probate division with the fee and a bond (or a request that the bond be waived).
  7. Pay for publication if the listed property exceeds $15,000.
  8. Obtain certified copies of the affidavit and certificate, present them to banks and holders, and record them with the recorder of deeds for any real estate.
  9. Pay debts, then distribute as the affidavit states.

A worked example

A Springfield man died in 2026 without a will, survived by one adult son. His estate: a house worth $85,000 with a $58,000 mortgage, a checking account with $9,000, a car worth $6,000, and $4,000 in credit card debt and medical bills.

  • House equity: $85,000 − $58,000 = $27,000.
  • Personal property: $9,000 + $6,000 = $15,000.
  • Less unsecured debts: $27,000 + $15,000 − $4,000 = $38,000, under $40,000.

After 30 days, the son files the affidavit with a bond of at least $15,000 (the personal property value) or asks the court to waive it. The listed property exceeds $15,000, so the clerk publishes the notice to creditors for two weeks. He pays the credit card and medical bills, collects the checking account and car title with the clerk’s certificate, and records the affidavit and certificate with the Greene County recorder of deeds, taking the house subject to its mortgage.

A related option for spouses and minor children: refusal of letters

The small estate statute refers to another Missouri procedure, the refusal of letters under RSMo 473.090. It is designed for a surviving spouse or unmarried minor children when the property they are entitled to as exempt property and family allowances would take up the estate anyway. In that situation the court can decline to open an administration and order the property delivered directly to the spouse or children.

The two procedures overlap but are not the same:

  • Small estate affidavit: available to any distributee, capped at $40,000 net, can include real estate, and usually needs a bond.
  • Refusal of letters: available only to a surviving spouse or unmarried minor children, depends on what they are entitled to take ahead of creditors, and is decided by the court on application.

If you are a surviving spouse with a small estate, ask the probate clerk which route your county uses more often and which paperwork it prefers. A pending or granted refusal of letters must be accounted for in the small estate affidavit, which is why the statute mentions it.

Common mistakes in Missouri

  • Forgetting to subtract debts. Missouri measures the estate less liens, debt and encumbrances, which often brings an estate under $40,000.
  • Skipping the bond question. A bond is required unless the court dispenses with it.
  • Not budgeting for publication. Above $15,000 in listed property, a two-week newspaper notice is required.
  • Not recording for real estate. Without recording the affidavit and certificate, the land records will not show the transfer.
  • Missing the will deadline. If there is a will, it must be presented within the statutory time limits for the named personal representative to sign.

How Missouri compares

At $40,000, Missouri’s limit is among the lowest in the country, well under the national middle value of $75,000. But because it subtracts debts and includes real estate, it can cover estates that would fail higher gross-value limits elsewhere. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Missouri small estate affidavit limit?

$40,000 for the entire estate, measured after subtracting liens, debts and encumbrances. Both real and personal property count.

Can a Missouri small estate affidavit transfer a house?

Yes. Distributees establish their right to real estate by recording the affidavit and the clerk’s certificate with the recorder of deeds in the county where the property is located.

Do I need a bond for a Missouri small estate affidavit?

Generally yes, in an amount at least equal to the value of the personal property. The court can dispense with the bond if it finds it unnecessary.

When is a notice to creditors required in Missouri?

When the property listed in the affidavit is worth more than $15,000. The clerk publishes the notice once a week for two consecutive weeks, at the affiant’s cost.

How long do I have to wait in Missouri?

30 days after the death, and no application for letters can be pending or granted.

Am I liable for the decedent’s debts?

The affidavit states that debts have been or will be paid, but your personal liability for unpaid claims is limited to the value of the property you receive.
