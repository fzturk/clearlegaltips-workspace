The short version (2026): North Carolina’s small estate affidavit, called collection of property by affidavit, covers personal property worth up to $20,000 after liens, or $30,000 when the surviving spouse is the sole heir (after the spouse’s allowance). You wait 30 days, file the affidavit with the clerk of superior court, collect with certified copies, pay debts in the statutory order, and file a final affidavit within 90 days. A surviving spouse who inherits everything can instead use summary administration, which has no dollar limit. The spouse’s year’s allowance is $60,000.

The North Carolina small estate affidavit has one of the lowest limits in the country, but it pairs that limit with two generous rules for surviving spouses. The result is that the right route in North Carolina depends less on how much the person owned than on who inherits.

This guide covers the collection-by-affidavit process under Article 25 of Chapter 28A of the General Statutes, the steps the clerk of superior court requires, the $60,000 spousal allowance, and summary administration for a spouse who inherits everything. The process is simple once you know which box your family falls into.

The North Carolina limits: $20,000 and $30,000

G.S. 28A-25-1 (no will) and 28A-25-1.1 (with a will) use the same limits:

  • $20,000 in personal property, less liens and encumbrances; or
  • $30,000 when the affiant is the surviving spouse and sole heir (or sole devisee under the will), measured after any spousal allowance paid under G.S. 30-15.

Only personal property is measured. Real estate is listed in the affidavit so the record identifies it, but it is not counted toward the limit and is not transferred by the affidavit. In North Carolina, real property generally passes directly to the heirs or devisees at death, subject to the estate’s creditors.

What counts toward $20,000

The limit measures the decedent’s personal property less liens and encumbrances. That means a car worth $15,000 with a $10,000 loan counts as $5,000.

Counted: bank and credit union accounts in the decedent’s sole name without a payable-on-death beneficiary, vehicles, stocks and brokerage accounts without a transfer-on-death registration, household goods and valuables, and money owed to the decedent, such as a final paycheck or a refund.

Not counted: real estate (listed in the affidavit but not measured), joint accounts with right of survivorship, payable-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, and property held in a trust.

Because the spousal and children’s allowances come first in the payment order, a small estate that is technically over $20,000 may still end up entirely with the surviving spouse once the allowance is assigned.

Who can file the affidavit

When there is no will, the affidavit can be made by an heir, a creditor of the decedent, or the county’s public administrator. When there is a will, the person named as executor, a devisee, an heir, a creditor or the public administrator can make it, and the will should be probated with the clerk. A person disqualified from serving as a personal representative under G.S. 28A-4-2 cannot use the procedure.

What the affidavit must state

  1. The affiant’s name, address and status (heir, creditor, executor named in the will, devisee or public administrator);
  2. The decedent’s name and residence at death;
  3. The date and place of death;
  4. That 30 days have passed since the death;
  5. That the value of all personal property, less liens and encumbrances, does not exceed $20,000 (or $30,000 in the spouse-sole-heir case);
  6. That no application to appoint a personal representative is pending or granted anywhere;
  7. The names, addresses and relationships of those entitled to the personal property; and
  8. A description of each tract of real property the decedent owned.

The North Carolina Administrative Office of the Courts publishes standard forms for the affidavit and for the final account of collection and disbursement; the clerk’s estates division can tell you which version to use.

How the clerk process works

  1. File first. Before collecting anything, file the affidavit with the clerk of superior court in the county where the decedent lived, and pay the fee set by G.S. 7A-307. The clerk indexes it with the estates and mails a copy to the people listed as entitled to the property.
  2. Collect with certified copies. Institutions must honor a certified copy of the filed affidavit. It is enough to transfer a car’s title and registration, bank and credit union accounts, and other personal property.
  3. Disburse in order under G.S. 28A-25-3: first the spouse’s and children’s year’s allowances, then debts and claims in the statutory priority order (or reimburse whoever already paid them), then distribute the rest under the will or the Intestate Succession Act.
  4. File the final affidavit within 90 days of filing the first one, showing what you collected and how you disbursed it. If you cannot finish in 90 days, file a report with the clerk explaining why.

The final affidavit is what separates North Carolina from states where the family never returns to the courthouse. Missing it can lead the clerk to require a formal administration.

The $60,000 spousal allowance and $10,000 child allowance

North Carolina gives a surviving spouse an allowance worth $60,000 for their support during the year after the death (G.S. 30-15), and each qualifying child an allowance of $10,000 (G.S. 30-17). These come first in the disbursement order, ahead of ordinary creditors.

The allowance matters for the small estate test too. In the spouse-sole-heir case, the $30,000 limit is measured after any spousal allowance paid. A spouse who takes the $60,000 allowance can therefore reach far more property than the headline figure suggests.

Summary administration: when the spouse inherits everything

Article 28 of Chapter 28A offers a separate route with no dollar limit. When the surviving spouse is the sole heir or sole devisee, the spouse can petition the clerk of superior court for summary administration. The petition states, among other things, the date and place of marriage, the real and personal property with probable values, that no personal representative has been appointed, and that the spouse is the only heir or devisee.

It is not available if the will forbids it or if the property passes to the spouse in trust rather than outright. When granted, the clerk’s order lets the spouse take the property without an administration, and the spouse generally takes on responsibility for the decedent’s debts up to the value of the property received.

A worked example

A Raleigh man died in 2026 without a will. His wife survived him, along with an adult son from his first marriage. He left a checking account ($14,000), a savings account ($9,000), a car worth $12,000 with a $7,000 loan, and a house owned jointly with his wife with right of survivorship.

  • House: jointly owned with survivorship, so it belongs to his wife and is not part of the estate.
  • Personal property less liens: $14,000 + $9,000 + ($12,000 − $7,000) = $28,000.
  • Who inherits: because he had a son, his wife is not the sole heir. The $30,000 spouse limit and summary administration are not available; the $20,000 limit applies.

At $28,000 the estate is over $20,000. But his wife is entitled to the $60,000 spousal allowance, which she can have assigned through the clerk and which takes priority over everything else. In practice, the allowance would absorb the entire $28,000. The clerk’s estates division can explain how the allowance assignment and a collection affidavit interact in her county.

Common mistakes in North Carolina

  • Collecting before filing. The statute requires filing the affidavit with the clerk before recovering any asset.
  • Forgetting the 90-day final affidavit. It is mandatory, and it is the most commonly missed step.
  • Paying heirs before creditors. The disbursement order puts allowances and debts before distributions.
  • Assuming the spouse is the sole heir. A child from another relationship means the spouse is not the only heir under intestacy.
  • Leaving out real estate. Each tract must be described in the affidavit even though it is not transferred by it.

When these routes do not fit

If the personal property exceeds the limits and the spouse routes are not available, a personal representative qualifies with the clerk and administers the estate. How North Carolina compares

North Carolina’s $20,000 limit is one of the lowest in the country, well below the national middle value of $75,000, and its filing-and-report process is more hands-on than affidavit-only states like Virginia. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small estate limit in North Carolina?

$20,000 in personal property, less liens and encumbrances. When the surviving spouse is the sole heir or devisee, the limit is $30,000, measured after any spousal allowance paid.

Do I have to file the North Carolina small estate affidavit with the court?

Yes. It must be filed with the clerk of superior court in the county where the decedent lived before you collect any assets, and you must file a final affidavit of collection and disbursement within 90 days.

How long do I have to wait in North Carolina?

30 days after the death before the affidavit can be used.

Can a surviving spouse avoid probate in North Carolina?

Often, yes. A spouse who is the sole heir or sole devisee can petition the clerk for summary administration, which has no dollar limit, unless the will forbids it or leaves the property in trust. A spouse is also entitled to a $60,000 year’s allowance.

Does the North Carolina small estate affidavit transfer a house?

No. The affidavit must describe the decedent’s real estate, but it collects personal property only. Real estate generally passes to the heirs or devisees at death, subject to the estate’s creditors.

What happens if I miss the 90-day final affidavit?

File a report with the clerk explaining the delay. Leaving it unfiled can lead the clerk to require the appointment of a personal representative and a formal accounting.
