The short version (2026): Small estate affidavit limits by state run from $15,000 in Rhode Island to $400,000 in Wyoming, and the middle state sits at $75,000. Georgia and New Hampshire use procedures with no dollar cap at all. In 30 states the affidavit cannot move real estate, most states make you wait 30 days after the death, and 13 jurisdictions changed their numbers in 2025 or 2026, so older charts are often wrong.

When someone dies with a modest estate, the family usually does not need a full probate case. Every state has a shortcut, most often a small estate affidavit: a sworn form an heir signs and hands to the bank, brokerage or DMV to collect the property directly. The catch is that each state sets its own dollar limit, waiting period and list of assets that count, and those limits have moved a lot since 2025.

This page lists the small estate affidavit limits by state for all 50 states and the District of Columbia, checked against each state’s own statute or court materials in September 2026. Use the checker below for a quick answer, then read the rules for your state in the table.

Key Findings From the 2026 Data

Putting all 51 jurisdictions side by side shows how uneven the shortcut is. An estate that qualifies easily in one state can require full probate one border over.

  • The typical limit is $75,000. Half of the 49 jurisdictions with a dollar cap allow less and half allow more. Twenty-two sit at $50,000 or below, and eighteen allow $100,000 or more.
  • Wyoming is the outlier on top. Its limit doubled from $200,000 to $400,000 on July 1, 2025, and Wyoming also offers a summary distribution route that reaches real property.
  • Rhode Island is the tightest. Its affidavit covers only $15,000, though tangible personal property such as furniture and cars is not counted toward that figure.
  • Two states have no dollar test. Georgia lets heirs ask the probate court to declare that no administration is necessary when they all agree and the debts are handled; New Hampshire offers a waiver of administration at any size.
  • Real estate is usually excluded. In 30 jurisdictions the shortcut cannot transfer a house or land at all. Eleven include real estate within the dollar limit, seven (including Arizona, California and Michigan) use a separate real-property procedure, and Texas allows only the homestead.
  • Most families never see a judge. In 28 jurisdictions the affidavit goes straight to the bank or other holder. Eleven require a filing with a court office, eleven are short court petitions, and Texas requires a judge to approve the affidavit.
  • Thirty days is the usual wait. Colorado is fastest at 10 days; Virginia is slowest at 60. California, Iowa, Nevada and Washington use 40 days, and Arkansas, Indiana and Tennessee use 45.

What Counts Toward the Limit (and What Doesn’t)

The number in the table is measured against the probate estate: property titled in the person’s name alone with no built-in way to pass at death. A lot of what people own never enters that pool.

Usually left out of the count:

  • Bank and brokerage accounts with a payable-on-death or transfer-on-death beneficiary
  • Life insurance, 401(k) plans and IRAs with a living named beneficiary
  • Property owned jointly with right of survivorship, such as a joint checking account or a house held as joint tenants
  • Anything already titled in a living trust
  • Real estate passing by a transfer on death deed in states that allow them

Usually counted: accounts in the person’s sole name with no beneficiary, a car titled only to them (though Alaska, Hawaii, Illinois, Massachusetts and Nevada treat vehicles separately), final paychecks and refunds, household goods, and in the states that include it, real estate in their sole name.

How value is measured also varies. Many states use value less liens and encumbrances, so a car worth $20,000 with a $15,000 loan counts as $5,000. Others, including Louisiana and New York, look at gross value. Texas measures the estate without the homestead and exempt property, which is why a Texas family with a paid-off house can still qualify. Affidavit, Filing or Petition: The Four Kinds of Shortcut

“Small estate affidavit” is the common name, but the table’s Court step column shows four different mechanisms hiding under it. Knowing which one your state uses tells you how much work is ahead.

1. Affidavit only (28 jurisdictions)

You complete the state’s form, sign it before a notary once the waiting period has passed, attach a certified death certificate, and present it to whoever holds the property. The bank or transfer agent pays out on the strength of your sworn statement. Colorado, Minnesota, Virginia and Washington work this way. Nothing is filed with a court.

2. File first, then use (11 jurisdictions)

The affidavit or statement must be filed with a court office before it works. New York’s voluntary administration runs through the Surrogate’s Court, Missouri files with the probate division, and North Carolina files with the clerk of superior court. Filing fees are small (New York’s is $1; Massachusetts charges $115 for its voluntary administration statement), but you will get a certified copy that institutions accept more readily.

3. Short court petition (11 jurisdictions)

Some states call their shortcut a petition because a judge or registrar signs an order. Florida’s summary administration, Ohio’s release from administration, Pennsylvania’s small estate petition, Maryland’s small estate administration and Tennessee’s petition for limited letters (rewritten in 2023) all fit here. They are still far faster than full probate, often a single filing with no hearing, but plan on a few weeks for the order.

4. Judge approval (Texas)

Texas asks for the most. The affidavit is filed with the court that would handle the estate, two disinterested witnesses swear to the family facts, and the judge must approve it before it has any effect. It is only available when the person died without a will.

How to Use a Small Estate Affidavit, Step by Step

The details differ by state, but the order of operations is nearly always the same.

  1. Get certified death certificates. Order several; each bank and agency keeps one.
  2. List every asset and how it is titled. Separate the probate assets (sole name, no beneficiary) from everything else. Only the first group is measured against the limit.
  3. Value the probate assets. Use statements dated near the date of death. Subtract loans if your state measures value less liens.
  4. Check the limit and the real estate rule for the state where the person lived. Use the table above. If real estate is involved and your state excludes it, the house needs another route.
  5. Wait out the required period. Count from the date of death.
  6. Complete the state form. Many states publish an official form (Colorado JDF 999, Michigan PC 598, Connecticut PC-212, Alaska P-110). Where there is none, as in California,, the affidavit must still contain the statements the statute lists.
  7. Sign before a notary and, where required, have witnesses sign too.
  8. File it if your state requires filing, then present copies to each institution.
  9. Pay debts and distribute. The person using the affidavit is generally responsible for handing the property to the people legally entitled to it, and may be liable to creditors and other heirs if they do not.

When the Estate Is Over the Limit

Going over the limit does not automatically mean a long, expensive probate. Check these first:

  • Recount. Families often include accounts that pass by beneficiary designation. Taking those out can bring the probate estate under the line.
  • Look for a spouse limit. Maryland ($100,000), Nevada ($150,000), New Jersey ($50,000), North Carolina ($30,000) and Ohio ($100,000) allow more when the claimant is the surviving spouse.
  • Use a separate real-property procedure where one exists (Arizona, California, Indiana, Michigan, Nebraska, South Dakota, Wyoming), or a recorded transfer on death deed.
  • Ask about summary or unsupervised probate. Many states have a middle path between the affidavit and full administration.

Common Mistakes With Small Estate Affidavits

  • Using the wrong state’s law. The rules of the state where the person lived control personal property. Real estate follows the state where the land sits.
  • Using an old limit. With 13 changes in two years, a chart from 2024 is unreliable for many states.
  • Signing too early. An affidavit signed before the waiting period ends is defective, and banks will reject it.
  • Leaving out an heir. Most affidavits require you to swear that you are entitled to the property or acting for everyone who is. Other heirs can come after you later.
  • Ignoring debts. The shortcut skips the court process, not the creditors. Unpaid debts can follow the property into the hands of whoever received it.
  • Trying to move real estate in a state that excludes it. A deed signed on the strength of a personal-property affidavit will not give clear title.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small estate affidavit limit in my state?

It ranges from $15,000 in Rhode Island to $400,000 in Wyoming, with $75,000 as the middle value across the 49 jurisdictions that use a dollar cap. Georgia and New Hampshire have procedures with no dollar limit. Find your state in the table above; each row links to the statute, and states that adjust for inflation show the figure for 2026 deaths.

Can a small estate affidavit transfer a house?

Usually not. In 30 jurisdictions the affidavit covers personal property only. Eleven states, including Arkansas, Missouri and Oregon, count real estate within the limit, and seven states use a separate procedure for real property. Texas allows only the homestead to pass this way. If the house cannot go through the affidavit, look at a transfer on death deed, joint ownership, or a separate real-property procedure.

How long do I have to wait after a death to use a small estate affidavit?

Most states require 30 days. Colorado allows 10 days, Michigan 28, California, Iowa, Nevada and Washington 40, Arkansas, Indiana and Tennessee 45, and Virginia 60. A few procedures, such as Florida’s summary administration or Maryland’s small estate administration, have no fixed waiting period but still take several weeks because a court or registrar must act.

Do life insurance and retirement accounts count toward the limit?

Generally no, as long as a living beneficiary is named. Those assets pass directly by contract and are not part of the probate estate. The same is true of payable-on-death bank accounts, jointly owned property with survivorship rights, and assets in a living trust. If the estate itself is named as beneficiary, or the named beneficiary has died, the asset can fall back into the probate estate and count.

Does a small estate affidavit need to be notarized?

In practice, yes. Nearly every state requires the affiant to swear to the statements under oath, which means signing before a notary. Texas also requires two disinterested witnesses, and states that require court filing may have their own signature rules. Banks and transfer agents will reject an affidavit that is not properly sworn.

Which states raised their small estate limits recently?

Thirteen changed a small estate number in 2025 or 2026: the District of Columbia, California, North Dakota, Maine, Virginia, Wyoming, Arizona, Illinois, Nevada, Delaware, Florida, Iowa and Louisiana. Several increases apply only to deaths on or after the effective date, so check the date of death as well as the new figure.
