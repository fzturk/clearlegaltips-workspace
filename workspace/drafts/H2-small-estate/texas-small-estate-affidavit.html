The short version (2026): A Texas small estate affidavit works only when the person died without a will, at least 30 days have passed, and the estate is worth $75,000 or less not counting the homestead and exempt property. Unlike most states, Texas makes you file it with the court and get a judge’s approval, sworn by every heir plus two disinterested witnesses. Filing costs about $360 in Dallas County. It can pass the family homestead, but no other real estate.

The Texas small estate affidavit is the state’s shortcut for settling a modest estate without appointing an executor or administrator. It is also the strictest version of the shortcut in the country. Where most states let an heir sign an affidavit and walk it into a bank, Texas routes it through the probate court and a judge.

That extra step is worth it for the right family. A court-approved affidavit gives you a certified document that banks, the DMV and title companies accept, and it can transfer a paid-off family home. This guide covers who qualifies, what counts toward the $75,000 limit, how the filing works in practice, and what to do when the affidavit does not fit. Every rule links to Chapter 205 of the Texas Estates Code.

Who can use a Texas small estate affidavit: 6 conditions

Section 205.001 lets the heirs (the statute calls them distributees) collect the estate without a personal representative only if all six of these are true:

  1. The person died without a will. The statute applies to a decedent “who dies intestate.” If there is a valid will, this affidavit is not available, even for a tiny estate.
  2. At least 30 days have passed since the death.
  3. No petition to appoint an administrator is pending or has been granted.
  4. The estate is worth $75,000 or less on the date of the affidavit, excluding the homestead and exempt property.
  5. The affidavit is filed with the clerk of the court that has jurisdiction over the estate, usually the county where the person lived.
  6. The judge approves it and the heirs then give certified copies to each person holding estate property.

A seventh practical condition sits inside the sixth: the estate’s assets, not counting the homestead and exempt property, must be enough to cover its known debts. Section 205.001 lets heirs take the estate only “to the extent the estate assets, excluding homestead and exempt property, exceed the known liabilities.” An estate that owes more than it owns is not a candidate.

The $75,000 limit: what counts and what doesn’t

The Texas limit is lower than it looks on paper, but it measures a narrow slice of what the person owned. Three categories fall outside the count.

1. The homestead

The family home that qualified as the person’s homestead is excluded from the $75,000 entirely, whatever it is worth. A Texas family can use the affidavit for a $300,000 paid-off house plus $40,000 in bank accounts, because only the $40,000 is measured.

2. Exempt property

Texas also leaves out “exempt property,” the personal property the law protects from creditors. The Property Code protects household furnishings, one vehicle for each licensed family member, tools and equipment of a trade, and certain other items up to an aggregate value of $100,000 for a family or $50,000 for a single adult. The asset list you file must mark which items you claim as exempt (Section 205.002(b)). Whether an item truly counts as exempt can depend on who survives the person, so this is the part of the form clerks and judges look at most closely.

3. Assets that pass outside the estate

As in every state, anything with its own built-in transfer never enters the estate: life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, payable-on-death accounts, survivorship accounts, and property already in a living trust. Leave them off the count.

What does count: bank accounts in the person’s sole name without a beneficiary, non-exempt vehicles, investment accounts without a transfer-on-death designation, final wages and refunds, and any valuables beyond the exemption limits.

Who signs: every heir plus 2 disinterested witnesses

Section 205.002 requires the affidavit to be sworn to by three groups:

  • Two disinterested witnesses: people who know the family history and will not inherit anything. A longtime neighbor or family friend is typical.
  • Each heir who has legal capacity. Every adult heir signs, not just one representative.
  • A guardian or next of kin for any minor or incapacitated heir, where the facts call for it.

The affidavit must also include a list of all known assets and debts, the name and address of each heir, and the family history showing why each heir inherits. Because Texas intestacy rules turn on details like whether the person was married, had children from another relationship, or owned community property, this family history section decides who gets what. If the facts are complicated, a court determination of heirship may be the safer route.

How to file: the court step, the fee and the county form

Texas probate filings are local, and so is the small estate affidavit.

  1. Get the county’s own form. Several counties, including Dallas and Travis, require their mandatory form and reject generic templates. Check the county clerk or probate court website for the county where the person lived.
  2. Wait 30 days from the date of death, then have all heirs and both witnesses sign before a notary.
  3. File with the county clerk’s probate division with a death certificate and the filing fee. Dallas County lists a $360 filing fee for small estates. Fees vary by county, so confirm before you go. A person who cannot afford the fee can ask about a statement of inability to pay.
  4. The judge examines the affidavit and may approve it if it meets Chapter 205 (Section 205.003). There is usually no hearing, but the court can ask for corrections.
  5. Order certified copies from the clerk. Section 205.004 requires the heirs to give a certified copy to each bank, company or agency holding estate property.
  6. Record it in the deed records if it transfers the homestead (see below).

Once approved, the affidavit becomes a local government record (Section 205.005), and institutions that pay out in reliance on it are protected. That protection is why Texas banks generally accept an approved small estate affidavit without further paperwork.

Passing the homestead with the affidavit

Texas is one of the few states whose small estate affidavit can move real estate, but only one kind. Under Section 205.006, if the homestead is the only real property in the estate, title to it can pass under the affidavit. The approved affidavit must then be recorded in the deed records of the county where the home sits.

Two limits matter. First, if the person owned any other real estate (a rental house, a lot, mineral interests), Section 205.008 says the affidavit does not transfer title to that property, and the family needs another route for it. Second, a buyer can rely on the recorded affidavit. An heir left off it can lose their claim against a good-faith buyer and be left to recover their share from the heir who sold. Getting the family history right protects everyone.

What if there is a will, or the estate is too big?

Texas offers other low-cost paths when the affidavit does not fit.

  • Muniment of title: When there is a valid will and the estate has no unpaid debts other than those secured by real estate, a court can admit the will to probate as a muniment of title. The order itself transfers the property, with no executor appointed. For small estates with a will, this is usually the cheapest court option.
  • Affidavit of heirship: For real estate beyond the homestead, heirs often record an affidavit of heirship in the deed records. It does not require court approval, but title companies decide whether to rely on it. See our affidavit of heirship template.
  • Determination of heirship: A court proceeding that declares who the heirs are. It costs more but settles disputed or complicated family facts.
  • Independent administration: Full probate, Texas style. It is less supervised than in many states, but it still means an application, a hearing and an appointed executor or administrator.

Liability: what you are promising when you sign

The affidavit shifts risk from the institutions to the family. Under Section 205.007, a bank that pays out on an approved affidavit is released as if it had paid a court-appointed representative, and it does not have to check whether the statements are true. The heirs who receive property remain answerable to anyone with a better right to it, and every person who signed is liable for losses caused by a payout made in reliance on the affidavit.

In plain terms: if you leave out an heir or a known debt, the people who signed can be on the hook. If a bank refuses to honor a valid approved affidavit, the heirs can sue to recover the property.

How Texas compares with other states

At $75,000, Texas sits exactly at the national middle value. It stands out for three other reasons:

  • It is the only state that requires a judge to approve the affidavit.
  • It works only for estates without a will.
  • Its homestead and exempt-property carve-outs let a family with a paid-off home and a car still qualify, which is impossible in the many states that count everything.

For comparison, California allows $208,850 with no court filing, while Florida uses a court-supervised summary administration up to $150,000. The full comparison is in our small estate affidavit limits by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a small estate affidavit in Texas if there is a will?

No. Section 205.001 applies only when the person died intestate, meaning without a valid will. If there is a will and no unpaid debts other than those secured by real estate, ask the probate court about probating the will as a muniment of title, which transfers property by court order without appointing an executor.

How much does a Texas small estate affidavit cost to file?

Fees are set by each county. Dallas County lists $360, due when you file. Add certified copies for each bank or agency and, if the affidavit passes the homestead, a recording fee in the deed records. People who cannot afford court costs can ask the clerk about filing a statement of inability to pay.

Does the house count toward the $75,000 limit in Texas?

Not if it was the person’s homestead. The homestead and exempt personal property are excluded from the $75,000 calculation. The affidavit can also transfer the homestead, as long as it is the only real property in the estate, by recording the approved affidavit in the county deed records.

Who are the two disinterested witnesses?

They are two people who know the family facts, such as marriages, children and deaths, and who will not inherit anything from the estate. Friends, neighbors and in some cases distant relatives who are not heirs can serve. They swear to the family history in the affidavit along with the heirs.

How long does the judge take to approve a small estate affidavit?

There is no statutory deadline. The judge examines the filing and may approve it if it meets Chapter 205, usually without a hearing. Busy urban courts can take several weeks, and incomplete asset lists or family histories are the most common reason for a request to correct and refile.

Can a small estate affidavit transfer a car in Texas?

Yes. A vehicle is personal property, and the Texas DMV accepts a certified copy of the approved affidavit for a title transfer. Vehicles can also fall within the exempt property that does not count toward the $75,000 limit.
