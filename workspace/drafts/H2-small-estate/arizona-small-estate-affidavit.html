The short version (2026): Arizona has two small estate affidavits. For personal property worth up to $200,000 (after liens), heirs can collect 30 days after the death with no court filing. For real estate worth up to $300,000 in equity, an affidavit of succession is filed with the probate court six months after the death, then recorded. Both limits rose on September 26, 2025 (HB 2116), from $75,000 and $100,000, and apply to affidavits filed on or after that date.

Arizona made the biggest small estate change of 2025. HB 2116 nearly tripled the personal property limit and tripled the real property limit, and it applies to affidavits signed and filed from September 26, 2025 on, whenever the person died. Families who were told a year ago that they needed probate may now qualify for the Arizona small estate affidavit instead.

Arizona is also one of the few states with a separate small estate affidavit for real estate. This guide covers both affidavits under A.R.S. 14-3971, how value is measured, the steps and fees, and the details that most often send an affidavit back.

Both limits are measured net of liens and encumbrances, so they test equity, not gross value. Arizona also lets an employer pay a surviving spouse up to $5,000 in unpaid wages on a short spouse’s affidavit at any time after the death (§14-3971(A)).

Affidavit 1: Personal property up to $200,000

Thirty days after the death, anyone holding the decedent’s personal property, such as a bank, brokerage or employer, must pay or deliver it to a successor who presents an affidavit stating, among other things:

  • 30 days have passed since the death;
  • no personal representative has been appointed or applied for in any jurisdiction (or one was discharged, or more than a year has passed since a closing statement);
  • the value of all personal property in the estate, wherever located, less liens and encumbrances, does not exceed $200,000 as of the date of death;
  • the claiming successor is entitled to the property; and
  • funeral expenses and expenses of the last illness have been paid.

With the same affidavit, a transfer agent must re-register securities in the successor’s name, and the Motor Vehicle Division must transfer vehicle titles on payment of its fees. Arizona courts publish a standard personal property affidavit form.

Affidavit 2: Real property up to $300,000

Arizona’s real property affidavit, often called the affidavit of succession to real property, lets heirs take title to a house or land without a probate case, subject to stricter conditions:

  1. Six months have passed since the death (a certified death certificate is attached).
  2. No personal representative has been appointed or applied for, and the value of all Arizona real property in the estate, less liens, does not exceed $300,000 at the date of death.
  3. Funeral expenses, last-illness expenses and all unsecured debts have been paid.
  4. The signers are entitled to the property as sole heirs, as devisees under a will (the original attached or already probated), or through the homestead allowance, exempt property or family allowance.
  5. No one else has a right to the decedent’s interest in the property, and no federal estate tax is due.

How value is measured. Arizona does not use market value here. The decedent’s interest is valued at the full cash value on the county assessment rolls for the year of death, less liens. For many homes, the assessor’s full cash value is below the sale price, which can bring a house within the limit. A mortgage is measured by the unpaid principal balance at death.

Filing. The affidavit is filed with the superior court (probate) in the county where the decedent lived, or where the property is for a non-resident, with the normal filing fee unless waived. The probate registrar issues a certified copy, which you then record with the county recorder where the property sits.

What counts and what doesn’t

Not part of the estate (and not counted against either limit): property with right of survivorship, including a home held as community property with right of survivorship or in joint tenancy; payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts; real estate covered by a recorded Arizona beneficiary deed; life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary; and trust assets.

Community property. Arizona is a community property state. When a married person dies, their half of community property that passes to the surviving spouse can often be confirmed through other procedures, and a home held as community property with right of survivorship passes to the spouse automatically. Check how each asset was titled before you total anything.

How to use the Arizona affidavits, step by step

  1. Get certified death certificates.
  2. Sort assets into personal property, Arizona real property, and non-probate assets.
  3. Value personal property net of liens; value real property at the assessor’s full cash value less the mortgage balance.
  4. Pay the funeral and last-illness bills (and, for real property, all unsecured debts) before signing.
  5. After 30 days, sign the personal property affidavit before a notary and present it to each holder and to MVD for vehicles.
  6. After six months, file the real property affidavit with the superior court, get the certified copy, and record it with the county recorder.
  7. Distribute to the heirs or devisees entitled to the property.

A worked example under the new limits

A Tucson widower died in March 2026 with a will leaving everything equally to his two children. He owned a house in his name with an assessor’s full cash value of $310,000 and a $40,000 mortgage balance, a brokerage account worth $120,000, a checking account with $18,000, and a car worth $15,000. His final medical bills and funeral are paid.

  • Personal property: $120,000 + $18,000 + $15,000 = $153,000. Under $200,000, so after 30 days the children collect it with the personal property affidavit and retitle the car at MVD.
  • Real property: $310,000 full cash value − $40,000 mortgage = $270,000. Under $300,000. After six months, with the original will attached or already probated, they file the real property affidavit, obtain the certified copy and record it.

Before HB 2116, both parts of this estate would have been far over the limits ($75,000 and $100,000), and the family would have needed probate.

After you sign: responsibility and records

The Arizona affidavits are sworn statements. Every signer acknowledges that a false statement can be prosecuted as perjury, and banks, transfer agents and the MVD are protected when they rely on an affidavit that looks proper on its face. That shifts the risk to the family.

If a creditor or another heir later shows a better right to property collected by affidavit, the people who received it can be required to answer for it. The best protection is a paper trail:

  • Keep copies of every affidavit you signed and every institution you presented it to.
  • Keep receipts for the funeral, the last-illness bills and, for real property, every unsecured debt you paid.
  • Keep the assessor’s printout showing the full cash value for the year of death and the lender’s payoff statement showing the unpaid principal at death.
  • Deposit collected funds into one account and distribute from there, so the flow of money is easy to follow.

Title companies that later insure a sale of the house will ask for much of this, so gathering it now saves time when the property is sold or refinanced.

Common mistakes in Arizona

  • Using market value for the house. The statute uses the assessor’s full cash value, less liens.
  • Filing the real property affidavit too early. Six full months must pass.
  • Leaving unsecured debts unpaid. The real property affidavit requires that all unsecured debts, not only funeral and medical bills, have been paid.
  • Forgetting to record. The court’s certified copy only transfers title once it is recorded with the county recorder.
  • Relying on old limits. Many older Arizona court forms and websites still show $75,000 and $100,000.

When the affidavits do not fit

If the estate exceeds the limits, or debts cannot be paid first, Arizona’s informal probate is the usual next step and is relatively streamlined. Our probate cost estimator helps you budget. How Arizona compares

With $200,000 for personal property plus a separate $300,000 real property affidavit, Arizona is now among the most generous states, alongside California ($208,850) and Wyoming ($400,000). Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Arizona small estate affidavit limit in 2026?

$200,000 for personal property and $300,000 for real property, both measured net of liens, for affidavits filed on or after September 26, 2025. Before that date the limits were $75,000 and $100,000.

Does the new Arizona limit depend on the date of death?

No. The higher limits apply to affidavits filed on or after September 26, 2025, regardless of when the person died.

Can I transfer a house in Arizona without probate?

Yes, if the decedent’s Arizona real property is worth $300,000 or less after liens, using the assessor’s full cash value. Six months after the death, file the affidavit of succession to real property with the superior court, then record the certified copy with the county recorder.

Do I have to file the Arizona personal property affidavit with the court?

No. The personal property affidavit goes directly to the bank, brokerage, employer or MVD. Only the real property affidavit is filed with the court.

How long do I have to wait in Arizona?

30 days for personal property and six months for real property. A surviving spouse can collect up to $5,000 in unpaid wages from the employer on a spouse’s affidavit without waiting.

Do debts have to be paid first?

For personal property, the affidavit confirms that funeral and last-illness expenses were paid. For real property, all unsecured debts must also be paid before the affidavit is filed.
