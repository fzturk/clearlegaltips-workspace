The short version (2026): New York’s small estate process is called voluntary administration. It covers estates with $50,000 or less in personal property, not counting the family set-off (up to $20,000 in household items, a $25,000 car and $25,000 in cash for a spouse or minor children). You file an affidavit with the Surrogate’s Court in the county where the person lived, pay a $1 fee, and receive a short certificate that banks accept. There is no bond, no waiting period and no court order, but real estate cannot pass this way, and you must file a final account.

People often search for a New York small estate affidavit expecting a form they can hand to a bank. New York works a little differently. Instead of a private affidavit, it has a short court filing called voluntary administration under Article 13 of the Surrogate’s Court Procedure Act (SCPA). One family member files a sworn affidavit with the Surrogate’s Court clerk, becomes the “voluntary administrator,” and uses the court’s certificate to collect and distribute the estate.

It is still dramatically simpler than probate. There is no judge, no bond and no hearing, and the filing fee is one dollar. This guide covers who qualifies, what counts toward the $50,000 limit, who can serve, and the duties that come with the certificate.

Who qualifies for New York voluntary administration

An estate qualifies as a “small estate” under SCPA 1301 when the person, whether they lived in New York or elsewhere, left personal property with a gross value of $50,000 or less, not counting the property set off to the family under EPTL 5-3.1. New York raised the threshold from $30,000 to $50,000 in November 2019.

Three limits follow from that definition:

  • Personal property only. Real estate cannot be administered through voluntary administration, whatever it is worth. A house in the decedent’s sole name needs a regular probate or administration proceeding.
  • Gross value. Debts are not subtracted when testing the $50,000 limit.
  • No appointed fiduciary. If an executor or administrator has already been appointed, voluntary administration is not available.

There is no minimum waiting period after the death. The procedure may be used any time after the death, once you have a certified death certificate.

What counts toward the $50,000

New York measures only property that would pass through the estate. Leave out:

  • Jointly held property and joint accounts with right of survivorship;
  • Payable-on-death and in-trust-for accounts, transfer-on-death securities and U.S. savings bonds with a named co-owner or beneficiary;
  • Life insurance and retirement accounts with a living named beneficiary; and
  • The family set-off described next.

The EPTL 5-3.1 family set-off

When a person dies leaving a surviving spouse or children under 21, certain property is not an estate asset at all. It vests directly in the family:

  • Household furniture, appliances, clothing, jewelry, electronics and similar items, up to $20,000 in total;
  • One motor vehicle worth up to $25,000, or cash in that amount instead;
  • Money (cash, bank and money market accounts, CDs and marketable securities) up to $25,000, reduced by any shortfall in the other categories; and
  • Certain other family items the statute lists, such as family books and pictures.

For a family with a surviving spouse, this can take $70,000 or more out of the calculation before the $50,000 test is even applied. Without a spouse or minor children, the set-off does not apply and everything in the estate counts.

Who can be the voluntary administrator

SCPA 1303 sets a strict order of who may serve.

If there is no will: the surviving adult spouse has first priority. If there is none, or the spouse renounces, a competent adult child or grandchild, then a parent, then a brother or sister, then a niece or nephew, then an aunt or uncle, in that order. If no one in those categories will act, a guardian of a minor distributee may act, and failing that, the county’s chief fiscal officer.

If there is a will: the named executor (or alternate) has the right to act. The will is filed with the court. If the executor renounces or does not file the affidavit within 30 days after the will is filed, a person who would be entitled to letters of administration with the will annexed may step in.

No one else can become a voluntary administrator. A friend or unmarried partner who is not a distributee cannot serve, even if the family agrees.

How to file, step by step

  1. Get a certified death certificate.
  2. Total the personal property using the rules above. Confirm it is $50,000 or less after the set-off.
  3. Complete the official voluntary administration affidavit from the Unified Court System’s Surrogate’s Court forms. It lists the assets, the distributees, and whether there is a will.
  4. File it with the Surrogate’s Court clerk in the county where the decedent lived (or, for a non-resident, the county where the property is located), together with the death certificate and the original will if there is one. The filing fee is $1.
  5. Receive the short certificate. The clerk files and indexes the affidavit and issues a certificate showing you have qualified. The clerk may limit it to specific transactions. The court mails notice to the distributees and beneficiaries.
  6. Open an estate account and collect the assets using the certificate. All checks are signed in the estate’s name by you as voluntary administrator.
  7. Pay expenses and debts in order, then distribute.
  8. File your account with the court showing everything collected, paid and distributed, with receipts. There is no fee for this final filing.

No bond is required, and no court order is issued. Your authority ends if a regular executor or administrator is later appointed.

Your duties once you have the certificate

A voluntary administrator is a fiduciary. SCPA 1306 and 1307 give you real powers and real duties.

Powers. You can collect the decedent’s personal property, sell it for reasonable cash value, and sue to recover property someone refuses to hand over. A certified copy of your affidavit is prima facie proof of its facts in that proceeding. You cannot pursue a wrongful death or personal injury claim; those need a full administration.

The cap follows you. Your powers only reach property as long as the total you are administering stays within the $50,000 small estate limit. If you discover more, a regular proceeding is needed.

Payment order. Pay the necessary administration expenses first, then reasonable funeral expenses, then the decedent’s debts in the order New York law provides. Only then distribute, following the will or the intestacy rules.

No fee for yourself. Voluntary administrators serve without compensation.

Accounting. Keep every receipt and cancelled check. You must file an account with the court listing all assets collected and all payments and distributions.

A worked example

A Queens resident died in 2026 leaving her husband and an adult son. She owned, in her sole name, a checking account with $41,000, a savings account with $22,000, a 2019 car worth $14,000, household furniture and jewelry worth about $9,000, and a life insurance policy naming her son.

  • The life insurance goes to the son and is not counted.
  • The furniture and jewelry ($9,000) and the car ($14,000) are set off to her husband under EPTL 5-3.1.
  • Up to $25,000 in cash is also set off to him, reduced by any excess value in the other categories (there is none here).
  • That leaves $63,000 in accounts minus $25,000 set off = $38,000 counted, under $50,000.

Her husband, who has first priority, files the affidavit in Queens County Surrogate’s Court, pays $1, and uses the certificate to collect the accounts, pay the funeral bill and her final credit card, and distribute the rest under the intestacy rules.

When voluntary administration will not work

  • There is real estate in the decedent’s sole name. File for probate (with a will) or letters of administration (without one). Real estate held jointly, in a trust or by transfer on death deed passes outside the estate.
  • Personal property exceeds $50,000 after the set-off. A full proceeding follows. Our probate cost estimator helps you budget.
  • A lawsuit is involved, such as a wrongful death claim, which a voluntary administrator cannot bring.
  • No eligible person will serve. Only the relatives listed in SCPA 1303 (or the named executor) can act.

How New York compares

New York’s $50,000 limit is below the national middle value of $75,000, and it applies to personal property only. What sets New York apart is the family set-off, which removes up to $70,000 in cars, household items and cash before the limit is tested, and the formal but inexpensive court filing, which gives banks a court certificate instead of a private affidavit. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small estate limit in New York?

$50,000 in personal property, measured by gross value and not counting the family set-off under EPTL 5-3.1. The limit has been $50,000 since November 2019. Real estate cannot be handled through the small estate process at any value.

How much does voluntary administration cost in New York?

The Surrogate’s Court filing fee is $1, and there is no fee for filing the final account. Your other costs are certified death certificates and any certified copies of the court certificate the banks request. No bond is required.

Is there a waiting period for voluntary administration?

No statutory waiting period applies; the procedure can be used any time after the death. If there is a will, the named executor has 30 days after the will is filed to act before others may step in.

Can I use voluntary administration if the person owned a house?

Not for the house. Real estate in the decedent’s sole name must go through a regular probate or administration proceeding. If the house was held jointly with right of survivorship, in a trust, or by transfer on death deed, it passes outside the estate and does not block voluntary administration of the personal property.

Who can be a voluntary administrator in New York?

If there is no will, the surviving spouse first, then an adult child or grandchild, a parent, a sibling, a niece or nephew, or an aunt or uncle, in that order. If there is a will, the named executor. No one outside those categories can serve.

Does a voluntary administrator get paid?

No. SCPA 1307 says the voluntary administrator serves without compensation, though necessary administration expenses are paid from the estate before debts and distributions.
