The short version (2026): Ohio does not use a small estate affidavit. Its shortcut is a release from administration: an application to the probate court for estates worth $35,000 or less, or $100,000 or less when everything goes to the surviving spouse. Unlike most state shortcuts, the court’s order can transfer real estate as well as personal property. Before you total the estate, remember two spouse rules: up to $65,000 in cars can pass to the spouse outside the estate, and the spouse (and minor children) receive a $40,000 support allowance.

Families looking for an Ohio small estate affidavit find a different tool: the release from administration under Ohio Revised Code section 2113.03. You file an application with the probate court in the county where the person lived. If the estate fits the limits and the notice requirements are met or waived, the court signs an order that delivers the personal property and transfers the real estate to the people entitled to them. No executor or administrator is appointed and no formal accounting is filed.

Ohio also has two spouse-friendly rules that often shrink an estate below the limit, and a fast “summary release” for estates that do not exceed the funeral bill. This guide covers all of them.

The two Ohio limits: $35,000 and $100,000

Section 2113.03(A) allows a release from administration when either:

  1. The value of the estate’s assets is $35,000 or less; or
  2. The value is $100,000 or less and the surviving spouse takes everything, either because a valid will leaves all the assets to the spouse, or because there is no will and the spouse is entitled to the whole estate under Ohio’s intestacy rules (plus the support allowance).

The $100,000 route is narrower than it looks under intestacy. An Ohio spouse inherits everything only in certain family situations, typically when all of the decedent’s children are also the surviving spouse’s children, or when there are no children. When the decedent had a child from another relationship, the spouse does not take the whole estate without a will, and the $35,000 limit applies.

The limits have not changed since the current version of the section took effect in January 2012.

Two spouse rules that come off the top

Cars up to $65,000 (§2106.18)

A surviving spouse can select one or more of the decedent’s automobiles, with a total value up to $65,000, and have them retitled through the BMV with an affidavit. Those cars are not estate assets and are not included in the estate inventory, so they do not count toward the $35,000 or $100,000 limit. The rule does not apply to cars already set to pass by joint ownership, a transfer-on-death designation, or a specific gift in the will.

The $40,000 support allowance (§2106.13)

When a person dies leaving a surviving spouse, minor children, or both, they are entitled to $40,000 as an allowance for support, paid from the estate before general creditors. If the spouse selected more than one car under the $65,000 rule, the allowance is reduced by the value of the lowest-valued car. The allowance is why a modest Ohio estate often ends up entirely with the spouse, which in turn is what opens the $100,000 limit.

What counts toward the limit

The limit measures the assets of the estate: property in the decedent’s sole name that does not pass by beneficiary designation, survivorship or trust. Unlike many states, Ohio includes real estate the decedent owned alone.

Left out: joint accounts and real estate held with right of survivorship; payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts; real estate with a transfer on death designation affidavit on record; life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary; trust assets; and cars the spouse selects under section 2106.18.

If the estate is not all cash, stocks or other easily valued property, section 2113.03(C) requires an appraiser selected by the applicant and approved by the court. The appraiser’s valuation goes into the application.

How to apply for a release from administration

  1. Gather the facts: death certificate, the original will if any, a list of assets with values, and a list of debts including funeral bills.
  2. Apply the spouse rules first. Transfer selected cars through the BMV under section 2106.18 and account for the $40,000 support allowance.
  3. Get an appraisal for anything other than cash or securities, such as a house or vehicles not selected by the spouse.
  4. Present the will for probate, if there is one. The court releases a testate estate and orders distribution under the will once it is admitted (§2113.03(F)).
  5. File the application with the probate court in the county where the decedent lived, using the Ohio Supreme Court’s standard probate forms. Pay the court’s filing fee, which varies by county.
  6. Give notice. The court directs notice to the surviving spouse and heirs and, unless waived or found unnecessary, publication in a local newspaper. Signed waivers from heirs can shorten this step.
  7. Receive the order. The court orders delivery of personal property and transfer of real property to those entitled. It may appoint a commissioner to sign deeds or other transfer documents and to report back once the property is delivered.

An order relieving an estate from administration protects a good-faith buyer of the real estate from unsecured creditors of the decedent, the same way a full administration would (§2113.03(G)). Creditors’ rights are otherwise limited by the normal Ohio claim deadlines, generally six months from the date of death.

Summary release: when the estate does not exceed the funeral bill

Section 2113.031 offers an even faster summary release from administration for the smallest estates:

  • For the person who paid the funeral: if the estate is worth no more than the lesser of $5,000 or the funeral and burial expenses, the person who paid (or is obligated in writing to pay) those expenses, other than a surviving spouse, can apply for an order releasing the assets to them.
  • For a surviving spouse: if the estate does not exceed the support allowance plus up to $5,000 for funeral and burial expenses, and the spouse has prepaid, paid or is obligated to pay the funeral, the spouse can apply for summary release.

Because the amounts are small and the applicant has already paid for the funeral, summary release is usually the quickest court option Ohio offers. Ask the probate clerk which notice, if any, your county requires.

A worked example: how the spouse rules change the answer

A Columbus man died in 2026 without a will. He and his wife had two adult children together, and he had no other children. In his sole name he owned a $22,000 SUV, a $9,000 pickup, a checking account with $48,000, and a condo worth $45,000. His retirement account named his wife as beneficiary.

  • Retirement account: passes to his wife by beneficiary designation. Not counted.
  • Cars: his wife selects both under section 2106.18. Their total, $31,000, is under $65,000, so both pass to her through the BMV and leave the estate. Because she chose two cars, her support allowance is reduced by the lower-valued one ($9,000).
  • What remains: checking ($48,000) + condo ($45,000) = $93,000.
  • Who inherits: with no will, and with all of his children also being her children, Ohio intestacy gives the whole estate to his wife. That makes the $100,000 limit available.

Result: his wife can apply for a release from administration. The court orders the bank to release the checking account and transfers the condo to her, typically after an appraisal of the condo and waivers of notice signed by the two children.

Change one fact, and the answer flips. If he had a child from an earlier relationship, his wife would not take everything under intestacy, the $35,000 limit would apply, and $93,000 would require a full administration. A will leaving everything to her would have kept the $100,000 route open.

When the estate is too large

If the estate exceeds the limit after the spouse rules, Ohio probate follows with a court-appointed executor or administrator. How Ohio compares

Ohio’s $35,000 general limit is below the national middle value of $75,000, and its $100,000 spouse limit is about average. What makes Ohio different is that the release is a court order that can move real estate, something most state affidavits cannot do, and that the $65,000 car rule and $40,000 support allowance remove so much from the estate for a surviving spouse. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Ohio have a small estate affidavit?

No. Ohio uses a release from administration, which is an application to the probate court. For estates worth $35,000 or less, or $100,000 or less when everything goes to the surviving spouse, the court can release the estate and order the property delivered without appointing an executor or administrator.

Can an Ohio release from administration transfer a house?

Yes. The court’s order directs delivery of personal property and transfer of real property to the people entitled to them, and the court can appoint a commissioner to sign the deed. Real estate in the decedent’s sole name counts toward the $35,000 or $100,000 limit and usually needs an appraisal.

Does the car count toward the Ohio small estate limit?

Not if the surviving spouse selects it under section 2106.18. A spouse can take one or more automobiles with a total value up to $65,000, and those cars are not estate assets. Cars not selected by a spouse do count.

What is the Ohio support allowance for a surviving spouse?

$40,000, payable to the surviving spouse and any minor children, ahead of general creditors. It is reduced by the value of the lowest-valued car if the spouse selected more than one car under the $65,000 rule.

How long does a release from administration take in Ohio?

It depends on the county and on whether heirs waive notice. When publication is required, the court sets the notice period. Many families with signed waivers and simple assets receive an order within a few weeks of filing.

Is there a waiting period after death in Ohio?

The statute does not set a minimum waiting period for applying. In practice, families wait until they know the debts and have valued the assets, because the court will want both before releasing the estate.
