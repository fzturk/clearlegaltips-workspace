The short version (2026): For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, the California small estate affidavit covers estates up to $208,850. You wait 40 days, sign a declaration under penalty of perjury, and hand it to the bank; no court filing. Cars, boats, mobile homes, joint tenancy property and trust assets do not count toward the limit. Real estate needs its own route: an affidavit for property worth up to $69,625, or, new since 2025, a court petition for a primary residence worth up to $750,000. These figures hold until March 31, 2028.

California’s small estate affidavit, sometimes called a Probate Code 13100 affidavit, is one of the most generous shortcuts in the country. A family can collect bank accounts, brokerage accounts and personal belongings worth up to $208,850 without opening a probate case, paying court fees or waiting for a judge.

The rules around it are also more layered than most states, because California uses different procedures, and different dollar limits, for personal property and real estate. This guide covers who qualifies, what counts toward the California small estate affidavit limit, how the declaration works, and the separate paths for a house, including the $750,000 primary-residence petition that took effect in 2025.

The California limits for 2026: 4 numbers to know

The Judicial Council adjusts California’s small estate amounts every three years under Probate Code section 890. The current figures apply to deaths from April 1, 2025 through March 31, 2028:

If you see a higher “2026” figure for the California affidavit elsewhere, it is not what the courts use. The Judicial Council’s table and Form DE-300 both show $208,850, and the next scheduled change is April 1, 2028.

Who can use the California small estate affidavit

Under Probate Code section 13100, a successor can collect the decedent’s personal property by affidavit when:

  1. The gross value of the decedent’s real and personal property in California, after the exclusions below, is $208,850 or less;
  2. At least 40 days have passed since the death; and
  3. No probate case is pending or planned, or the personal representative has consented in writing.

A “successor” is whoever inherits the property: the beneficiary under the will if there is one, or the heir under California’s intestacy rules if there is not. Unlike Texas, California lets you use the affidavit whether or not there is a will. If several people inherit, each can sign for their share, or one can sign on behalf of all of them with their authorization.

Note the word gross. California does not subtract mortgages or loans. A $150,000 condo with a $140,000 mortgage counts as $150,000 toward the limit.

What does not count toward $208,850

Section 13050 removes several kinds of property from the calculation. That list is often the difference between qualifying and not.

  • Joint tenancy property and any interest that ended at death (the survivor already owns it).
  • Property in a revocable living trust. The trustee handles it under the trust.
  • Vehicles registered or titled under the Vehicle Code. The DMV has its own transfer process.
  • Boats and vessels numbered under the Vehicle Code.
  • Manufactured homes, mobilehomes, truck campers and floating homes registered with the state.
  • Up to $20,875 in unpaid salary or compensation owed to the decedent, and amounts due for military service.
  • Payable-on-death and beneficiary assets: life insurance, retirement accounts, and POD or TOD accounts with a living beneficiary pass outside the estate.

What does count: bank and investment accounts in the decedent’s sole name with no beneficiary, personal belongings of value, money owed to the decedent, and the value of California real estate the decedent owned alone. Real estate counts toward the $208,850 even though the affidavit cannot transfer it. A home in the decedent’s sole name will usually push an estate over the limit on its own, which is exactly why the $750,000 primary-residence route matters.

Property passing to a surviving spouse follows its own rules. A spouse can often confirm community and separate property through a spousal property petition, and up to $20,875 in unpaid wages can be paid directly to the spouse under sections 13600 and 13601.

How to use the affidavit, step by step

  1. Wait 40 days after the date of death. The declaration must state that 40 days have passed.
  2. Total the estate using the inclusions and exclusions above. Keep your worksheet.
  3. Complete the declaration. California has no mandatory statewide form for a section 13100 affidavit. Many banks provide their own; others accept a declaration that contains every statement section 13101 lists. Courts and law libraries publish samples.
  4. Include the required statements: the decedent’s name, date and place of death; that 40 days have passed; that no probate is pending or planned; that the gross value does not exceed the limit; a description of the property you are collecting; the name of each successor; and that you are the successor or authorized to act for them. You declare it true under penalty of perjury under California law.
  5. Attach a certified death certificate and, if the institution asks, identification. Securities transfer agents often want a medallion signature guarantee or notarization.
  6. Present it to each holder: bank, credit union, brokerage, employer, storage facility. The holder must pay or deliver once it receives a proper affidavit, and it is protected if it does.

Nothing is filed with the superior court. That is the main advantage over probate, which in California carries statutory attorney and executor fees calculated on the gross estate.

Transferring a California house without full probate

The section 13100 affidavit cannot move real estate. California gives you three other routes, depending on value and timing.

1. Affidavit re real property of small value (up to $69,625)

If all the decedent’s California real estate is worth $69,625 or less, section 13200 lets the successor use Form DE-305. It can only be used six months after the death. The affidavit, with a certified inventory and appraisal by a probate referee, is filed with the superior court, and a certified copy is then recorded with the county recorder.

2. Petition for a primary residence (up to $750,000)

Since April 1, 2025, Assembly Bill 2016 lets a successor ask the superior court for an order that they have succeeded to the decedent’s primary residence in California, if its gross value is $750,000 or less and 40 days have passed. This uses a petition to determine succession (Form DE-310) and a short court hearing, not a full probate administration. Rentals, vacation homes and land that was not the decedent’s main home do not qualify. Title insurers set their own conditions for insuring a later sale, so check with a title company before relying on the order to sell.

3. Joint tenancy, trust or TOD deed

If the home was held in joint tenancy, a living trust, or a revocable transfer on death deed, it passes outside the estate, and none of the dollar limits apply. For a surviving joint tenant, recording an affidavit of death of joint tenant is typically enough.

When the estate is over the limit

If the countable estate exceeds $208,850 and none of the real property routes fit, California probate follows. Before accepting that, check three things:

  • Recount exclusions. Vehicles, mobilehomes, trust assets and joint tenancy property are often counted by mistake.
  • Consider the spouse. If everything passes to a surviving spouse or domestic partner, a spousal property petition can confirm ownership with less cost than probate.
  • Look at the small estate set-aside. When there is a surviving spouse or minor children, sections 6602 and 6609 let a court set aside an estate worth up to $107,900 for them.

Liability: the promise you make in the declaration

The affidavit shifts responsibility to you. A person who collects property with a section 13100 affidavit is personally liable, up to the value received, for the decedent’s unpaid debts and to anyone with a better right to the property. If you leave out a successor, or the estate turns out to be over the limit, you can be required to return the property or its value. Keep your worksheet and copies of every declaration you hand out.

How California compares

At $208,850, California’s personal property limit is nearly three times the national middle value of $75,000. Only Wyoming ($400,000), Oregon (combined $275,000) and Arizona ($200,000 personal plus a separate $300,000 real property track) are in the same range. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the California small estate affidavit limit in 2026?

$208,850 for deaths on or after April 1, 2025. That figure stays in place through March 31, 2028, when the Judicial Council next adjusts it. For deaths between April 1, 2022 and March 31, 2025, the limit is $184,500.

Does a California small estate affidavit need to be notarized?

The statute allows either an affidavit or a declaration under penalty of perjury, so notarization is not legally required. In practice, many banks and nearly all securities transfer agents ask for a notarized signature or a medallion guarantee, so plan to sign before a notary.

Does the house count toward the $208,850?

Yes, if the decedent owned it alone. The value of California real estate counts toward the limit, even though the affidavit cannot transfer it. Property held in joint tenancy, a living trust or by transfer on death deed is excluded. For a primary residence worth up to $750,000, a court petition to determine succession is available for deaths on or after April 1, 2025.

Do I have to file the small estate affidavit with the court?

No. The section 13100 declaration goes directly to the bank or other holder. Only the real property procedures involve the court: the DE-305 affidavit for property worth up to $69,625 and the petition to determine succession for a primary residence.

Can I use the affidavit if there is a will?

Yes. The successor under the will can use it, as long as no probate is pending and the estate is within the limit. If an executor has already been appointed, the executor must consent in writing.

How long does the bank have to pay?

The statute does not set a deadline, but a holder that receives a proper affidavit with the required documents must pay or deliver the property. If a holder refuses, the successor can sue to recover the property.
