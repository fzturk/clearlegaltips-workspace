The short version (2026): New Jersey has two small estate affidavits, both for people who died without a will. A surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner can use one for an estate worth up to $50,000, with the first $10,000 free from debts. When there is no spouse, one heir can use the other for up to $20,000, with the written consent of all other heirs. Both count real and personal property, are signed before the county surrogate, and give the affiant the powers of an administrator. Watch for New Jersey inheritance tax waivers.

The New Jersey small estate affidavit is a surrogate’s office procedure, not a form you hand straight to a bank. Under Title 3B of the New Jersey Statutes, a spouse or a consenting heir signs an affidavit before the county surrogate, and from that moment has the same rights, powers and duties as a court-appointed administrator for the small estate. There is no bond and no court hearing.

New Jersey’s version is unusual in two ways. It counts real estate within the dollar limit, and it applies only when the person died without a will. This guide covers both affidavits, what counts, what the surrogate needs, and the inheritance tax waivers that sometimes matter more than the affidavit itself.

Affidavit 1: Surviving spouse or partner, up to $50,000 (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-3)

When a person dies intestate and the total value of all their real and personal assets does not exceed $50,000, the surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner is entitled to all of those assets without administration. The spouse or partner signs an affidavit before the surrogate stating:

  • that they are the surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner;
  • that the value of the intestate’s real and personal assets does not exceed $50,000;
  • the decedent’s residence at death; and
  • the nature, location and value of the real and personal assets.

The statute adds a valuable protection: the first $10,000 of the estate is free from all debts of the decedent. Anything above that remains available to pay the decedent’s creditors.

Affidavit 2: One heir with consent, up to $20,000 (N.J.S.A. 3B:10-4)

When there is no surviving spouse or partner and the total value of all real and personal assets does not exceed $20,000, one heir can collect the estate without administration if every other heir consents in writing. The consenting heirs’ written consent and the affiant’s affidavit are filed and recorded with the surrogate. The affidavit states:

  • the decedent’s residence at death;
  • the names, residences and relationships of all the heirs;
  • the nature, location and value of the real and personal assets; and
  • that the total does not exceed $20,000.

There is no debt-free portion under this affidavit.

What both affidavits do

Once filed, the affiant has all the rights, powers and duties of an administrator for the estate. The affiant can collect the assets, sign transfer documents and pay debts, and can also be sued and required to account as if they had been appointed administrator. In plain terms: you get the authority, and you also take on the responsibility to handle the estate properly.

What counts toward the limit

New Jersey measures all of the intestate’s real and personal assets, which is broader than most states.

Counted: real estate in the decedent’s sole name, bank and investment accounts in their sole name with no beneficiary, vehicles, and other personal property.

Not counted: assets that pass outside the estate, such as property held as tenants by the entirety or joint tenants with survivorship, payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, and trust assets.

Because real estate counts, most New Jersey homeowners exceed the limits unless the house was held jointly. In practice these affidavits are most often used for estates made up of a bank account or two, a car and personal belongings.

New Jersey inheritance tax waivers: the step families miss

New Jersey has an inheritance tax, and it affects even small estates because of how banks and brokerages handle a decedent’s accounts.

Who pays. Transfers to “Class A” beneficiaries (spouse or civil union or domestic partner, children, grandchildren and other lineal descendants, and parents and grandparents) are exempt. Siblings and sons- or daughters-in-law (“Class C”) are exempt on the first $25,000 and taxed above that. Most other beneficiaries (“Class D”) are taxed on the whole amount.

Why it matters for small estates. New Jersey banks generally may release only part of a decedent’s accounts until they receive a tax waiver from the Division of Taxation. For Class A beneficiaries, the estate representative can often complete a self-executing waiver for bank accounts and securities; for real estate and for other beneficiaries, a waiver from the Division is usually needed. Ask the bank what it requires before you plan distributions, and check the Division of Taxation’s current guidance and forms.

New Jersey repealed its separate estate tax for deaths after 2017, so for most families the inheritance tax waiver is the only state tax step.

How to use a New Jersey small estate affidavit, step by step

  1. Confirm there is no will. Both affidavits are for intestate estates only. If there is a will, it goes to the surrogate for probate.
  2. Identify the heirs under New Jersey intestacy law.
  3. List and value all assets, including any real estate in the decedent’s sole name.
  4. Choose the affidavit: spouse or partner up to $50,000, or one heir with all others’ consent up to $20,000.
  5. Gather consents if using the heir affidavit. Every other heir signs.
  6. Contact the surrogate in the county where the decedent lived. Many surrogates require an appointment and prepare the affidavit themselves. Bring a certified death certificate, identification, and details of each asset.
  7. Sign before the surrogate and pay the surrogate’s fee. Order short certificates or certified copies for each institution.
  8. Handle tax waivers with each bank or brokerage.
  9. Collect, pay debts and distribute. Keep records, because you can be required to account like an administrator.

A worked example

A Trenton man died in 2026 without a will. He was not married, and he left three adult daughters. His estate: a checking account ($9,500), a savings account ($6,000) and a car worth $3,500. His condo had been held with his eldest daughter as joint tenants with right of survivorship.

  • Condo: passes to the eldest daughter as surviving joint tenant. Not counted.
  • Estate for the affidavit: $9,500 + $6,000 + $3,500 = $19,000, under $20,000.
  • No spouse: the heir affidavit (3B:10-4) applies.

The two younger daughters sign written consents. The eldest meets with the Mercer County Surrogate, signs the affidavit listing all three daughters and every asset, and receives certified copies. Because the daughters are Class A beneficiaries, no inheritance tax is due, and she works with the bank on the waiver paperwork. She then pays the final bills and divides the rest equally.

Common mistakes in New Jersey

  • Trying to use the affidavit with a will. Both procedures require that the decedent died intestate.
  • Forgetting the real estate. Real property in the decedent’s sole name counts toward the $50,000 or $20,000 limit.
  • Missing a consent. The heir affidavit needs written consent from every other heir.
  • Distributing before debts. Only the spouse’s first $10,000 is protected from debts; the affiant can be sued and required to account like an administrator.
  • Ignoring the tax waiver. Banks may hold part of the account until the waiver issue is resolved.

When the affidavits do not fit

If there is a will, or the assets exceed the limits, the surrogate’s office handles probate or letters of administration, often in a single visit for uncontested estates. The surrogate can tell you on the phone whether your situation fits one of the two affidavits or needs letters of administration, which is worth a call before you gather consents or order extra death certificates. How New Jersey compares

New Jersey’s limits, $50,000 for a spouse and $20,000 for other heirs, are below the national middle value of $75,000, and counting real estate makes them tighter still. Its surrogate system, however, is fast and inexpensive, similar in spirit to New York’s voluntary administration. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small estate limit in New Jersey?

$50,000 when the surviving spouse, civil union partner or domestic partner uses the affidavit, and $20,000 when there is no spouse and one heir uses it with the other heirs’ written consent. Both limits count real and personal property.

Can I use a New Jersey small estate affidavit if there is a will?

No. Both affidavits apply only when the person died without a will. A will must be probated with the county surrogate.

Where do I file a small estate affidavit in New Jersey?

With the surrogate of the county where the decedent lived. If the decedent lived out of state, it can be filed in a county where assets are located. Many surrogates prepare the affidavit at an appointment.

Does the house count toward the New Jersey limit?

Yes, if it was in the decedent’s sole name. Real estate counts toward the $50,000 or $20,000 limit. A house held as tenants by the entirety or joint tenants with survivorship passes to the survivor and is not counted.

Am I responsible for the decedent’s debts?

The affiant has the duties of an administrator and can be required to account and be sued as one. Under the spouse affidavit, the first $10,000 is free from all debts.

Do I need an inheritance tax waiver for a small estate in New Jersey?

Often, yes, to release the full balance of bank and investment accounts. Transfers to Class A beneficiaries such as spouses and children are exempt from tax, but banks and brokerages still follow the waiver rules. Ask each institution and check the Division of Taxation’s current forms.
