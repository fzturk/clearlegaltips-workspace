The short version (2026): Florida has no general small estate affidavit. Its main shortcut is summary administration, a court petition that since July 1, 2026 covers estates up to $150,000 (up from $75,000), not counting exempt property and protected homestead. Three narrower routes skip most court work: disposition without administration (now up to $20,000 plus final expenses for intestate estates after one year), a bank affidavit for accounts totaling $2,000 after six months, and direct payment of federal tax refunds up to $5,000.

People searching for a Florida small estate affidavit are usually surprised to learn that Florida does not have one in the form most states do. There is no sworn statement you can hand a bank to collect $50,000 without going near the courthouse. Instead, Florida channels small estates through summary administration, a streamlined court proceeding, plus a few narrow paperwork-only options.

That picture changed on July 1, 2026. Chapter 2026-57 (CS/HB 1337) doubled the summary administration limit to $150,000 and raised every smaller threshold. This guide explains each route, which one fits your situation, and what the 2026 changes mean for families settling an estate now.

Summary administration: the main Florida shortcut

Summary administration is available under section 735.201 when two things are true:

  1. If there is a will, it does not direct formal administration; and
  2. The value of the entire estate subject to administration in Florida, minus property exempt from creditors, is $150,000 or less, or the person has been dead for more than two years.

The two-year rule is easy to miss. When a family discovers an old bank account or a house still titled to a parent who died years ago, summary administration is available regardless of the estate’s size.

What is excluded from the $150,000

  • Protected homestead. A Florida homestead that passes to the spouse or heirs is not an asset “subject to administration” and is protected from most creditors. Summary administration is often used simply to get a court order confirming who inherits it.
  • Exempt property under section 732.402, which includes household furniture and appliances up to $20,000 and up to two regularly used motor vehicles, when claimed by the spouse or children.
  • Non-probate assets: life insurance and retirement accounts with a living beneficiary, payable-on-death and joint accounts, trust property, and real estate held with survivorship rights or passing by a Lady Bird (enhanced life estate) deed.

Who files and signs

Any beneficiary, or the person nominated as personal representative in the will, can file the petition. It must be signed and verified by the surviving spouse, if any, and the beneficiaries, except that a beneficiary receiving a full share under the proposed distribution does not have to join. Anyone who does not join must be formally served. If there is a will, it is admitted to probate as part of the same case.

Florida probate rules require a personal representative to be represented by a lawyer unless they are the only interested person. Summary administration appoints no personal representative, and some counties publish self-help packets through their law libraries, but many clerks expect an attorney unless the petitioner is the only beneficiary. Ask your county clerk how it handles self-represented petitioners before filing.

The creditor step

Before the court signs the order, the petitioner must make a diligent search for known or reasonably ascertainable creditors, serve them with the petition, and provide for payment from available assets (section 735.206). Afterward, the recipients may publish a notice to creditors; if proof of publication is filed, unknown creditors are barred unless they file claims within three months (section 735.2063).

Skipping this has teeth. A known creditor who was not notified can enforce the claim against the people who received the property and, if the creditor wins, collect attorney’s fees from those who signed the petition.

What the order does

The order of summary administration assigns specific assets to specific people. Banks, brokerages and transfer agents can then pay or transfer to those named, and they are protected when they do. A buyer who later purchases property from a person named in the order takes it free of creditor claims. The recipients remain personally liable for a pro rata share of lawful claims, up to the value each actually received (not counting exempt property), until two years after the death, when most claims are barred.

Disposition without administration: the paperwork-only options

Florida has two versions of disposition without administration, and 2026 made one of them far more useful.

For intestate estates after one year (section 735.304)

When the person died without a will more than one year ago and no administration is pending, an heir can request distribution by affidavit if the estate consists only of exempt property plus non-exempt personal property worth no more than $20,000 plus the funeral expenses and the reasonable medical and hospital costs of the last 60 days of illness. The affidavit must be signed and verified by the surviving spouse and the heirs (an heir receiving a full share need not join), and before filing, the affiant must search for creditors and provide for them. The 2026 law raised the base amount from $10,000 to $20,000.

For final expenses (section 735.301)

When the estate is only exempt property plus non-exempt personal property worth no more than the funeral expenses and last-60-day medical costs, any interested person can apply informally, by affidavit or letter. If the court agrees, it issues a letter authorizing the transfer, and anyone who pays or delivers under it is discharged. This route is typically used to reimburse whoever paid for the funeral.

The $2,000 bank affidavit and the $5,000 tax refund

Two provisions skip the court entirely.

Small bank accounts (section 735.303). A Florida bank may pay a family member the combined balance of all “qualified accounts” the person held there, up to $2,000, without any court order. A qualified account is a deposit account or CD in the decedent’s sole name with no payable-on-death or survivor designation. The bank cannot pay until six months after the death. The family member (spouse first, then an adult child, then an adult descendant, then a parent) signs a sworn affidavit and provides a certified death certificate. The limit doubled from $1,000 on July 1, 2026.

Federal income tax refunds (section 735.302). A refund of up to $5,000 can go directly to the surviving spouse, or if none, to a child designated by all the children over 14, on a verified application stating that debts are paid or provided for and no administration is planned.

Which route fits: 4 common Florida situations

  • A parent died with $90,000 in a bank account in their name alone and a homestead. Summary administration. The homestead does not count, and $90,000 is under $150,000.
  • A widow left a car, furniture and $1,500 in checking. The car and furniture are likely exempt property for the family, and the $1,500 fits the bank affidavit after six months.
  • An uncle died without a will 18 months ago leaving $14,000 in a credit union. Disposition without administration under section 735.304 now covers it.
  • A house is still titled to a grandparent who died in 2019. Summary administration under the two-year rule, whatever the house is worth.

At $150,000, Florida’s summary administration limit is now double the national middle value of $75,000 for small estate procedures. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Florida have a small estate affidavit?

Not a general one. Florida’s closest equivalents are the sworn affidavit a family member can give a bank for up to $2,000 in accounts after six months, and the affidavit used for disposition without administration of small intestate estates. Most small estates go through summary administration, which requires a court petition and order.

What is the Florida summary administration limit in 2026?

$150,000, effective July 1, 2026, raised from $75,000 by chapter 2026-57. The figure measures the estate subject to administration in Florida minus exempt property, so protected homestead does not count. Summary administration is also available at any value if the person has been dead for more than two years.

How long does summary administration take in Florida?

Usually weeks to a few months, depending on the county and on how quickly the creditor search and any required service are completed. There is no mandatory creditor waiting period before the order, which is why it is much faster than formal administration.

Do I need a lawyer for summary administration in Florida?

It depends on the county and on who is involved. Probate rules require a personal representative to have a lawyer unless they are the only interested person, and summary administration has no personal representative, but many clerks still expect representation unless the petitioner is the sole beneficiary. Check with the clerk of the circuit court before filing on your own.

Does the Florida homestead count toward the $150,000?

No, when it is protected homestead. Protected homestead passes to the spouse or heirs by law and is not part of the estate subject to administration. A summary administration order or a separate petition to determine homestead is often used to confirm who owns it so the property can later be sold or refinanced.

Can I get money out of a Florida bank account without probate?

Yes, if the accounts in the person’s sole name at that bank total $2,000 or less and six months have passed. A family member signs a sworn affidavit and provides a certified death certificate. Larger balances need summary administration unless the account had a payable-on-death beneficiary.
