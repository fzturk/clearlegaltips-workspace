The short version (2026): Tennessee no longer uses a stand-alone small estate affidavit. Since its 2023 rewrite of the small estate law (T.C.A. 30-4-101 to -105), a family with $50,000 or less in personal property files a sworn petition with the probate court 45 days after the death, and the court issues limited letters that authorize collecting each listed asset. A bond equal to the property’s value is required unless the petitioner is the sole heir or beneficiary, or all adult heirs consent in writing. Real estate is not included.

Most websites describing a Tennessee small estate affidavit are out of date. Tennessee rewrote Chapter 4 of Title 30 in 2023. The old affidavit that an heir filed with the clerk has been replaced by a short court proceeding: a sworn petition, a short order, and limited letters that name the specific assets the family may collect.

The result is still far simpler than a full probate case, and the $50,000 limit is unchanged. But it is a court process with a bond question attached, and families who show up with an old affidavit form will be sent back. This guide explains how the current Tennessee process works, step by step.

What changed in 2023, and why older guides are wrong

Before the rewrite, a Tennessee heir used a small estate affidavit: after 45 days, the heir filed a sworn affidavit with the clerk, the clerk could require a bond, and certified copies of the filed affidavit were presented to banks. Many form websites and older articles still describe that process.

The current law keeps the $50,000 limit and the 45-day wait but changes the tool. The family now asks the court, by sworn petition, for limited letters. Three practical differences follow:

  • A court order replaces the clerk-filed affidavit. Institutions receive letters issued by the court rather than a copy of a private affidavit.
  • The letters are asset-specific. Each account or item the family may collect is listed, which makes banks more comfortable releasing funds.
  • The bond rule is written into the statute. A bond equal to the property value is the default, with clear waivers for sole heirs and for unanimous written consent.

If a clerk’s office hands you an affidavit form, ask whether it is the current small estate petition. Using the right paperwork the first time avoids a second trip to the courthouse, and a delay of several weeks while a corrected petition is prepared.

What counts as a Tennessee small estate

Under T.C.A. 30-4-102, a small estate is the probate estate of a decedent whose probate property does not exceed $50,000. “Property” here means personal property the decedent owned that would be subject to probate. The definition leaves out:

  • property held as tenants by the entirety or jointly with right of survivorship;
  • property payable to a named beneficiary rather than the estate, such as life insurance, retirement accounts and payable-on-death accounts; and
  • real property, which is outside the small estate procedure entirely.

Counted: bank and investment accounts in the decedent’s sole name with no beneficiary, vehicles, household goods and valuables, and money owed to the decedent.

Who can petition, and when

Who: one or more of the decedent’s competent adult heirs, or the personal representative named in the will. Where there is a will, it is presented with the petition.

When: after 45 days from the date of death, shown by a copy of the death certificate. The court may waive the 45-day wait for good cause, for example when a creditor action or a time-sensitive account makes waiting harmful.

Where: the probate court in the county where the decedent lived at death. In many Tennessee counties that is the chancery or probate court clerk’s office.

What the petition must include

The petition is sworn and must contain the standard information required for a probate petition under T.C.A. 30-1-117(a) (the decedent’s identity, residence and date of death, the heirs or beneficiaries and their addresses, and similar facts), plus:

  • an itemized list of each item of property and its value;
  • the identity of each creditor and the amount owed; and
  • a statement that the total value does not exceed $50,000 and that no real property is at issue.

The court then issues limited letters of administration (or limited letters testamentary if there is a will). The letters list the specific assets the personal representative may collect. Banks and other holders deal with the letters just as they would with ordinary letters, but only for the listed assets.

The bond: when you need one and how to avoid it

By default, the petitioner must post a bond payable to the clerk, with a corporate surety, in an amount equal to the value of the decedent’s property. The bond protects heirs and creditors if the property is mishandled.

The bond is waived when:

  • the petitioner is the sole heir or sole beneficiary; or
  • all the adult heirs and beneficiaries consent in writing.

In practice, most families get the bond waived by collecting signed consents from every adult heir before filing. If a minor or an incapacitated person is an heir, or an adult heir will not sign, expect to need a surety bond, which a bonding company issues for a premium.

How to use Tennessee’s small estate process, step by step

  1. Order certified death certificates.
  2. Confirm the property is personal property only and totals $50,000 or less after leaving out survivorship and beneficiary assets.
  3. List creditors and what each is owed, including the funeral home.
  4. Gather bond-waiver consents from all adult heirs and beneficiaries, unless the petitioner is the only one.
  5. Wait 45 days (or ask the court to waive the wait for good cause).
  6. File the sworn petition with the probate court clerk in the county of residence, with the death certificate, the will if any, the itemized property and creditor lists, and the consents. Pay the court’s filing fee.
  7. Receive limited letters listing the assets.
  8. Collect the listed assets, pay the creditors, and distribute the rest to the heirs or beneficiaries.
  9. Close out: the statute provides for discharge from the bond after the required period or upon filing the necessary documentation, so keep records of every payment and distribution.

A worked example

A Knoxville widow died in 2026 with a will leaving everything to her son and daughter equally. She owned a checking account with $21,000, a CD worth $15,000, and a car worth $8,000. Her house had been held with her late husband as tenants by the entirety and passed to her at his death; she later moved it into a revocable living trust for her children. Her final hospital bill was $2,400 and the funeral $9,000.

  • House: held in the living trust, so the trustee transfers it; it is real property and outside the small estate procedure anyway.
  • Personal property: $21,000 + $15,000 + $8,000 = $44,000, under $50,000.
  • Bond: both adult children are the only beneficiaries, so each signs a written consent and the bond is waived.

After 45 days, the son, named as executor, files the sworn petition with the will, the itemized property list and the two creditors. The court issues limited letters testamentary listing the checking account, the CD and the car. He collects them, pays the hospital and funeral, and splits the remainder with his sister.

Common mistakes in Tennessee

  • Using an old affidavit form. The pre-2023 affidavit process has been replaced by the petition for limited letters.
  • Including real estate. The small estate procedure covers personal property only.
  • Forgetting creditors. The petition must identify each creditor and the amount owed.
  • Missing a consent. Without written consent from every adult heir or beneficiary (or a sole heir petitioning), a surety bond is required.
  • Collecting unlisted assets. Limited letters authorize only the assets they list. Newly found property may need an amended petition.

When the small estate process does not fit

If the personal property exceeds $50,000, or the family needs to deal with real estate through the estate, a regular Tennessee administration follows. Our probate cost estimator can help you budget. For future planning, note that Tennessee does not recognize transfer on death deeds for real estate; bills to adopt them have been introduced repeatedly but none has passed. Tennessee families typically use survivorship titling, a life estate deed or a revocable living trust for real estate, and payable-on-death and beneficiary designations for accounts; see our guide to creating a living trust online.

How Tennessee compares

Tennessee’s $50,000 limit is below the national middle value of $75,000, and its court-issued limited letters are more formal than the affidavit-only approach in states like Virginia or Georgia’s bank rule. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Tennessee still have a small estate affidavit?

Not in the old form. Since the 2023 rewrite of T.C.A. 30-4-101 to -105, small estates use a sworn petition filed with the probate court, which then issues limited letters listing the assets the family may collect.

What is the Tennessee small estate limit?

$50,000 in personal property subject to probate. Real property, survivorship property and assets payable to a named beneficiary are not included.

How long do I have to wait in Tennessee?

45 days after the death. The court can waive the waiting period for good cause.

Do I need a bond for a Tennessee small estate?

Yes, equal to the property’s value, unless the petitioner is the sole heir or sole beneficiary, or all adult heirs and beneficiaries consent in writing.

Can a Tennessee small estate include a house?

No. The small estate procedure covers personal property only. Real estate passes by survivorship, a life estate deed, a living trust, the will through probate, or intestacy. Tennessee does not recognize transfer on death deeds.

What are limited letters?

Letters of administration or letters testamentary issued in a small estate that authorize the personal representative to collect only the specific assets listed. Banks and other holders treat them like ordinary letters for those assets.
