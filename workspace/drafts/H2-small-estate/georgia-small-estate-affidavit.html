The short version (2026): Georgia has no dollar-based small estate affidavit. It has two shortcuts instead. Banks and credit unions may pay a deceased depositor’s accounts, up to $15,000, straight to the spouse or next of kin on an affidavit when there is no will (O.C.G.A. §7-1-239). For everything else, heirs of a person who died without a will can ask the probate court for an order that no administration is necessary (§53-2-40). It has no dollar cap, but all heirs must agree on the division, and debts must be paid or creditors must consent.

If you are looking for a Georgia small estate affidavit, the closest match depends on what the person left behind. A single bank account under $15,000 can often be collected with a bank’s affidavit form. A larger estate, including a house, can skip formal administration entirely through a petition for an order declaring no administration necessary, as long as the family is in agreement.

That petition is unusual among state shortcuts because it is not limited by value. A family can use it for an estate of any size. It is also stricter than most, because it depends on unanimous agreement and on dealing with creditors. This guide explains both routes and how year’s support fits in.

Route 1: The $15,000 bank rule (O.C.G.A. §7-1-239)

Georgia law lets a financial institution pay a deceased depositor’s funds, up to $15,000 in total, without any administration of the estate and without a court order, when the depositor died without a will. The bank may pay, in this order:

  1. the surviving spouse;
  2. if no spouse, the children, in equal shares;
  3. if no spouse or children, the father and mother, in equal shares;
  4. if none of those, the brothers and sisters, in equal shares.

The family member signs an affidavit, and the institution may rely on it. Many probate courts and banks publish a “banking affidavit of surviving relative” for this purpose. The statute also allows an institution to apply up to $15,000 toward funeral and last-illness expenses if no family claimant comes forward within 45 days of the death.

Two limits to note: the rule is permissive (a bank may pay, but can insist on a court order), and it applies only to deposits at financial institutions, not to brokerage accounts, cars or real estate.

Route 2: Order that no administration is necessary (§53-2-40)

For anything beyond a small bank account, Georgia’s main shortcut is a petition to the probate court asking it to declare that no administration is necessary. Once the order is entered, the heirs can divide and transfer the property as they agreed, without a personal representative.

Who can use it

  • The person died intestate (without a will). If there is a will, it must go through probate instead.
  • No personal representative has been appointed in Georgia.
  • Any heir may file, but all heirs must have agreed on how to divide the estate.

What the petition must show

  • the decedent’s name and domicile;
  • the names, ages or majority status, and addresses of all heirs;
  • a description of the property in Georgia;
  • that the estate owes no debts, or that all creditors have consented or will be served; and
  • that the heirs have agreed on a division of the estate.

The heirs’ written agreement, with original signatures attested by a probate clerk or a notary, is attached. The standard statewide form is GPCSF 9, Petition for Order Declaring No Administration Necessary.

Where to file

In the probate court of the county where the decedent lived, or, for a non-Georgia resident, the county where Georgia real estate is located. If the order covers real estate, it is recorded, and the filing and recording fees are charged as costs to the estate.

Why the Georgia petition has no dollar limit

Most states protect creditors and absent heirs by capping the size of estates that can skip administration. Georgia protects them differently: through the unanimity requirement and the debt requirement. If every heir agrees and creditors are paid or have consented, there is little for an administrator to do, whatever the estate is worth.

That makes the petition powerful for estates with a house and no will, where many states would require full probate. It also makes it fragile: one heir who will not sign, or one creditor who objects, sends the family back to regular administration.

Year’s support: the spouse and minor children first

Georgia gives a surviving spouse and minor children a right to year’s support, property set aside from the estate for their support for a year after the death. Property awarded as year’s support is protected from most creditors. A petition for year’s support is filed in the probate court; the court issues a citation and publishes notice once a week for four weeks so interested persons can object.

Families sometimes use year’s support alongside or instead of a no-administration petition, especially when the estate is small and a spouse needs the house or accounts protected from creditors. Which combination fits depends on the debts, the heirs and the property involved.

How to proceed, step by step

  1. Check for a will. If there is one, it must be probated; the no-administration petition is for intestate estates only.
  2. Collect bank accounts under $15,000 with the bank’s affidavit of surviving relative, if the bank will accept it.
  3. Identify all heirs under Georgia intestacy law, including any minors or incapacitated heirs who will need a guardian’s signature.
  4. List all debts. Pay them, or get creditors’ written consent, or plan to serve them.
  5. Agree on the division in writing, with every heir’s signature attested by a clerk or notary.
  6. File GPCSF 9 with the probate court in the county of domicile and pay the filing fee.
  7. After the order, transfer the property as agreed and record the order in the deed records for any real estate.

A worked example

A Macon woman died in 2026 without a will. Her husband had died earlier, and she left three adult children. Her estate: a paid-off house worth $190,000, a credit union account with $11,000, a car worth $7,000, and a final hospital bill of $3,200.

  • Credit union ($11,000): under $15,000, so the children can ask the credit union to pay them in equal shares on the affidavit of surviving relative.
  • House and car: no dollar cap applies to the no-administration petition. The children agree that the eldest will keep the house and buy out the others, and that the car goes to the youngest.
  • Debt: they pay the $3,200 hospital bill before filing, so the petition can state the estate owes no debts.

They file GPCSF 9 with their signed agreement in the Bibb County Probate Court. Once the order is entered, it is recorded with the deed, and the house is retitled without anyone serving as administrator.

Common mistakes in Georgia small estates

  • Filing the petition when there is a will. The no-administration order is only for people who died without a will.
  • Collecting without every heir on board. The petition depends on a written agreement signed by all heirs. A missing half-sibling or a grandchild representing a deceased child is the classic gap.
  • Ignoring minors. A minor heir cannot sign the agreement personally; a guardian usually has to act, which can require its own court step.
  • Treating the bank rule as automatic. Banks may pay up to $15,000 under §7-1-239, but they are not required to.
  • Forgetting to record the order. If real estate is included, the order should be recorded so the land records show who owns the property. Title companies will look for it when the house is later sold or refinanced.

When these shortcuts do not fit

  • There is a will: it must be probated, often in solemn form or common form. The no-administration petition is not available.
  • An heir will not agree or cannot be found: regular administration.
  • Creditors will not consent and cannot be paid: regular administration, where the administrator deals with claims.

How Georgia compares

Georgia is one of only two jurisdictions, with New Hampshire, whose main small estate route has no dollar limit. In exchange it demands unanimous agreement and resolved debts, which most states do not. Its $15,000 bank rule is small next to affidavit limits elsewhere. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Georgia have a small estate affidavit?

Not a general one with a dollar limit. Georgia allows banks to pay up to $15,000 in deposits to close family on an affidavit when there is no will, and it lets heirs petition the probate court for an order that no administration is necessary, which has no dollar cap.

Is there a dollar limit for “no administration necessary” in Georgia?

No. The petition under O.C.G.A. §53-2-40 is not limited by estate value. It requires that the person died without a will, that all heirs agree on the division, and that debts are paid or creditors consent or are served.

Can I use no administration necessary if there is a will?

No. The petition is only for intestate estates. A will must be offered for probate in the probate court.

Can a Georgia bank release money without probate?

Yes, up to $15,000 in total, when the depositor died without a will. The bank may pay the spouse, then the children, then the parents, then the siblings, relying on their affidavit. Banks can still require a court order.

Does the no-administration order transfer real estate?

Yes. The order covers the estate property described in the petition, including real estate, and it is recorded so the heirs’ agreed division appears in the land records.

What is year’s support in Georgia?

It is property set aside from the estate for the surviving spouse and minor children for their support during the year after the death. The probate court publishes notice for four weeks before granting it, and property awarded as year’s support is protected from most creditors.
