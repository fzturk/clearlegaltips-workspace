The short version (2027): Divorce requirements by state come down to three numbers: how long you must live in the state before filing, how long you must wait after filing, and what the court charges to open the case. Six months of residency is the most common rule (26 of 51 jurisdictions), ten states set one year, and four have no minimum period at all. Fifteen jurisdictions have no statutory waiting period, while California makes every couple wait six months and a day from service. Every state now offers a no-fault divorce, but North Carolina, South Carolina, Virginia, Vermont and Louisiana first require a period of living apart.

Getting divorced is a state-law process, and the rules change sharply at each state line. A couple who could be divorced in about three weeks in Florida might wait six months in California, and a spouse who moved to New York last spring may not be able to file there at all until next year. Those differences decide when you can file, which court hears the case, and the earliest date your divorce can become final.

This page puts divorce requirements for all 50 states and the District of Columbia in one table: residency rules, waiting periods, the no-fault ground each state uses, filing fees where the state sets one, and a link to the statute for every row. Key Findings From the 2027 Data

  • Six months is the standard residency rule. 26 jurisdictions, including Texas, California, Florida, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Georgia and Michigan, require about six months (180 days) of residency before filing.
  • Ten states require a year. Connecticut, Iowa, Massachusetts, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Rhode Island, South Carolina and West Virginia use one year as their main rule, although most have exceptions, such as South Carolina’s three months when both spouses live there, or Massachusetts when the cause arose in the state.
  • Four states have no minimum period. Alaska, Louisiana, South Dakota and Washington require that the filing spouse be a resident or domiciliary when the case is filed, without a fixed number of days.
  • The shortest fixed periods are six weeks. Idaho and Nevada require only six weeks of residency, and Arkansas, Kansas and Wyoming require 60 days.
  • Fifteen jurisdictions have no statutory waiting period. In Delaware, D.C., Hawaii, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Dakota, Oregon and West Virginia, the law sets no minimum gap between filing and judgment. The court’s calendar decides how fast it goes.
  • California has the longest mandatory wait for everyone. A California divorce cannot be final until six months and one day after the respondent is served or appears, even when both spouses agree on everything.
  • Children lengthen the wait in five states. Michigan (60 days becomes six months), Tennessee (60 becomes 90 days), Oklahoma (none becomes 90 days), Louisiana (180 becomes 365 days apart) and Kentucky (a 60-day wait before testimony) all add time when there are minor children.
  • Five states make you live apart first. North Carolina and South Carolina require a year of separation for a no-fault divorce, Virginia six months or a year, Vermont six consecutive months, and Louisiana 180 or 365 days.

What Changed Recently

Divorce statutes change slowly, but three jurisdictions rewrote key rules in the last few years, and many online guides still describe the old law:

  • District of Columbia (2024). D.C. Law 25-115 removed the separation requirement. A divorce can now be granted when one or both spouses no longer wish to remain married. The six-month residency rule stays.
  • Maryland (2023). Maryland replaced its old grounds, including the 12-month separation and limited divorce, with three no-fault grounds: a six-month separation, irreconcilable differences, and mutual consent. There is no longer any requirement to live apart before filing on irreconcilable differences.
  • Utah (September 1, 2024). Utah moved its divorce law from Title 30 to a new Title 81. Section 81-4-402 now holds the rules: 90 days of residence in the county before filing and a 30-day wait after filing. Older articles cite sections that no longer exist.

We also recheck court fee schedules each January, when many counties adjust their filing fees. Harris County, Texas, for example, published a new schedule effective January 1, 2026: $350 for a divorce without children and $365 with children.

Residency Requirements Explained

A court can only end a marriage if it has jurisdiction, and for divorce that usually means at least one spouse lives in the state. Residency rules exist so that people cannot shop for a court in a state with more favorable law. Four details make them trickier than a single number:

  • Who has to meet it. In most states either spouse can satisfy the rule, so if you just moved but your spouse has lived in the state for years, the case may still be filed there. A few states look only at the person filing.
  • When it is measured. Most states measure residency on the day you file. Some measure it when the decree is entered: Connecticut lets you file at any time but generally requires 12 months of residence before the judgment, and Vermont requires six months to file and a year before the final hearing.
  • County rules. Several states add a county requirement on top of the state rule: 90 days in the county in Texas, three months in California and Indiana, 30 days in Wisconsin and Oklahoma, 90 days in Ohio under the civil rules, and ten days in Michigan.
  • Exceptions. Many one-year states shorten the period when the marriage took place in the state, when both spouses live there, or when the grounds arose there. New York, for instance, accepts one year of residency if the couple married in New York, lived there as a married couple, or the grounds arose there, and otherwise requires two years.

Military families have extra options. Many states let a service member, or a spouse, file where the service member is stationed, even without meeting the usual residency period. Check the state statute or ask the base legal assistance office.

Waiting Periods: The Rule That Sets Your Timeline

If residency decides where you can file, the waiting period decides when it can end. States use four different starting points, and it matters which one applies:

No state lets couples skip the waiting period by agreement. An online service, a lawyer or a signed settlement can make sure the paperwork is ready on the first possible day, but it cannot make the court sign earlier than the statute allows. The flip side is that the waiting period is a minimum: most uncontested cases take somewhat longer because of court scheduling, and contested cases often take a year or more.

No-Fault Grounds in Every State

Every state now allows a divorce without proving that either spouse did something wrong. The wording varies: “irretrievably broken” (Florida, Arizona, Colorado, Georgia), “irreconcilable differences” (California, Illinois, Tennessee, Utah), “insupportability” (Texas), “incompatibility” (Kansas, New Mexico, Oklahoma) or “breakdown of the marriage” (Michigan, Iowa). In practice they mean the same thing: the marriage cannot be saved.

A handful of states attach conditions to the no-fault ground:

  • Separation first. North Carolina and South Carolina require one year of living apart; Virginia requires six months (with no minor children and a signed separation agreement) or one year; Vermont six consecutive months; and Louisiana 180 days, or 365 days with minor children.
  • A sworn statement of time. New York’s no-fault ground requires one spouse to swear that the marriage has been irretrievably broken for at least six months.
  • Agreement of both spouses. Mississippi grants an irreconcilable-differences divorce only when both spouses agree to it and to the terms (or ask the court to decide the terms). Otherwise a fault ground is needed.
  • Long separation only. Arkansas’s only no-fault ground is 18 months of separation, so many uncontested Arkansas divorces are filed on a fault ground such as general indignities.

Most states still keep fault grounds such as adultery or cruelty, and in some states fault can affect alimony or the division of property. That is a question for a family law attorney in your state.

How an Uncontested Divorce Works, Step by Step

The forms and names differ, but an uncontested divorce follows the same basic path in almost every state:

  1. Confirm residency and the right county. Check the table above. File in a county where you or your spouse lives, and meet any county residency rule.
  2. Agree on the terms. Property and debts, alimony, and, if you have children, custody, a parenting schedule and child support. Our parenting plan template and alimony calculator can help you prepare.
  3. Complete the court forms. Most state court systems publish free official forms and instructions. The petition (or complaint) starts the case; a financial affidavit or disclosure is required in most states.
  4. File and pay the fee. File with the clerk of the family or circuit court, in person or through the state’s e-filing system, and pay the fee or file a fee waiver.
  5. Serve your spouse. Your spouse must receive formal notice. In an uncontested case, the easiest route is usually a signed acceptance or waiver of service, which many states allow.
  6. Exchange financial disclosures. States like California require both spouses to exchange disclosures even when they agree on everything.
  7. Sign the settlement agreement. Signatures usually need to be notarized or made under penalty of perjury.
  8. Wait out the statutory period. The clock starts at filing, service or separation, depending on your state.
  9. Finalize. Some states require a short hearing (often a few minutes); others let the judge sign the decree on the papers. Get certified copies once it is signed.

When You Have Minor Children

Children add requirements in nearly every state, even when the parents agree:

  • Longer waiting periods in Michigan, Tennessee, Oklahoma, Louisiana and Kentucky, as noted above.
  • A parenting plan covering the schedule, decision-making and holidays. Many states require a specific form; see our child custody agreement template for the typical content.
  • Child support worksheets using the state’s guideline formula, which the judge checks even when parents agree on an amount.
  • Parenting classes in many counties, completed before the final hearing.
  • Jurisdiction over custody. Custody is decided by a different rule than divorce: under the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act, adopted in almost every state, the child’s “home state” (where the child lived for the last six months) usually decides custody. You might be able to get divorced in one state while custody must be decided in another.

Divorce vs. Legal Separation

A legal separation divides property and sets support and custody but leaves the marriage in place. People choose it for religious reasons, to keep health insurance or benefits, or because they are not sure about divorce. Not every state offers it, and some (such as Texas) have no formal legal separation at all. Our guide on how to file for legal separation covers the differences.

Do not confuse legal separation with the separation periods in North Carolina, South Carolina, Virginia, Vermont and Louisiana. Those are simply time spent living apart, usually without any court filing, that the law requires before a no-fault divorce.

Filing in a Different State Than Your Spouse

When spouses live in different states, either one can usually file where they live once they meet that state’s residency rule. A few points to keep in mind:

  • The state where you file can end the marriage, but it may not be able to divide property or order support against a spouse who has never lived there and has no other connection to it.
  • Custody follows the child’s home state, not the state where the divorce is filed.
  • Waiting periods and filing fees follow the state where you file, so the choice can change your timeline by months.

If you and your spouse live in different states and you have significant property or children, a consultation with a family law attorney before filing is often worth the cost.

Common Mistakes That Delay a Divorce

  • Filing before meeting residency. The court can dismiss the case, and you lose the filing fee. Count the days carefully.
  • Filing before a required separation ends. In North Carolina and South Carolina the year apart must usually be complete before filing a no-fault case.
  • Waiting to serve the other spouse. In states where the clock starts at service, every week of delay pushes the final date back.
  • Using outdated forms. Courts revise forms regularly, and old versions can be rejected. Download them from the official court site the week you file.
  • Leaving out retirement accounts. Dividing a 401(k) or pension usually needs a separate court order (a QDRO). If the decree is silent, fixing it later is expensive.
  • Assuming the divorce is final when you sign. It is final when the judge signs the decree and, in some states, after a further period (such as the Massachusetts nisi period). Do not remarry before then.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which state has the shortest residency requirement for divorce?

Alaska, Louisiana, South Dakota and Washington have no fixed minimum; the filing spouse must be a resident or domiciliary when the case is filed. Among states with a set period, Idaho and Nevada require six weeks.

Which states have no waiting period for divorce?

Fifteen jurisdictions have no statutory waiting period: Delaware, the District of Columbia, Hawaii, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Dakota, Oregon and West Virginia. The court’s schedule still decides how long it takes.

What is the fastest state to get a divorce?

For couples who agree on everything, states with a short residency rule and no waiting period, such as Nevada (six weeks’ residency, no wait), can finalize quickly. You still have to actually live in the state for the residency period, and a divorce in a state where neither spouse lives is open to challenge.

Can I file for divorce in a state I just moved to?

Usually not right away. Most states require six months or a year of residency before filing. If your spouse still lives in the state you left, you may be able to file there instead.

Do all states allow no-fault divorce?

Yes. Every state and D.C. allows a no-fault divorce, but some require a period of living apart first, including North Carolina and South Carolina (one year), Virginia (six months or one year), Vermont (six months) and Louisiana (180 or 365 days).

How much does it cost to file for divorce?

Filing fees vary by state and often by county. Examples we verified: $230 in Colorado, $335 in New York, $350 or $365 in Harris County, Texas, $408 in Collier County, Florida, and $435 in California. Every state offers a fee waiver if you cannot afford it.

Does the waiting period start when I file?

It depends on the state. It runs from filing in states like Texas and Florida, from service on the other spouse in states like California, Arizona and Georgia, and from separation in states like North Carolina and Virginia.

The Bottom Line

Before you file, check three things for your state: whether you meet the residency rule today, what starts the waiting-period clock, and what the court charges. Then get your agreement in writing and use the official court forms. If you and your spouse agree on everything, an uncontested divorce in most states costs little more than the filing fee and ends shortly after the waiting period. If there are children, significant property or a disagreement, talk to a family law attorney before you sign anything.
