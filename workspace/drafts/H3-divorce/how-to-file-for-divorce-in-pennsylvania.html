The short version (2027): Here is how to file for divorce in Pennsylvania in brief: one spouse must have lived in the state for 6 months. If you both agree, a mutual consent divorce can be finalized after both spouses sign consent affidavits at least 90 days after the complaint is served. If your spouse will not agree, you can proceed on your own after living separate and apart for one year. Pennsylvania divides marital property by equitable distribution, and you must raise property and alimony claims before the decree.

Pennsylvania has two no-fault paths, and choosing the right one is the most important decision in the case. Couples who agree can use the mutual consent route and be divorced in about three to four months. If one spouse refuses to cooperate, the other can still get a divorce, but only after a year of living apart. Either way, the process runs through the county’s Court of Common Pleas, and each county has its own family court office, fee schedule and local rules.

Pennsylvania Residency Requirement: 6 Months

Under 23 Pa.C.S. §3104(b), no spouse is entitled to a divorce unless at least one of them has been a bona fide resident of Pennsylvania for at least six months immediately before the complaint is filed. There is no county residency rule; you file in the Court of Common Pleas of a county where either spouse lives, or in certain other counties if both agree.

“Bona fide resident” means you actually live in Pennsylvania and intend to stay. A driver’s license, lease, voter registration or tax return with a Pennsylvania address helps show it.

The Two No-Fault Grounds

Pennsylvania law (23 Pa.C.S. §3301) offers two no-fault routes, plus fault grounds that are now rarely used.

Mutual consent: §3301(c)

The court may grant a divorce where the marriage is irretrievably broken, 90 days have passed since the action began, and each spouse files an affidavit consenting to the divorce. Under the Pennsylvania Rules of Civil Procedure, the consent affidavits cannot be signed until at least 90 days after the complaint is served. Both spouses then waive the notice of intention to request the decree, and the case can be finalized.

This is the fastest route and the one most uncontested Pennsylvania divorces use. There is a narrow exception: when a spouse has been convicted of a personal injury crime against the other, consent can be presumed.

Irretrievable breakdown after separation: §3301(d)

If your spouse will not sign a consent, you can still get a divorce by filing an affidavit stating that you have lived separate and apart for at least one year and the marriage is irretrievably broken. The other spouse can accept or deny the allegations; if they deny them, the court decides. The separation period was shortened from two years to one year for separations beginning on or after December 5, 2016.

“Separate and apart” means no cohabitation, and in Pennsylvania it can happen even under the same roof if you truly live separate lives. The date of separation often matters for property as well, so write it down.

Fault grounds

Fault grounds under §3301(a), such as desertion for one year, adultery, cruel and barbarous treatment, bigamy, imprisonment for two years or more, and indignities, still exist but are rarely worth the cost of proving.

Each county’s Prothonotary or Office of Judicial Records sets its fees under state law, so costs differ across the state. Philadelphia’s Office of Judicial Records publishes this schedule, effective November 12, 2025:

Fee waiver. If you cannot afford the fees, file a Petition to Proceed In Forma Pauperis with a sworn statement of your income, expenses and assets. If the court grants it, the filing fees are waived.

Pennsylvania Divorce Forms

The Unified Judicial System of Pennsylvania publishes self-help information on divorce, and most county family court offices provide packets for mutual consent divorces. A typical mutual consent case uses:

  • Notice to Defend and Claim Rights, which must be the first page of the complaint;
  • Complaint in Divorce, stating the ground (§3301(c) or (d)) and any related claims;
  • Proof of service (an affidavit of service or the signed certified mail receipt);
  • Affidavit of Consent from each spouse, signed at least 90 days after service;
  • Waiver of Notice of Intention to Request Entry of a Divorce Decree from each spouse;
  • Praecipe to Transmit Record, which asks the court to enter the decree; and
  • Divorce Decree and, if there is one, the property settlement agreement.

Many counties also require a cover sheet, a vital statistics record form, or local forms. Check your county’s family court website before you file.

How to File for Divorce in Pennsylvania, Step by Step

  1. Confirm residency (six months for either spouse) and choose the ground: mutual consent (§3301(c)) or one-year separation (§3301(d)).
  2. Prepare the complaint with the Notice to Defend. Add counts for equitable distribution, alimony, custody or support if you need the court to decide them.
  3. File with the Prothonotary or Office of Judicial Records in the right county and pay the fee (or file an IFP petition).
  4. Serve your spouse within 30 days of filing, by personal delivery by an adult who is not a party, or by certified mail, restricted delivery, with a return receipt signed by your spouse. File proof of service.
  5. Wait 90 days after service.
  6. Sign the consent affidavits. Each spouse signs an Affidavit of Consent and a Waiver of Notice of Intention and files them.
  7. Finalize the property settlement, if you have one, and make sure it resolves every claim you raised.
  8. File the Praecipe to Transmit Record with the proposed decree and pay the transmittal fee.
  9. Receive the decree. A judge reviews the file and signs the Divorce Decree, usually without a hearing.

Property: Equitable Distribution

Pennsylvania divides marital property (generally what either spouse acquired from the wedding to the date of final separation) by equitable distribution under 23 Pa.C.S. §3502. Equitable means fair, not necessarily equal; the court considers factors such as the length of the marriage, each spouse’s income and earning capacity, contributions as a homemaker, and the tax consequences.

The key procedural point: raise every economic claim before the decree. Once the divorce decree is entered, unresolved claims for equitable distribution and alimony are generally lost unless the court kept them open (bifurcation). If you are not sure your agreement covers everything, get advice before signing the consent.

Alimony and Support

Pennsylvania distinguishes three kinds of support between spouses: spousal support (before a divorce complaint is filed), alimony pendente lite (while the case is pending) and alimony after the decree, which a court awards only when it is necessary, considering the factors in §3701. Support guidelines set by the Pennsylvania Supreme Court are used for spousal support and alimony pendente lite. Our alimony calculator by state gives a general comparison.

Divorce With Children in Pennsylvania

  • Custody. Pennsylvania distinguishes legal custody (major decisions) from physical custody (where the child lives) and decides by the best-interest factors in 23 Pa.C.S. §5328. Many counties require a parenting class and a custody conference before any hearing. Our parenting plan template helps you work out a schedule.
  • Child support. Support is set by statewide guidelines based on both parents’ incomes and is usually handled through the county Domestic Relations Section.
  • Separate from the divorce. Custody and support can be handled in the divorce complaint or in separate actions, and they do not have to be resolved before a mutual consent decree.

Common Mistakes in Pennsylvania Divorces

  • Signing the consent too early. An affidavit of consent signed before 90 days after service is invalid and must be redone.
  • Serving by regular mail. Certified mail must be restricted delivery with a return receipt signed by your spouse. Otherwise use personal service.
  • Losing economic claims. If you did not raise equitable distribution or alimony before the decree, you may not be able to later.
  • Waiting on a spouse who will not sign. If your spouse will not consent, plan for the one-year separation route under §3301(d) instead.
  • Forgetting the praecipe. The case does not end on its own. Without a Praecipe to Transmit Record, no decree is entered.

Online Divorce in Pennsylvania

Many Pennsylvania counties, including Philadelphia, accept electronic filing in family cases, and the forms for a mutual consent divorce are simple enough for many couples to prepare themselves. How Pennsylvania Compares

Pennsylvania’s 90-day mutual consent path is slower than Florida (20 days) but much faster than California (six months). Its one-year separation route for a non-consenting spouse is similar to the separation requirements in North Carolina and South Carolina. Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to live in Pennsylvania to file for divorce?

At least one spouse must have been a bona fide resident of Pennsylvania for six months immediately before filing (23 Pa.C.S. §3104(b)).

How long does a mutual consent divorce take in Pennsylvania?

The consent affidavits cannot be signed until 90 days after the complaint is served, so most mutual consent divorces take about three to four months if the paperwork is complete.

Can I get divorced in Pennsylvania if my spouse won’t sign?

Yes. After living separate and apart for at least one year, you can file an affidavit under §3301(d) and ask the court to grant the divorce without your spouse’s consent.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in Pennsylvania?

It varies by county. Philadelphia charges $334.73 to file the complaint and $66.22 for the praecipe to transmit the record (schedule effective November 12, 2025). You can petition to proceed in forma pauperis if you cannot afford the fees.

Do I have to go to court for a Pennsylvania divorce?

Usually not for a mutual consent divorce with an agreement. The judge signs the decree on the papers. Contested property or custody issues can require a master’s hearing or a custody conference.

What happens to property claims after the divorce decree?

Claims for equitable distribution and alimony must generally be raised and resolved before the decree. After the decree, unresolved claims are usually lost unless the court kept them open.
