The short version (2027): Here is how to file for divorce in Illinois (legally a dissolution of marriage) in brief: one spouse must have lived in Illinois for 90 days, one of the shortest residency rules in the country. Illinois is no-fault only: the single ground is irreconcilable differences, and there is no statutory waiting period. Filing costs $388 in Cook County, and the other spouse pays $250 to file an appearance. Couples married 8 years or less with no children and modest income and property can use a joint simplified dissolution, often finished in one court date.

Illinois rewrote its divorce law in 2016. Fault grounds such as adultery and cruelty were abolished, the old two-year separation rule was removed, “custody” became the “allocation of parental responsibilities,” and a statutory maintenance formula replaced much of the guesswork. The result is a simpler system: one ground, a short residency rule, and statewide standardized forms that people without lawyers can use.

This guide explains how to file for divorce in Illinois step by step: residency, the ground, the joint simplified option, fees, forms, service, property, maintenance and children. Rules link to the Illinois Marriage and Dissolution of Marriage Act (750 ILCS 5), checked in September 2026. For other states, see our divorce requirements by state table.

Illinois Residency Requirement: 90 Days

Under 750 ILCS 5/401(a), the court can enter a judgment of dissolution if, when the case was started, one spouse was a resident of Illinois (or stationed there as a member of the armed services) and that residence or military presence had been maintained for 90 days before filing or before the court’s finding. There is no separate county requirement; you usually file in the county where either spouse lives.

Ninety days is among the shortest residency periods in the country, after the states with no fixed period and the six-week rules in Idaho and Nevada.

Grounds: Irreconcilable Differences Only

Since January 1, 2016, Illinois has had a single ground for dissolution: irreconcilable differences have caused the irretrievable breakdown of the marriage, and reconciliation has failed or would be impracticable (§401(a)). Fault is not a ground, and marital misconduct is generally not considered in dividing property or setting maintenance.

If the spouses have lived separate and apart for at least six continuous months immediately before the judgment, the law creates an irrebuttable presumption that the ground is met (§401(a-5)). Without six months apart, the ground can still be proven; the other spouse simply has room to argue. In an uncontested case, both spouses usually agree that irreconcilable differences exist.

Is There a Waiting Period?

No. Illinois has no statutory waiting period between filing and judgment. An uncontested divorce is usually finished at a short “prove-up” hearing once the paperwork is complete, which can be within a few weeks to a few months depending on the county. A joint simplified dissolution can often be finished on the day of the hearing. Contested cases commonly take a year or more.

Joint Simplified Dissolution

Illinois offers a streamlined joint procedure under 750 ILCS 5/452. Both spouses must certify that all of the following are true:

  • the marriage has lasted 8 years or less;
  • no children were born to or adopted by the spouses during the marriage, and the wife is not pregnant;
  • neither spouse owns real estate or retirement benefits, other than IRAs worth less than $10,000 combined;
  • the total value of marital property, after debts, is less than $50,000;
  • combined gross income is less than $60,000, and neither spouse earns more than $30,000;
  • each spouse waives maintenance;
  • the spouses have exchanged tax returns and disclosed their assets and debts, and have a written agreement dividing any assets worth more than $100 (and any companion animals); and
  • at least one spouse meets the 90-day residency rule.

Both spouses file the joint petition and appear together before the judge, who can enter the judgment the same day. If any condition is not met, use a regular petition for dissolution.

You qualify if you receive need-based public benefits, your household income is at or below 125% of the federal poverty level, or paying would cause substantial hardship; partial waivers are available at higher incomes.

Illinois Divorce Forms

The Illinois Supreme Court approves statewide standardized forms that every circuit court must accept, available free from Illinois Courts and Illinois Legal Aid Online. A typical case uses:

  • Petition for Dissolution of Marriage (with or without children) or the Joint Petition for Simplified Dissolution;
  • Summons and, for the other spouse, an Appearance (and often a waiver of service or answer);
  • Financial Affidavit, required from both spouses in most cases;
  • Marital Settlement Agreement and, with children, an Allocation Judgment and Parenting Plan and a Uniform Order for Support;
  • Judgment for Dissolution of Marriage; and
  • the Certificate of Dissolution for the state vital records office.

Illinois requires e-filing for most civil cases, including domestic relations, through an approved e-filing service provider; self-represented litigants can ask for an exemption.

How to File for Divorce in Illinois, Step by Step

  1. Check residency: 90 days for either spouse.
  2. Choose the procedure: joint simplified dissolution if you qualify; otherwise a regular petition.
  3. Complete the petition and financial affidavit using the statewide forms.
  4. E-file with the Circuit Clerk in the county where either spouse lives and pay the fee, or file the fee waiver application.
  5. Serve your spouse by sheriff or special process server, or have your spouse file an appearance and sign a waiver. A served spouse has 30 days to file an appearance and response.
  6. Exchange financial information and sign the marital settlement agreement (and, with children, the parenting plan and allocation judgment).
  7. Complete a parenting class if you have children and your county requires it.
  8. Schedule the prove-up. The filing spouse testifies briefly about residency, irreconcilable differences and the agreement. Many courts allow remote appearances.
  9. Get the signed judgment and certified copies.

Property: Equitable Division

Illinois is an equitable distribution state. Under 750 ILCS 5/503, each spouse keeps their non-marital property (owned before the marriage, gifts, inheritances and certain other items), and the court divides marital property in “just proportions” without regard to misconduct, considering factors such as each spouse’s contribution, the length of the marriage, and each spouse’s economic circumstances. Retirement accounts usually need a separate QDRO.

Maintenance: The Illinois Formula

Illinois uses a statutory formula for maintenance (alimony) when the court finds it appropriate and the spouses’ combined gross annual income is less than $500,000 and the payor has no prior support obligations (750 ILCS 5/504(b-1)):

  • Amount: 33⅓% of the payor’s net annual income minus 25% of the payee’s net annual income, but the payee’s income plus maintenance cannot exceed 40% of the spouses’ combined net income.
  • Duration: the length of the marriage multiplied by a factor that rises from 0.20 for marriages under five years to 0.80 for marriages of 19 to 20 years. For marriages of 20 years or more, the court may order maintenance for the length of the marriage or for an indefinite term.

The court first decides whether maintenance is appropriate at all. See our alimony calculator by state for comparisons.

Divorce With Children in Illinois

  • Parental responsibilities. Illinois allocates “decision-making responsibilities” and “parenting time” instead of custody. Each parent must file a proposed parenting plan within 120 days after service or the filing of the petition, or the parents can file one agreed plan. Our parenting plan template covers the typical terms.
  • Child support. Illinois uses an income shares model, combining both parents’ net incomes and dividing the guideline amount in proportion to income.
  • Mediation and classes. Many counties require a parenting education class and mediation of contested parenting issues.
  • No simplified option. Couples with children must use a regular petition.

Common Mistakes in Illinois Divorces

  • Choosing joint simplified when you do not qualify. One disqualifying fact, such as owning a home or a 401(k), means you need a regular petition.
  • Missing the financial affidavit. Judges often will not finish a prove-up without complete financial affidavits.
  • Relying on fault. Illinois does not consider fault, so evidence of misconduct rarely helps in property or maintenance.
  • Forgetting the parenting plan deadline. Parents must file a proposed plan within 120 days.
  • Not filing an appearance. A spouse who does not file an appearance risks a default judgment.

How Illinois Compares

Illinois combines a short 90-day residency rule with no waiting period, making it faster than Pennsylvania (90 days after service) or Texas (60 days). Its Cook County fee is among the higher ones in the country, and its maintenance formula is one of the most specific. See all 50 states in our divorce requirements by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to live in Illinois to file for divorce?

One spouse must have lived in Illinois (or been stationed there in the military) for 90 days before filing or before the court’s finding (750 ILCS 5/401(a)).

Is there a waiting period for divorce in Illinois?

No. Illinois has no statutory waiting period. Uncontested divorces are finished at a short prove-up hearing once the paperwork is complete, and a joint simplified dissolution can be finished the same day.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in Illinois?

In Cook County, $388 to file the petition and $250 for the other spouse’s appearance. Other counties are usually lower. You can apply for a fee waiver if you cannot afford it.

Who qualifies for a joint simplified dissolution in Illinois?

Couples married 8 years or less with no children, no real estate, marital property under $50,000, combined income under $60,000 (neither over $30,000), and who both waive maintenance, among other conditions in 750 ILCS 5/452.

Does Illinois still have fault divorce?

No. Since 2016, irreconcilable differences is the only ground. If spouses have lived apart for six months before judgment, the ground is conclusively presumed.

How is maintenance calculated in Illinois?

When combined income is under $500,000, guideline maintenance is 33⅓% of the payor’s net income minus 25% of the payee’s net income, capped so the payee does not receive more than 40% of combined net income, for a duration based on the length of the marriage.
