The short version (2027): Before you learn how to file for divorce in New York, check residency: you generally need one year of residency if you married in New York, lived there as a married couple, or the grounds arose there, and two years otherwise. Divorce cases are heard in the Supreme Court, New York’s trial-level court.

New York’s divorce process has a reputation for paperwork, and it is earned. An uncontested divorce uses a dozen or more forms, several required notices, and a set of statutory fees. But once you understand the sequence, it is very doable without a lawyer: the courts publish a free online program that generates the uncontested divorce forms, and most uncontested cases are decided on the papers without anyone appearing before a judge.

This guide explains how to file for divorce in New York step by step: the residency rules, the no-fault ground, fees and waivers, service, the required notices, property, maintenance and child support. New York Residency Requirements

Domestic Relations Law §230 lets you file for divorce in New York only if one of these is true:

  1. you married in New York and either spouse has lived there continuously for one year before filing;
  2. you lived in New York as a married couple and either spouse has lived there continuously for one year before filing;
  3. the grounds arose in New York and either spouse has lived there continuously for one year before filing;
  4. the grounds arose in New York and both spouses live there when the case is filed; or
  5. either spouse has lived in New York continuously for two years before filing.

Most couples who married or lived together in New York qualify after one year. If you married elsewhere and moved to New York alone, you may need to wait two years, or file in the state where your spouse lives.

Grounds: Irretrievable Breakdown for Six Months

New York was the last state to adopt no-fault divorce, in 2010. Under DRL §170(7), a divorce may be granted when the relationship “has broken down irretrievably for a period of at least six months,” provided that one spouse has so stated under oath. The other spouse does not have to agree, and nobody has to prove fault.

There is an important condition: the court cannot grant a divorce on this ground until all economic issues (property, maintenance, child support, counsel fees) and custody and visitation have been resolved, either by agreement or by the court. New York still keeps fault grounds (cruel and inhuman treatment, abandonment, imprisonment, adultery) and grounds based on a separation agreement or judgment of separation, but almost all uncontested divorces use §170(7).

Is There a Waiting Period?

No. New York has no statutory waiting period between filing and judgment. The six months in §170(7) is how long the marriage must have been broken before you swear to it, not a wait after filing.

In practice, uncontested New York divorces often take several months because of court processing, and the time varies a lot by county. Contested divorces usually take a year or more.

If a motion is needed, it usually costs $45, and certified copies of the judgment are extra. Service by a professional process server is a separate cost, though any adult over 18 who is not a party can serve the papers.

Fee waiver. If you cannot afford the fees, apply for poor person relief under CPLR §1101 with a sworn affidavit about your income and assets. If the court grants it, the index number, RJI and note of issue fees are waived.

New York Uncontested Divorce Forms

The New York State Unified Court System publishes an uncontested divorce packet and a free online DIY Uncontested Divorce Program that fills in the forms from your answers. A typical case uses:

  • Summons with Notice (or Summons and Verified Complaint), which starts the case;
  • Notice of Automatic Orders, Notice of Guideline Maintenance, and Notice Concerning Continuation of Health Care Coverage, all served with the summons;
  • Affidavit of Service;
  • Affidavit of Defendant, in which the other spouse consents to the divorce and waives further notice;
  • Affidavit of Plaintiff, including the sworn statement under §170(7);
  • Sworn Statement of Removal of Barriers to Remarriage, if you married in a religious ceremony (DRL §253);
  • Child Support Worksheet and support forms, if you have children;
  • Settlement agreement (if any), Note of Issue, Request for Judicial Intervention, Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law, Judgment of Divorce, and the state Department of Health Certificate of Dissolution.

How to File for Divorce in New York, Step by Step

  1. Check residency under DRL §230.
  2. Prepare the Summons with Notice. Use the court’s DIY program or the uncontested divorce packet.
  3. Buy an index number. File the summons with the County Clerk in the county where either spouse lives, in person or through NYSCEF e-filing where available, and pay $210 (or file for poor person relief).
  4. Serve your spouse. An adult who is not a party must personally deliver the summons and the required notices. You have 120 days after filing to serve (CPLR §306-b). You cannot serve the papers yourself, and mail is not enough.
  5. Get the defendant’s response. In an uncontested case, your spouse signs the Affidavit of Defendant. A spouse served in New York has 20 days to respond (30 days if served another way or outside the state); if there is no response, you can proceed on default.
  6. Sign the settlement agreement, or state in your papers that there are no issues to resolve.
  7. Submit the final papers. File the Affidavit of Plaintiff, Note of Issue ($30), RJI ($95), findings and proposed judgment, plus the child support forms if you have children.
  8. Wait for the judge to review. Most uncontested cases are decided without a court appearance. If papers are missing, the clerk sends them back for correction.
  9. Serve the Notice of Entry. Once the judgment is signed and entered, serve a copy with Notice of Entry on your spouse.

Property: Equitable Distribution

New York divides marital property by equitable distribution under DRL §236(B). Marital property is almost everything acquired by either spouse during the marriage, regardless of whose name is on it. Separate property includes what you owned before the marriage, inheritances, gifts from third parties, and compensation for personal injuries. Equitable means fair, not necessarily equal; the court weighs factors such as the length of the marriage, each spouse’s income and contributions, and the needs of a custodial parent.

The automatic orders served with the summons prevent both spouses from selling, hiding or borrowing against marital property, or changing insurance beneficiaries, while the case is pending, except in the usual course of business.

Maintenance (Alimony)

New York uses a statutory formula for temporary and post-divorce maintenance, applied to the payor’s income up to a cap that is adjusted every two years. Post-divorce maintenance lasts for a period tied to the length of the marriage under an advisory schedule. The Notice of Guideline Maintenance served with the summons explains the formula. Couples can agree to different terms if they explain why. Our alimony calculator by state shows how New York compares.

Divorce With Children in New York

  • Child support. The Child Support Standards Act sets support as a percentage of combined parental income: 17% for one child, 25% for two, 29% for three, 31% for four and at least 35% for five or more, divided between the parents in proportion to income (DRL §240(1-b)), up to an income cap. Parents may agree to a different amount only with a written explanation.
  • Custody and parenting time. The court decides based on the child’s best interests. A parenting plan in the settlement agreement is usually approved if it is reasonable; our parenting plan template covers the typical terms.
  • Health insurance and child care costs are addressed in every support order.

Common Mistakes in New York Divorces

  • Missing the required notices. The automatic orders, guideline maintenance and health coverage notices must be served with the summons. Leaving one out can mean re-serving.
  • Serving by mail or yourself. Personal delivery by a non-party adult is required for the summons.
  • Letting the 120 days run. If you do not serve within 120 days of filing, the case can be dismissed.
  • Unresolved issues. A no-fault judgment cannot be signed until property, support and custody are resolved.
  • Skipping the barriers statement. If you married in a religious ceremony, the sworn statement of removal of barriers to remarriage is required.

Online Divorce in New York

The court’s DIY Uncontested Divorce Program lets you prepare the forms online for free, and many counties accept e-filing through NYSCEF. Paid online services prepare the same forms; our guide to filing for an uncontested divorce online explains when that is worth it.

How New York Compares

New York’s one- or two-year residency rule is among the strictest in the country, but it has no waiting period after filing, unlike California (six months) or Texas (60 days). Its $335 in fees is set statewide, while Florida and Texas fees vary by county. See every state in our divorce requirements by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to live in New York to get divorced?

One year if you married in New York, lived there as a married couple, or the grounds arose there; otherwise two years (DRL §230). If the grounds arose in New York and both spouses live there when you file, there is no minimum period.

How long does a New York divorce take?

There is no statutory waiting period. Uncontested divorces often take several months because of court processing; contested cases usually take a year or more.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in New York?

$335 in court fees for an uncontested divorce: $210 for the index number, $95 for the Request for Judicial Intervention and $30 for the note of issue. You can apply for poor person relief under CPLR §1101 to waive them.

Can I get divorced in New York if my spouse does not agree?

Yes. Under DRL §170(7), only one spouse needs to swear that the marriage has been irretrievably broken for at least six months. The court must still resolve property, support and custody before granting the divorce.

Do I have to go to court for an uncontested divorce in New York?

Usually not. Most uncontested divorces are decided on the papers. The judge may ask for corrections or, rarely, a short appearance.

Can I serve the divorce papers myself in New York?

No. The summons must be personally delivered to your spouse by an adult who is not a party to the case, within 120 days after filing.
