The short version (2027): Here is how to file for divorce in Ohio in brief: the filing spouse must have lived in Ohio for 6 months, and the case is filed in a county where a spouse has lived for 90 days. Ohio has two ways to end a marriage. A dissolution is for spouses who agree on everything: they file jointly and attend a hearing 30 to 90 days after filing. A divorce is a lawsuit that cannot be heard until 42 days after service. In Franklin County, filing costs $225 for a dissolution and $275 for a divorce. Ohio divides marital property equally unless that would be inequitable.

Ohio is unusual in giving agreeing couples a completely separate procedure. In a dissolution of marriage, nobody sues anybody: both spouses sign one petition with a signed separation agreement attached, and a judge confirms it at a short hearing. A divorce, by contrast, is a lawsuit filed by one spouse against the other, even if they later settle. Choosing the right path is the first and most important decision in an Ohio case.

This guide explains how to file for divorce or dissolution in Ohio step by step: residency, grounds, the waiting periods, fees, the Supreme Court of Ohio’s uniform forms, property, spousal support and children. Rules link to the Ohio Revised Code and the Ohio Rules of Civil Procedure, checked in September 2026. For other states, see our divorce requirements by state table.

Ohio Residency Requirements

Under Ohio Revised Code §3105.03, the plaintiff (the filing spouse) in a divorce must have been an Ohio resident for at least six months immediately before filing. For a dissolution, at least one spouse must meet the six-month rule (§3105.62).

The case must also be filed in the proper county under Ohio Civil Rule 3, which generally means a county where the plaintiff has lived for at least 90 days before filing, or where the defendant lives. If you moved recently, you may need to wait or file where your spouse lives.

If you and your spouse already agree on every issue, dissolution is usually cheaper, faster and less adversarial. If you do not agree, or your spouse will not sign, you need a divorce.

Grounds for Divorce in Ohio

A dissolution requires no grounds. A divorce requires one of the grounds listed in §3105.01. The two no-fault grounds are:

  • Living separate and apart without cohabitation, without interruption, for one year (§3105.01(J)); and
  • Incompatibility, unless denied by either party (§3105.01(K)). If your spouse denies incompatibility, you need a different ground.

Ohio also keeps fault grounds, including willful absence for one year, adultery, extreme cruelty, fraudulent contract, gross neglect of duty, habitual drunkenness, and imprisonment. In practice, most uncontested divorces use incompatibility.

Waiting Periods and Timelines

  • Dissolution: both spouses must appear before the court not less than 30 nor more than 90 days after the petition is filed and confirm under oath that they signed the separation agreement voluntarily and are satisfied with it (§3105.64). Many courts schedule the hearing around six weeks after filing.
  • Divorce: under Ohio Civil Rule 75(K), no divorce may be heard and decided until 42 days after service of the complaint (or 28 days after the last publication). The defendant has 28 days to answer. Uncontested divorces often finish in two to four months; contested ones often take a year or more.

Each county’s clerk or domestic relations court sets its own deposit. The Franklin County Clerk of Courts publishes these basic filing fees:

Service of the divorce complaint (by certified mail through the clerk, or by the sheriff) is charged separately. Other counties charge different amounts; check your county’s domestic relations fee schedule.

Fee waiver. If you cannot afford the deposit, file a sworn affidavit of indigency (poverty affidavit) with the complaint or petition. The court decides whether you can file without paying the deposit.

Ohio Divorce and Dissolution Forms

The Supreme Court of Ohio publishes uniform domestic relations forms, and many county courts offer free packets for dissolution and uncontested divorce. The uniform affidavits are required in most cases:

  • Affidavit of Income and Expenses;
  • Affidavit of Property;
  • Health Insurance Affidavit (with children);
  • Parenting Proceeding Affidavit (with children); and
  • Petition for Dissolution with separation agreement, or a Complaint for Divorce with the county’s service instructions, plus the county’s decree form and a vital statistics form.

With children, you also need a shared parenting plan or a parenting allocation, and a child support computation worksheet.

How to File in Ohio, Step by Step

  1. Check residency: six months in Ohio for the filing spouse, and the right county under Civil Rule 3.
  2. Choose dissolution or divorce. Dissolution only if you already agree on everything.
  3. Complete the forms: the petition and separation agreement (dissolution), or the complaint (divorce), plus the uniform affidavits.
  4. File with the clerk of the Court of Common Pleas, Domestic Relations Division, and pay the deposit or file a poverty affidavit.
  5. Serve the other spouse (divorce only), usually by certified mail through the clerk.
  6. Complete a parenting class if you have children and your county requires it.
  7. Attend the hearing. In a dissolution, both spouses attend 30 to 90 days after filing. In an uncontested divorce, the plaintiff attends a short final hearing after the 42-day period, often with a witness to confirm residency in some counties.
  8. Get the decree and certified copies once the judge signs it.

Property: Equal Division Unless Inequitable

Ohio law (§3105.171) requires the court to divide marital property equally, unless an equal division would be inequitable, in which case the court divides it in the manner it determines is equitable. Each spouse keeps their separate property, such as property owned before the marriage, inheritances and gifts to one spouse. In a dissolution, the spouses decide the division themselves in the separation agreement, and the judge approves it.

Spousal Support

Ohio does not use a statewide formula for spousal support. Under §3105.18, the court considers factors such as the spouses’ incomes, earning abilities, ages, the length of the marriage, the standard of living, and contributions to each other’s education. The court may keep jurisdiction to modify support only if the decree or agreement says so. See our alimony calculator by state for comparisons.

Divorce or Dissolution With Children in Ohio

  • Parenting. Ohio allocates “parental rights and responsibilities.” Parents can file a shared parenting plan, or the court designates one parent as residential parent and legal custodian with parenting time for the other. Our parenting plan template covers typical schedules.
  • Child support. Ohio uses an income shares worksheet under Chapter 3119 of the Revised Code, and every order includes health insurance terms.
  • Parenting classes. Many Ohio counties require a parenting seminar before the final hearing.

Common Mistakes in Ohio Cases

  • Filing a dissolution without a finished agreement. A dissolution requires the signed separation agreement at filing. If you are still negotiating, file a divorce instead.
  • Missing the 90-day window. In a dissolution, the hearing must take place within 90 days of filing, and both spouses must attend.
  • Filing in the wrong county. The 90-day county rule under Civil Rule 3 is separate from the six-month state rule.
  • Relying on incompatibility when your spouse denies it. If your spouse denies incompatibility, you need another ground, such as one year apart.
  • Incomplete affidavits. Missing income, property or parenting affidavits are a common reason hearings are continued.

Online Filing and Remote Hearings in Ohio

Many Ohio domestic relations courts, including Franklin County, accept electronic filing, and several allow uncontested hearings by video. Ohio Legal Help publishes free interactive forms for dissolutions and uncontested divorces that walk you through the uniform affidavits. Paid online services prepare the same forms; see how to file for an uncontested divorce online for when that is worth it. Whichever route you choose, a restored former name can be included in the decree, which avoids a separate name change case in probate court.

How Ohio Compares

Ohio’s dissolution procedure is a rare option; most states handle agreed cases as uncontested divorces. Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to live in Ohio to file for divorce?

The filing spouse must have lived in Ohio for six months before filing (R.C. §3105.03), and the case is filed in a county where the plaintiff has lived for 90 days, or where the defendant lives.

What is the difference between a dissolution and a divorce in Ohio?

A dissolution is a joint petition by spouses who already agree on everything, with a hearing 30 to 90 days after filing. A divorce is a lawsuit filed by one spouse, which the court can decide even if the spouses do not agree.

How long does a divorce take in Ohio?

A dissolution usually takes about one to three months. A divorce cannot be heard until 42 days after service; uncontested divorces often take two to four months and contested ones a year or more.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in Ohio?

It depends on the county. Franklin County charges $225 for a dissolution and $275 for a divorce. You can file a poverty affidavit if you cannot afford the deposit.

Is Ohio a 50/50 divorce state?

Ohio divides marital property equally unless an equal division would be inequitable (R.C. §3105.171). Separate property stays with its owner.

Can I get a divorce in Ohio if my spouse won’t agree?

Yes, by filing a divorce complaint. If your spouse denies incompatibility, you can use another ground, such as living separate and apart for one year.
