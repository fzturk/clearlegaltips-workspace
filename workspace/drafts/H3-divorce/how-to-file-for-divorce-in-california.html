The short version (2027): To file for divorce in California, one spouse must have lived in the state for 6 months and in the county for 3 months. The filing fee is $435 for the petition and $435 for the response, or $870 for a joint petition that covers both spouses (statewide fee schedule effective January 1, 2026). No matter how quickly you agree, your marriage cannot end until six months after your spouse is served or appears. California is a community property state and divides the community estate equally.

California has one of the most structured divorce processes in the country. Every case uses the same statewide Judicial Council forms, every couple must exchange financial disclosures, and the six-month waiting period applies even to the friendliest divorce. The upside is predictability: once you know the forms and the order, an uncontested California divorce is very manageable without a lawyer, and the courts’ self-help centers are free.

This guide explains how to file for divorce in California step by step: residency, the waiting period, fees and fee waivers, the forms, financial disclosures, property and support, and the faster options for short marriages. California Residency Requirements: 6 Months and 3 Months

Under Family Code §2320, a judgment of dissolution may not be entered unless one of the spouses has been a resident of California for six months and of the county where the case is filed for three months immediately before the petition is filed.

If neither of you meets the rule yet, California offers a practical workaround: file for legal separation, which has no residency requirement, and then amend the petition to ask for a divorce once you qualify (§2321). The amended petition counts as the filing date for residency purposes, and the case you already started keeps moving.

Same-sex couples who married in California but now live somewhere that will not dissolve their marriage can file in the county where they married, even without living in California (§2320(b)).

The Six-Month Waiting Period

Family Code §2339 says no judgment of dissolution is final for the purpose of ending your marital status before six months have passed from the earlier of (1) the date the respondent was served with the petition and summons, or (2) the date the respondent appeared in the case. The court can sign the judgment earlier, but the date your marriage actually ends will be at least six months after service.

That makes service the key date. If you file on December 1 but do not serve your spouse until February, the six months start in February. In an uncontested case, the fastest route is for your spouse to sign a Notice and Acknowledgment of Receipt (FL-117) right after you file, or to file a response.

In practice, many uncontested California divorces take seven to nine months because the judgment paperwork must be reviewed and processed. Contested cases often take a year or more.

Grounds: Irreconcilable Differences

California is a pure no-fault state. A divorce may be granted for irreconcilable differences that have caused the irremediable breakdown of the marriage, or for a spouse’s permanent legal incapacity to make decisions (§2310). Fault, such as adultery, is not a ground and generally does not affect property division.

A few counties (such as San Francisco, Riverside and San Bernardino) add a small courthouse construction surcharge, so check your court’s own fee schedule. Service by a process server or sheriff costs extra, but an adult friend or relative who is not a party can serve your spouse for free. Our divorce cost calculator adds up the other costs.

Fee waiver. If you cannot afford the fees, file a Request to Waive Court Fees (FW-001). You qualify automatically if you receive benefits such as Medi-Cal, CalFresh, SSI or CalWORKs, or if your household income is below the limits on the form. A waiver covers the filing fee and many other court costs.

California Divorce Forms

California uses mandatory statewide Judicial Council forms, available free on the California Courts Self-Help Guide. The core set for a divorce is:

  • FL-100 Petition, Marriage/Domestic Partnership, and FL-110 Summons;
  • FL-105 Declaration Under the UCCJEA, if you have minor children;
  • FL-115 Proof of Service of Summons (or FL-117 Notice and Acknowledgment of Receipt);
  • FL-120 Response, if the other spouse files one;
  • FL-140 Declaration of Disclosure, with FL-142 Schedule of Assets and Debts (or FL-160) and FL-150 Income and Expense Declaration;
  • FL-141 Declaration Regarding Service of Declaration of Disclosure;
  • FL-170 Declaration for Default or Uncontested Dissolution, FL-180 Judgment, and FL-190 Notice of Entry of Judgment.

Many courts also require their own local cover sheets or checklists. The self-help center at your courthouse will review your forms for free before you file.

How to File for Divorce in California, Step by Step

  1. Check residency. Six months in California and three months in the county for either spouse, or start with legal separation.
  2. Fill out the petition and summons. FL-100 and FL-110, plus FL-105 if you have children.
  3. File with the superior court. File in person or through the court’s e-filing system and pay $435, or file an FW-001 fee waiver. Couples who agree can instead file a joint petition for $870, which covers both spouses.
  4. Serve your spouse. Someone 18 or older who is not a party serves the papers in person, or you mail them with an FL-117 that your spouse signs and returns. File the proof of service. The six-month clock starts now.
  5. Wait for the response. Your spouse has 30 days after service to file a response. If they do not respond, you can ask for a default judgment. If they sign a written agreement, the case can proceed as uncontested even without a response.
  6. Exchange financial disclosures. Under §2104, each spouse must serve a Preliminary Declaration of Disclosure (FL-140 with FL-142 and FL-150, plus tax returns from the last two years). The petitioner serves it within 60 days of filing. This is required even when you agree on everything.
  7. Write the settlement agreement. Divide all community property and debts, confirm separate property, and set any spousal support, custody and child support.
  8. Submit the judgment paperwork. File FL-170, FL-180, the signed agreement, and any child support forms. Most uncontested cases are decided on the papers without a hearing.
  9. Wait for the judgment. The judge signs; your marital status ends on the date stated in the judgment, at least six months after service.

Summary Dissolution: The Short-Marriage Option

Couples in short, simple marriages may qualify for summary dissolution under Family Code §2400, a joint process with fewer forms and no court hearing. Both spouses must meet all the conditions, including:

  • married for five years or less as of the date of separation;
  • no children together (born or adopted before or during the marriage) and neither spouse pregnant;
  • no ownership interest in real estate (a residential lease of a year or less is allowed);
  • debts incurred since the marriage (not counting car loans) and community property (not counting cars) both below dollar limits that are adjusted for inflation every odd-numbered year (the current figures are in the court’s summary dissolution booklet, FL-810);
  • both spouses waive spousal support, sign an agreement dividing their property, give up the right to appeal, and have read the summary dissolution booklet.

The spouses file a Joint Petition for Summary Dissolution (FL-800). Either spouse can revoke it during the six-month waiting period; if nobody does, the court can enter judgment after six months.

Property: Community Property Split Equally

California is a community property state. Property and debts acquired by either spouse during the marriage are community property, and unless the spouses agree otherwise in writing or in court, the court must divide the community estate equally (§2550). Separate property (owned before marriage, or received by gift or inheritance, or earned after separation) stays with its owner.

Equal does not mean each item is split. Spouses usually trade assets: one keeps the house, the other keeps a retirement account of similar value. Retirement plans governed by federal law need a separate order, usually a QDRO.

Spousal Support

A judge can order temporary support while the case is pending, often using a local guideline formula, and long-term support at the end of the case based on the factors in §4320. For marriages of under ten years, support usually lasts about half the length of the marriage; for marriages of ten years or more (a “long duration” marriage), the court keeps jurisdiction indefinitely (§4336). Spouses can agree to different terms or waive support. See our alimony calculator by state for how California compares.

Divorce With Children in California

  • Custody and visitation. Parents can agree on a parenting plan; if they disagree, they must attend child custody mediation (called child custody recommending counseling in some counties) before a judge decides (§3170). Our parenting plan template helps you draft the schedule.
  • Child support. California uses a statewide guideline formula based on both parents’ incomes and time with the children (§4055). The judge will check any agreement against the guideline.
  • UCCJEA declaration. Form FL-105 lists where the children have lived for the last five years, which decides whether California can make custody orders.

Common Mistakes in California Divorces

  • Waiting to serve. The six months run from service or appearance, not from filing. Serve right after you file.
  • Skipping the disclosures. A judgment without the required declarations of disclosure can be rejected, and incomplete disclosure can later be grounds to set the judgment aside.
  • Serving the papers yourself. A party cannot serve the other spouse; use an adult who is not a party, a sheriff or a process server.
  • Forgetting the judgment. Some people file the petition and never submit a judgment. The case can be dismissed, and you are still married.
  • Assuming summary dissolution fits. One disqualifying fact, such as owning a condo or a child from the marriage, means you need a regular dissolution.

How California Compares

California’s six-month wait is the longest mandatory waiting period in the country for couples without a required separation. Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to live in California to file for divorce?

One spouse must have lived in California for six months and in the county for three months before filing (Fam. Code §2320). If you have not met that yet, you can file for legal separation and amend to a divorce once you qualify.

How long does a California divorce take?

At least six months from the date your spouse is served or appears in the case (§2339). Most uncontested divorces take seven to nine months; contested cases can take a year or more.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in California?

$435 to file the petition and $435 for the other spouse to respond, or $870 for a joint petition covering both spouses, under the statewide fee schedule effective January 1, 2026. You can ask for a fee waiver with form FW-001.

Do I need my spouse’s permission to divorce in California?

No. California is a no-fault state. If your spouse is served and does not respond within 30 days, you can ask for a default judgment.

What is summary dissolution in California?

A simpler joint process for couples married five years or less (as of separation), with no children, no real estate, limited property and debts, and no spousal support. Both spouses file form FL-800 together.

Is property split 50/50 in a California divorce?

Yes, community property is divided equally unless the spouses agree otherwise (§2550). Separate property stays with the spouse who owns it.
