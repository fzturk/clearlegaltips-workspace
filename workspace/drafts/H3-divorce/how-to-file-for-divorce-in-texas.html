The short version (2027): To file for divorce in Texas, one spouse must have lived in Texas for 6 months and in the county for 90 days. The court cannot grant the divorce until 60 days after filing. Filing costs $350 in Harris County, or $365 with children (2026 schedule), and you can ask to waive fees with a Statement of Inability. Texas uses a no-fault ground called insupportability, divides community property in a “just and right” way, and does not allow either spouse to marry someone else for 30 days after the decree.

Texas is one of the easier states to divorce in when both spouses agree. The waiting period is only 60 days, the state publishes free divorce forms through TexasLawHelp.org, and your spouse can sign a waiver instead of being served by a constable. But Texas also has rules that trip people up: a county residency requirement, a waiver of service that cannot be signed too early, and a 30-day ban on remarriage that surprises many couples.

This guide covers how to file for divorce in Texas step by step: who can file and where, the costs, the forms, what happens with children and property, and how long it really takes. Texas Residency Requirements: 6 Months and 90 Days

Under Texas Family Code §6.301, a divorce suit can be maintained only if, when the petition is filed, either spouse has been:

  • a domiciliary of Texas for the preceding six-month period; and
  • a resident of the county where the suit is filed for the preceding 90-day period.

Only one spouse has to meet the requirements. If you moved out and now live in another county, you can file in the county where your spouse lives if your spouse has been there 90 days, or wait until you have 90 days in your new county.

Two special rules help people with unusual situations. If your spouse has lived in Texas for at least six months, you can file in the Texas county where your spouse lives even if you now live in another state or country (§6.302). And members of the armed forces, and their spouses, who have been stationed in Texas for the required time are treated as Texas residents for divorce (§6.304). Time spent outside Texas on military or other public service can also count if Texas was your home before you left (§6.303).

The 60-Day Waiting Period

Texas Family Code §6.702 says the court may not grant a divorce before the 60th day after the petition was filed. The clock runs from filing, not from service, so an uncontested divorce filed on December 1 can be granted, at the earliest, around the end of January.

The only exception is family violence. The 60-day wait does not apply if the other spouse has been convicted of, or received deferred adjudication for, an offense involving family violence against you or a member of your household, or if you have an active protective order against your spouse because of family violence during the marriage.

In practice, most uncontested Texas divorces are final in about two to four months, depending on how quickly the court schedules the final hearing (often called the “prove-up”). A contested divorce with children or property disputes commonly takes six months to a year or longer.

Grounds: Insupportability and the Fault Options

Texas is a no-fault state. The court may grant a divorce without regard to fault if the marriage “has become insupportable because of discord or conflict of personalities that destroys the legitimate ends of the marital relationship and prevents any reasonable expectation of reconciliation” (§6.001). Almost every uncontested Texas divorce uses this ground.

Texas also keeps fault grounds: cruelty, adultery, conviction of a felony, abandonment for one year, living apart for three years, and confinement in a mental hospital (§§6.002–6.007). Fault is rarely worth pleading in an uncontested case, but in a contested case it can matter because the judge may consider it when dividing property.

What It Costs to File for Divorce in Texas

Texas district clerks set their fees under state law, and most large counties charge about the same. Harris County, the state’s largest, published this schedule effective January 1, 2026:

If your spouse signs a Waiver of Service, you skip the citation and service fees entirely. Constable or private process server fees apply if your spouse will not sign. Other counties are similar but not identical; check the district clerk’s fee schedule before you file.

Fee waiver. If you cannot afford the fees, file a Statement of Inability to Afford Payment of Court Costs under Texas Rule of Civil Procedure 145. You generally qualify if you receive need-based government benefits (such as SNAP or Medicaid), are represented by a legal aid provider, or your income is too low to pay the costs; the court can ask for proof. The form is free on TexasLawHelp.org.

Texas Divorce Forms

The Texas Supreme Court approved free divorce forms for people without lawyers, available through TexasLawHelp.org. There are separate sets for divorces with and without children. A typical uncontested case uses:

  • Original Petition for Divorce, which starts the case;
  • Waiver of Service, signed by the other spouse, or a citation for formal service;
  • Final Decree of Divorce, which the judge signs at the end;
  • Certificate of Last Known Mailing Address;
  • Information on Suit Affecting the Family Relationship (if you have children) and the Bureau of Vital Statistics divorce record form; and
  • for cases with children, child support and medical support terms and an Employer’s Order to Withhold, plus a possession schedule.

Many counties also have local rules, such as a required parenting class or a specific way to set the final hearing. Look at the district clerk’s family law page for your county.

How to File for Divorce in Texas, Step by Step

  1. Check residency. Six months in Texas and 90 days in the county, for either spouse.
  2. Agree on the terms. List all property and debts and decide who gets what. With children, agree on conservatorship, the possession schedule and child support.
  3. Fill out the petition. Use the TexasLawHelp.org form for your situation (with or without children).
  4. File with the district clerk. Most Texas counties require or strongly prefer e-filing through eFileTexas.gov. Pay the fee or file a Statement of Inability.
  5. Handle service. Your spouse can sign a Waiver of Service, but only after the petition is filed; under §6.4035 a waiver signed before filing is not valid. The waiver must be sworn before a notary public who is not an attorney in the case; an unsworn declaration is not enough unless the spouse is incarcerated. If your spouse will not sign, request a citation and have it served by a constable, sheriff or certified process server.
  6. Wait for the answer deadline. A spouse who was served has until 10:00 a.m. on the Monday after 20 days from service to file an answer (TRCP 99). If they do not answer, you may be able to proceed by default.
  7. Prepare the Final Decree. The decree must describe how every piece of property and debt is divided and, with children, all conservatorship, possession and support terms.
  8. Wait at least 60 days from filing.
  9. Attend the prove-up hearing. The filing spouse gives brief testimony about residency, the grounds and the agreement. Many courts allow this by video, and some let uncontested cases be finished on the papers.
  10. Get certified copies. Once the judge signs the decree, get certified copies for name changes, retirement accounts and title transfers.

Property: Community Property, Divided “Just and Right”

Texas is a community property state. Property either spouse acquired during the marriage is presumed to be community property (§3.003), and the court divides the community estate in a manner it considers “just and right” (§7.001). That is not always a 50/50 split: judges can consider fault, earning capacity, health and other factors.

Separate property, meaning what you owned before the marriage or received by gift or inheritance, stays yours, but you must prove it by clear and convincing evidence. Keep account statements and title documents that trace separate property.

Retirement accounts usually need a Qualified Domestic Relations Order (QDRO) in addition to the decree. If you have a 401(k) or pension to divide, plan for it before the final hearing.

Spousal Maintenance in Texas

Texas is strict about post-divorce support. Court-ordered spousal maintenance under Chapter 8 of the Family Code is available only in limited situations, such as a marriage of ten years or more where the requesting spouse cannot meet minimum reasonable needs, or cases involving family violence or disability. The amount is capped at the lesser of $5,000 per month or 20% of the paying spouse’s average monthly gross income (§8.055), and the duration is limited by the length of the marriage.

Spouses can agree to more generous “contractual alimony” in their settlement. Our alimony calculator by state shows how Texas compares.

Divorce With Children in Texas

Texas uses its own vocabulary for custody: parents are named conservators. The law presumes that appointing both parents as joint managing conservators is in the child’s best interest (§153.131), although one parent usually has the right to decide the child’s primary residence.

  • Possession schedule. Most decrees use the Standard Possession Order, which sets weekends, holidays and summer time for the other parent. Parents can agree to a different schedule; our parenting plan template helps you work one out.
  • Child support. Guideline support is a percentage of the paying parent’s net resources: 20% for one child, 25% for two, 30% for three, 35% for four and 40% for five (§154.125), up to a statutory cap on net resources.
  • Medical and dental support must be addressed in every order.

After the Decree: The 30-Day Remarriage Rule

Under §6.801, neither spouse may marry a third person before the 31st day after the divorce is decreed, unless the court waives the waiting period for good cause (§6.802). Former spouses may remarry each other at any time.

Other tasks after the decree: change your name if the decree restores a former name, update your beneficiary designations, transfer vehicle titles and deeds, and send the QDRO to the retirement plan administrator.

Common Mistakes in Texas Divorces

  • Filing in the wrong county. The 90-day county rule is separate from the state rule. A move three months ago may mean you file where your spouse lives.
  • A waiver signed too early. A Waiver of Service signed before the petition was filed is invalid, which can force you to re-serve and delay the final hearing.
  • A vague decree. A decree that says “the parties have divided their property” without listing accounts and vehicles causes trouble with banks and the DMV later.
  • Forgetting the retirement order. Without a QDRO, the plan cannot pay the other spouse’s share.
  • Assuming a hearing is optional. Some courts require you to appear for the prove-up even when everything is agreed. Check local practice.

Online Divorce in Texas

You can complete the whole process online in most Texas counties: TexasLawHelp.org has free interview-style forms, and eFileTexas.gov handles filing. Paid online divorce services prepare the same forms for a fee and can be worth it if you want help with a complicated property list. How Texas Compares

Texas’s 60-day wait is shorter than California’s six months and longer than Florida’s 20 days, and its six-month residency rule is the national norm. The county residency rule and the 30-day remarriage ban are less common. See all 50 states in our divorce requirements by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to live in Texas to file for divorce?

Either spouse must have lived in Texas for six months and in the county where you file for 90 days before the petition is filed (Tex. Fam. Code §6.301).

How long does a divorce take in Texas?

At least 60 days from filing, the minimum set by §6.702. Most uncontested divorces are final in two to four months; contested cases often take six months to a year or more.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in Texas?

In Harris County the filing fee is $350 without children and $365 with children (2026 schedule). Service costs extra unless your spouse signs a Waiver of Service. You can file a Statement of Inability if you cannot afford the fees.

Do both spouses have to go to court for a Texas divorce?

Usually only the filing spouse attends the short prove-up hearing in an uncontested case, and many courts allow it by video. The other spouse can sign the decree and a Waiver of Service instead of appearing.

Can I get divorced in Texas if my spouse won’t sign?

Yes. Texas is a no-fault state, so your spouse’s agreement is not required. Have your spouse formally served; if they do not answer, you may be able to finish the divorce by default after the 60-day wait.

How soon can I remarry after a Texas divorce?

You must wait 30 days after the divorce is granted before marrying someone else, unless the court waives the wait (§6.801). You may remarry your former spouse at any time.
