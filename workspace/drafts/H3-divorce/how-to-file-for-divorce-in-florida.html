A final judgment can be entered 20 days after filing, one of the shortest waits in the country. Filing costs about $400 in most counties ($408 in Collier County, plus $10 per summons). Florida can be one of the fastest states in the country for an agreed divorce. There is no county residency requirement, the statutory wait is only 20 days, and couples without children can use a simplified process where both spouses sign one petition and attend one short hearing. But speed depends on paperwork: Florida requires financial affidavits and mandatory disclosure in almost every case, and parents must complete a parenting course before the judge can sign.

Florida Residency Requirement: 6 Months

Under Florida Statutes §61.021, to obtain a dissolution of marriage one of the parties must have resided in Florida for six months before filing the petition. There is no separate county requirement; you file in the county where either spouse lives or where you last lived together as a married couple.

Residency must be proven, not just stated. Under §61.052(2), it can be corroborated by a valid Florida driver license, a Florida voter registration card, a Florida ID card, or the testimony or affidavit of a third party. Many filers use the Florida Supreme Court’s Affidavit of Corroborating Witness (Form 12.902(i)), signed by someone who knows you have lived in Florida for six months. Bring the document or witness affidavit to the final hearing.

The 20-Day Waiting Period

Florida Statutes §61.19 says no final judgment of dissolution may be entered until at least 20 days after the original petition was filed. The court can shorten the wait if the delay would cause injustice, but that is rare.

In practice, the 20 days is almost never the limiting factor. The real timeline depends on how fast your spouse is served, whether disclosure is complete, and when the court can schedule the final hearing. An uncontested Florida divorce often takes about one to three months; a contested case often takes a year or longer.

Grounds: Irretrievably Broken

Florida is a no-fault state. Under §61.052(1), a dissolution may be granted only if (a) the marriage is irretrievably broken, or (b) one spouse has been legally adjudged mentally incapacitated for at least three years. Almost all Florida divorces use the first ground. Adultery is not a ground, but the court may consider it, and its economic impact, when deciding alimony (§61.08).

If there are minor children, or if the other spouse denies that the marriage is irretrievably broken, the court can order counseling or delay the case up to three months to give the spouses a chance to reconcile. In practice this is uncommon when both spouses want the divorce.

What It Costs to File for Divorce in Florida

Florida’s circuit court clerks charge a statutory filing fee for family cases (up to $295 under §28.241, plus additional state surcharges), so the total is similar in every county. Collier County publishes this schedule:

Service by the sheriff or a certified process server is a separate charge paid to them. If your spouse will sign an answer and waiver, or if you file a simplified dissolution together, you may not need a summons at all.

Fee waiver. If you cannot afford the fees, file an Application for Determination of Civil Indigent Status with the clerk. The clerk decides based on your income, and if you are approved the filing fee is deferred or waived.

Simplified Dissolution vs. Regular Dissolution

Florida offers two routes. Simplified dissolution (Florida Family Law Rule of Procedure 12.105, Form 12.901(a)) is designed for couples who agree on everything. You can use it only if:

  • you have no minor or dependent children together, and the wife is not pregnant;
  • at least one spouse meets the six-month residency rule;
  • you both agree the marriage is irretrievably broken;
  • you have agreed on how to divide all property and debts;
  • neither spouse is asking for alimony; and
  • you are both willing to sign the petition together and attend the final hearing, giving up the right to trial and appeal.

If any condition is missing, including if either of you wants alimony or you have minor children, you file a regular dissolution. A regular dissolution can still be uncontested: one spouse files, the other signs an answer and a marital settlement agreement, and the case goes to a short final hearing.

Florida Divorce Forms

The Florida Supreme Court approves standard family law forms, available free on the Florida Courts website and at clerk self-help centers. A typical case uses:

  • Form 12.901(a) Petition for Simplified Dissolution, or 12.901(b)(1) (with children) or 12.901(b)(2) (with property but no children) or 12.901(b)(3) (no children and no property) for a regular dissolution;
  • Form 12.902(b) or 12.902(c) Family Law Financial Affidavit (short or long form, depending on income);
  • Form 12.932 Certificate of Compliance with Mandatory Disclosure;
  • Form 12.902(f)(1)–(3) Marital Settlement Agreement;
  • Form 12.902(j) Notice of Social Security Number and, with children, 12.902(d) UCCJEA Affidavit, 12.902(e) Child Support Guidelines Worksheet and a 12.995(a) Parenting Plan;
  • Form 12.902(i) Affidavit of Corroborating Witness (residency);
  • a Final Judgment of Dissolution of Marriage form that matches your case type.

How to File for Divorce in Florida, Step by Step

  1. Confirm residency and choose the route. Six months in Florida for either spouse. Decide between simplified and regular dissolution.
  2. Complete the petition and financial affidavit. Each spouse completes a financial affidavit, even in a simplified case.
  3. File with the circuit court clerk. File in the county where either spouse lives, in person or through the Florida Courts E-Filing Portal, and pay the fee or apply for indigent status.
  4. Serve or get an answer. In a regular case, have your spouse served by the sheriff or a certified process server, or have them sign and file an Answer. The respondent has 20 days after service to respond.
  5. Complete mandatory disclosure. Under Rule 12.285, each spouse exchanges the financial affidavit and supporting documents (tax returns, pay stubs, account statements) within 45 days of service and files a certificate of compliance.
  6. Sign the marital settlement agreement and, with children, the parenting plan and child support worksheet.
  7. Finish the parenting course if you have minor children (§61.21).
  8. Wait at least 20 days from filing and request a final hearing.
  9. Attend the final hearing. The judge confirms residency, asks whether the marriage is irretrievably broken, reviews the agreement and signs the final judgment.

Property: Equitable Distribution Starting at 50/50

Florida is an equitable distribution state. Under §61.075, the court first sets aside each spouse’s nonmarital assets and debts (generally what was owned before the marriage or received by gift or inheritance), then divides the marital assets and debts, beginning with the premise that the distribution should be equal. The court can depart from 50/50 only with a justification, such as one spouse’s contributions, the length of the marriage, or intentional waste of assets.

A marital home, retirement accounts and business interests are the assets that most often need careful handling. Retirement plans usually need a separate QDRO to divide.

Alimony After the 2023 Reform

Florida overhauled alimony in 2023. Section 61.08 now allows only temporary, bridge-the-gap, rehabilitative and durational alimony; permanent alimony was eliminated effective July 1, 2023 (Senate Bill 1416). The court must first find that one spouse needs support and the other can pay it.

Durational alimony is capped as a share of the length of the marriage, measured from the wedding to the filing date: up to 50% of a short-term marriage (under 10 years), 60% of a moderate-term marriage (10 to 20 years), and 75% of a long-term marriage (20 years or more), unless there are exceptional circumstances. Divorce With Children in Florida

  • Parenting plan. Florida uses “time-sharing” and “parental responsibility” instead of custody. Every case with children needs a parenting plan approved by the court. Our parenting plan template covers the typical terms.
  • Parenting course. Both parents must complete the Parent Education and Family Stabilization Course before the court enters a final judgment (§61.21(4)). Courses are offered online by approved providers.
  • Child support. Support is calculated with the statutory guidelines in §61.30 and the Child Support Guidelines Worksheet.
  • No simplified option. Couples with minor children must use a regular dissolution, even if they agree on everything.

Common Mistakes in Florida Divorces

  • No proof of residency at the hearing. Bring your Florida driver license or ID, or a signed corroborating witness affidavit. Without it, the judge cannot grant the divorce.
  • Choosing simplified dissolution when you do not qualify. If either spouse wants alimony, or you have minor children, you need a regular petition.
  • Skipping mandatory disclosure. Missing financial affidavits or a certificate of compliance is a common reason hearings are reset.
  • Forgetting the parenting course. The judge cannot sign the final judgment until both parents finish it or are excused.
  • Vague settlement terms. Name each account, vehicle and debt, and say who pays and by when.

Online Divorce in Florida

Florida’s E-Filing Portal accepts family filings in every county, and the Florida Courts website has free forms and instructions. Many courts hold uncontested final hearings by video. Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to live in Florida to file for divorce?

One spouse must have lived in Florida for six months before filing (Fla. Stat. §61.021). You prove it with a Florida driver license, ID card or voter registration card, or a third party’s affidavit or testimony.

How long does a divorce take in Florida?

At least 20 days from filing (§61.19). An uncontested divorce often takes one to three months, depending on service, disclosure and the court’s calendar. Contested cases often take a year or more.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in Florida?

About $400 in most counties. Collier County charges $408 for the petition plus $10 per summons. You can apply to the clerk for civil indigent status if you cannot afford the fee.

What is a simplified dissolution of marriage in Florida?

A faster process for couples with no minor children who agree on dividing property and debts and neither of whom wants alimony. Both spouses sign the petition and attend the final hearing together.

Does Florida still have permanent alimony?

No. Since July 1, 2023, Florida allows only temporary, bridge-the-gap, rehabilitative and durational alimony, and durational alimony is capped as a percentage of the length of the marriage.

Do both spouses have to go to court for a Florida divorce?

In a simplified dissolution, yes. In a regular uncontested case, usually only the filing spouse attends the short final hearing, and many courts allow it by video.
