The short version (2027): Here is how to file for divorce in North Carolina in brief: you and your spouse must have lived separate and apart for one year before you file, and one of you must have lived in North Carolina for 6 months. The filing fee is $225 statewide ($150 civil filing fee plus a $75 absolute divorce fee). Your spouse has 30 days to answer after service. Most important: claims for property division (equitable distribution) and alimony must be filed before the divorce judgment, or they are generally lost.

North Carolina’s divorce process is simple on paper and strict in practice. There is only one no-fault ground, one year of separation, and the divorce itself is usually a short, inexpensive court proceeding. But the absolute divorce deals only with ending the marriage. Property, alimony, custody and child support are separate claims, and two of them disappear if you do not file them before the divorce is granted.

This guide explains how to file for divorce in North Carolina step by step: the separation requirement, residency, fees, forms, service, the final hearing and the claims you must protect. Rules link to Chapter 50 of the North Carolina General Statutes, checked in September 2026. For other states, see our divorce requirements by state table.

The One-Year Separation Requirement

Under G.S. §50-6, a marriage may be dissolved on the application of either spouse if the spouses have lived separate and apart for one year and one of them has lived in North Carolina for six months. The year must be complete before you file the complaint.

What counts as separation:

  • Living in separate residences. Unlike some states, North Carolina generally requires that you actually live under different roofs; living apart in the same house usually does not count.
  • Intent. At least one spouse must intend the separation to be permanent when it begins.
  • No court filing needed. You do not need a separation agreement or a court order to start the clock. A written separation agreement is common, though, because it settles property and support.
  • Resuming the marriage resets it. If you resume marital relations, meaning a voluntary renewal of the relationship judged by the totality of the circumstances, the year starts over. Isolated incidents of sexual intercourse do not count as resumption (G.S. §52-10.2).

Write down the date of separation and keep proof, such as a lease, a utility bill or a change-of-address confirmation. You will state it under oath in the complaint.

Residency and Where to File

Either spouse must have lived in North Carolina for at least six months before the complaint is filed (§50-6). You file in the District Court of the county where either spouse lives (§50-3). If both spouses have moved out of state, you generally cannot file in North Carolina even if you married there.

What It Costs to File for Divorce in North Carolina

North Carolina’s court costs are set by statute and are the same in every county. Under G.S. §7A-305, an absolute divorce in district court costs:

Total$225

Service by the sheriff carries a separate fee, and adding claims such as equitable distribution or custody can add costs. Certified mail service costs only postage.

Fee waiver. If you cannot afford the costs, file a Petition to Proceed as an Indigent. You may qualify automatically if you receive certain public benefits or have legal aid representation; otherwise the clerk or judge decides.

Protect Your Claims Before the Divorce

This is the rule that catches people in North Carolina. Once an absolute divorce judgment is entered, rights arising from the marriage end unless they were already asserted or resolved (G.S. §50-11):

  • Equitable distribution. The right to ask the court to divide marital property is destroyed by the divorce unless it was asserted before the judgment (§50-11(e)).
  • Alimony. A claim for alimony (and postseparation support) must be pending, or already resolved in an order or agreement, before the divorce is granted.
  • Child custody and support are different: they can be filed before or after the divorce because they concern the children.

If you and your spouse have a signed separation and property settlement agreement that covers property and support, you may not need to file these claims at all. If you do not, talk to a lawyer about filing equitable distribution and alimony claims before the divorce hearing.

North Carolina Divorce Forms

The North Carolina Judicial Branch offers free self-help divorce forms and an online form tool for people without lawyers. A typical absolute divorce uses:

  • Complaint for Absolute Divorce, verified (sworn) as required by G.S. §50-8, stating the dates of marriage and separation, residency, and whether there are minor children;
  • Domestic Civil Action Cover Sheet and Civil Summons;
  • proof of service (sheriff’s return, certified mail affidavit, or an Acceptance of Service signed by your spouse);
  • a military status affidavit (Servicemembers Civil Relief Act) if your spouse does not answer;
  • a notice of hearing or motion for summary judgment, depending on your county’s practice; and
  • the proposed Judgment of Absolute Divorce.

How to File for Divorce in North Carolina, Step by Step

  1. Complete one year of separation and confirm six months of residency for either spouse.
  2. Decide how to handle property and support: a signed agreement, or claims filed before the divorce.
  3. Prepare the complaint and sign it before a notary.
  4. File with the Clerk of Superior Court in the county where either spouse lives and pay $225 (or file a petition to proceed as an indigent).
  5. Serve your spouse by sheriff, certified mail (restricted delivery, return receipt) or signed acceptance of service.
  6. Wait 30 days for your spouse to answer. They can also sign a waiver or simply not respond.
  7. Schedule the hearing or file a motion for summary judgment, following local practice, after the answer period ends.
  8. Attend the brief hearing (often a few minutes) where you confirm the facts under oath, or receive the judgment on the papers.
  9. Get a certified copy of the Judgment of Absolute Divorce.

Property: Equitable Distribution

North Carolina presumes an equal division of the net value of marital and divisible property, unless the court determines that an equal division is not equitable (G.S. §50-20(c)). Marital property is generally what either spouse acquired from the wedding until the date of separation, and it is valued as of the date of separation. Separate property includes property owned before the marriage and gifts or inheritances to one spouse.

Alimony, Postseparation Support and Marital Misconduct

North Carolina has postseparation support (temporary support during the case) and alimony. Marital misconduct matters: under G.S. §50-16.3A, if the dependent spouse committed illicit sexual behavior during the marriage and before separation, the court shall not award alimony; if the supporting spouse did, the court shall order alimony; if both did, it is up to the court. There is no statutory formula for amount or duration. See our alimony calculator by state for comparisons.

Divorce With Children in North Carolina

  • Custody mediation. Where there is a contested custody or visitation issue, it must be set for custody mediation before a hearing unless the court waives it (G.S. §50-13.1). Our parenting plan template helps if you reach agreement.
  • Child support. North Carolina uses statewide child support guidelines based on both parents’ incomes.
  • Separate claims. Custody and support are filed as separate claims (or in the same case) and can be decided before or after the divorce.

Divorce From Bed and Board

North Carolina also has a divorce from bed and board (G.S. §50-7), a court-ordered legal separation based on fault such as abandonment, cruel treatment or adultery. It does not end the marriage and is rarely needed, because spouses can separate without any court order. See our guide to legal separation for how it compares elsewhere.

Common Mistakes in North Carolina Divorces

  • Filing before the year is up. A complaint filed even one day early can be dismissed.
  • Losing property or alimony claims. Assert equitable distribution and alimony before the judgment, or have a signed agreement covering them.
  • Separating under the same roof. North Carolina generally requires separate residences.
  • Resuming the relationship. A genuine reconciliation restarts the one-year clock.
  • Skipping the military affidavit when the other spouse does not respond.

How North Carolina Compares

North Carolina is one of only a few states, along with South Carolina and Virginia, that require the separation period to be finished before filing. Once you file, though, it can be faster than Georgia or Pennsylvania, and its $225 fee is set statewide. See all 50 states in our divorce requirements by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do you have to be separated to get divorced in North Carolina?

One full year of living separate and apart, completed before you file the complaint (G.S. §50-6). At least one spouse must intend the separation to be permanent.

How long do you have to live in North Carolina to file for divorce?

Either spouse must have lived in North Carolina for six months before filing. You file in the county where either spouse lives.

How much does it cost to file for divorce in North Carolina?

$225 statewide: a $150 civil filing fee plus a $75 absolute divorce fee (G.S. §7A-305). Service by the sheriff costs extra. You can petition to proceed as an indigent if you cannot afford it.

What happens to property if I get divorced without filing for equitable distribution?

The right to have the court divide marital property is generally lost once the absolute divorce is granted, unless it was asserted before the judgment or resolved in a written agreement (G.S. §50-11(e)).

Can we live in the same house and still be separated in North Carolina?

Generally no. North Carolina courts require spouses to live in separate residences for the separation period to count.

Does adultery affect alimony in North Carolina?

Yes. Illicit sexual behavior by the dependent spouse before separation bars alimony, and the same behavior by the supporting spouse requires the court to award alimony (G.S. §50-16.3A).
