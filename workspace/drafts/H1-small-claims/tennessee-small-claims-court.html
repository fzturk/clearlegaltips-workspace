Tennessee does not have a separate “small claims court.” Instead, each county’s General Sessions Court handles civil disputes up to $25,000 using simple procedures: a short civil warrant, no formal written answer in most cases, and a trial on the date set in the warrant. General Sessions courts are busy, so hearings are short and preparation matters.

This guide covers the Tennessee General Sessions limit, fees, service, the trial, appeals and collection. Rules link to the Tennessee Code and official county clerk pages, checked in September 2026.

The Tennessee Limit: $25,000

Under Tenn. Code §16-15-501, General Sessions courts have jurisdiction up to $25,000 in all civil cases, both law and equity, in every county. Two kinds of cases have no dollar limit:

  • Forcible entry and detainer (evictions), where the court has unlimited original jurisdiction; and
  • Actions to recover personal property, including the power to award an alternative money judgment.

Attorney fees, court costs and discretionary costs do not count when deciding whether a judgment exceeds the $25,000 limit. Some older sources say that a few large counties have a lower $15,000 limit; the current statute sets $25,000 in all civil cases.

If your claim is over $25,000, you can reduce it and give up the difference, or file in circuit or chancery court.

How to File in Tennessee General Sessions Court, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Tennessee generally allows six years for contract claims; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Find the right county. Usually where the defendant lives or where the claim arose.
  4. Complete the civil warrant. Describe the claim briefly and state the amount. Use the defendant’s full legal name; for a business, check the Tennessee Secretary of State’s business search for its exact name and registered agent.
  5. File and pay. Pay the clerk’s fee, litigation tax and service fee for each defendant.
  6. Service. The sheriff (or a private process server, where allowed) serves the warrant, which states the trial date. Out-of-state defendants and some businesses can be served through the Secretary of State.
  7. Prepare for trial. Organize contracts, invoices, receipts, photos and messages, with copies for the judge and the other side.
  8. Go to court on the trial date. Be on time; many courts call the docket at the start of the session.

At Trial

General Sessions trials are before a judge, without a jury. In most civil warrant cases, the defendant does not have to file a written answer; they simply appear on the trial date and present their side. Tips:

  • Arrive early and listen for your case when the docket is called.
  • Lead with the amount and the reason in one or two sentences.
  • Bring documents and witnesses; the judge decides quickly.
  • If the defendant does not appear, ask for a default judgment and be ready to prove the amount.

Lawyers are allowed, and many businesses and landlords use them in General Sessions, but individuals often represent themselves.

Appeals: A New Trial in Circuit Court

Under Tenn. Code §27-5-108, either party can appeal a General Sessions civil judgment to the circuit court of the county within 10 days after the judgment. The appeal is heard de novo: the circuit court tries the case again from the beginning, and a jury may be available there. Ask the clerk about the costs or bond required to perfect the appeal.

Because the appeal is a full retrial, a losing defendant may appeal simply to delay. If you win in General Sessions, be ready to present your case again.

Collecting a Tennessee Judgment

If the defendant does not pay after the 10-day appeal period, you can use:

  • Wage garnishment. Tennessee follows the federal limit for ordinary debts: no more than 25% of disposable earnings, or the amount above 30 times the federal minimum wage if less, with an additional exemption for debtors supporting dependent children.
  • Bank garnishment and executions. Reach non-exempt bank funds and property.
  • Judgment lien. Recording a certified copy of the judgment with the county register of deeds creates a lien on the debtor’s real estate in that county.

Interest. Tennessee judgment interest changes every six months. Under Tenn. Code §47-14-121, the rate is two percentage points below the formula rate published by the Commissioner of Financial Institutions, and the Administrative Office of the Courts posts the current figure.

General Sessions or Circuit Court?

For claims of $25,000 or less, most people choose General Sessions because it is faster and cheaper than circuit or chancery court and does not require formal pleadings. Circuit court makes more sense if your claim is larger than $25,000, if you want a jury from the start, or if the case needs extensive discovery such as depositions. Keep in mind that a General Sessions judgment can be appealed for a full retrial in circuit court, so a well-funded opponent may end up there anyway. Filing first in General Sessions still gives you a quick decision and often encourages settlement.

Before You File: Plan for Collection

With a $25,000 limit, Tennessee plaintiffs can win large judgments in General Sessions, but collecting them takes planning. Under the federal 25% cap, a debtor with $800 a week in disposable earnings could have up to about $200 a week garnished, so a $10,000 judgment would take around a year to collect from wages alone. Before the trial date, write down what you know about the defendant’s employer, bank accounts, vehicles and real estate. If the defendant has little that can be reached, a signed payment agreement entered as a consent judgment may be a practical middle ground.

Suing or Defending as a Business

Businesses are frequent filers in General Sessions, especially landlords, contractors and lenders. Make sure the civil warrant names the correct legal entity, which you can confirm through the Tennessee Secretary of State’s business search, and serve its registered agent. A business defending a case should bring the person with firsthand knowledge of the transaction, plus the contract, invoices and payment records. Because lawyers are allowed in General Sessions, many businesses send counsel, especially in contested cases or when a large amount is at stake.

Common Tennessee General Sessions Cases

  • Security deposits. In counties covered by Tennessee’s Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, landlords must keep deposits in a separate account and provide a written list of damages; a landlord who fails to do both may not keep any part of the deposit (Tenn. Code §66-28-301). Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Evictions. Landlords file detainer warrants in General Sessions, with no dollar limit on possession.
  • Personal loans. A promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Contractor disputes. Contracts, payments, photos and a second estimate.

Common Mistakes in Tennessee General Sessions

  • Relying on the old $15,000 figure. The limit is $25,000 in every county.
  • Missing the docket call. If you are not in the courtroom when your case is called, it may be dismissed or decided without you.
  • Not budgeting for service. Each defendant adds a sheriff fee.
  • Serving the wrong party. For a company, serve its registered agent. Serving an employee at the front desk may not count.
  • Leaving evidence at home. The judge may decide your case in minutes. Bring every document and witness to the first trial date, with copies.
  • Forgetting to ask for costs. Ask the judge to tax court costs, including the sheriff’s fee, to the losing party.
  • Missing the 10-day appeal window. It is short and strictly applied.

How Tennessee Compares

Tennessee’s $25,000 limit ties Delaware for the highest in the country, well above neighbors Georgia ($15,000), North Carolina ($10,000) and Virginia ($5,000). See every state in our small claims court limits by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Tennessee?

$25,000 in General Sessions Court in every county. Evictions and suits to recover personal property have no dollar limit.

How much does it cost to file in Tennessee General Sessions Court?

It depends on the county. In Davidson County, a civil warrant costs $145.75 for one defendant, including sheriff service and litigation tax, plus $52 for each additional defendant.

Does the defendant have to file an answer?

In most General Sessions civil warrant cases, no. The defendant appears on the trial date and presents a defense.

How do I appeal a General Sessions judgment?

File an appeal to the circuit court within 10 days. The case is tried again from the beginning (de novo).

Can I have a lawyer in Tennessee General Sessions Court?

Yes. Lawyers are allowed, though many individuals represent themselves.

How is judgment interest set in Tennessee?

It resets every six months at two percentage points below a formula rate published by the state, under Tenn. Code §47-14-121. The Administrative Office of the Courts posts the current rate.
