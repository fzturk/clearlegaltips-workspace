The short version (2026): Ohio small claims court handles claims for money up to $6,000, not counting interest and costs (Ohio Rev. Code §1925.02). Cases are filed in the small claims division of the municipal or county court, and each court sets its own fee: Franklin County (Columbus) charges $92 plus $10 for certified mail service. Collection agencies and other assignees cannot use small claims, lawyers are allowed, and judgments filed in 2026 earn 7% interest.

Ohio’s small claims divisions are part of its municipal and county courts, and they handle a huge share of everyday disputes: unpaid rent and deposits, bad repair jobs, loans between friends, and car damage. The rules in Chapter 1925 of the Ohio Revised Code are short, and most cases are heard by a magistrate in a single, informal hearing.

This guide covers the Ohio small claims limit, who can and cannot file, fees and service, what happens at the hearing, how to challenge a magistrate’s decision, and how to collect. Rules link to the Ohio Revised Code and official court pages, checked in September 2026.

The Ohio Small Claims Limit: $6,000

Under Ohio Rev. Code §1925.02(A)(1), a small claims division has jurisdiction in civil actions for the recovery of taxes and money only, for amounts not exceeding $6,000, exclusive of interest and costs. The section was most recently amended effective April 9, 2025 (Senate Bill 237), but the $6,000 limit did not change.

Small claims divisions cannot hear:

  • libel, slander, replevin (getting specific property back), malicious prosecution or abuse of process;
  • claims brought by an assignee or agent, which keeps collection agencies and debt buyers out of small claims (with narrow exceptions for tax claims); and
  • claims for punitive damages, although statutory damages under Ohio’s Consumer Sales Practices Act are allowed.

If your claim is over $6,000, you can reduce it and give up the difference, or file on the court’s regular civil docket. A counterclaim over $6,000 can move the whole case to the regular docket.

Where to File

Ohio small claims divisions exist in municipal courts and county courts. Venue follows the court’s ordinary civil rules (§1925.02(A)(3)), which generally means the court covering the area where the defendant lives or does business, or where the events happened. Large cities have their own municipal courts; for example, the Franklin County Municipal Court serves Columbus and much of Franklin County.

One important limit: the court cannot get jurisdiction over a defendant through published or substituted service, so you need an address where the defendant can actually be served.

Filing Fees and Service Costs

There is no single statewide fee. Under §1925.04, each court sets its own filing fee, and you also pay the cost of serving each defendant. As an example, the Franklin County Municipal Court Clerk’s cost schedule lists:

Check your own court’s schedule before filing. If you cannot afford the fees, ask the clerk for a poverty affidavit. If you win, the magistrate normally adds your costs to the judgment.

Who Can Appear: People, Corporations and Lawyers

Individuals can represent themselves, and lawyers are allowed but not required. Corporations have a special rule under §1925.17: a corporation can file and present its claim through a bona fide officer or salaried employee, but without a lawyer it may not engage in cross-examination, argument or other acts of advocacy. In practice, a company that expects a contested hearing often sends a lawyer.

Because assignees cannot use small claims, a person who bought or was assigned someone else’s claim has to use the regular docket.

How to File an Ohio Small Claims Case, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter with a deadline and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Ohio generally allows six years for written contracts and four years for oral contracts; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Pick the right court. Find the municipal or county court for the defendant’s address.
  4. Complete the small claims complaint. State the amount and the nature of your claim. Use the defendant’s exact legal name; for a business, check the Ohio Secretary of State’s business search.
  5. File and pay. Pay the filing fee and service costs. By filing in small claims, the plaintiff waives the right to a jury trial (§1925.04).
  6. Service. The clerk serves the notice under the Ohio Rules of Civil Procedure, usually by certified mail first, with bailiff or process server service if needed. The notice tells the defendant the hearing date.
  7. Watch for a counterclaim. A defendant who files a counterclaim must file and serve it at least seven days before the trial (§1925.02(C)).
  8. Prepare and attend the hearing. Bring documents, photos and witnesses. Many Ohio courts offer free mediation before the hearing.

At the Hearing

Most Ohio small claims cases are heard by a magistrate, an attorney appointed by the court, who then issues a written decision that a judge adopts. Hearings are informal and usually short. To make them count:

  • Lead with the amount and the reason in a sentence or two.
  • Bring three copies of every document, in date order.
  • Show how you calculated the amount with an invoice, receipt or repair estimate.
  • Bring witnesses in person if possible; some magistrates give written statements less weight.

If the defendant does not appear, you may receive a default judgment after briefly proving your claim. If you do not appear, the case is likely to be dismissed.

Challenging the Decision: Objections and Appeals

Because a magistrate hears most cases, the first step to challenge the result is usually to file written objections to the magistrate’s decision within 14 days under Ohio Civil Rule 53. A judge then reviews the objections. You may need a transcript of the hearing to support factual objections.

After the judge enters final judgment, either party can appeal to the court of appeals within 30 days under Ohio Appellate Rule 4. An appeal reviews the record for legal errors; it is not a new trial.

Collecting an Ohio Judgment

If the defendant does not pay after judgment, Ohio courts provide the usual tools:

  • Judgment debtor examination. The court orders the debtor to appear and answer questions under oath about income, bank accounts and property.
  • Wage garnishment. A portion of the debtor’s wages is paid to you through the employer, within federal and Ohio limits.
  • Bank garnishment. Non-exempt funds in the debtor’s account can be taken.
  • Certificate of judgment. Filing it with the county clerk of courts creates a lien on the debtor’s real estate in that county.

Interest. Unless a written contract sets a different rate, judgments accrue interest at the rate certified each year by the Ohio Tax Commissioner under §1343.03. For judgments in 2026, the rate is 7% a year (it was 8% in 2025).

Common Ohio Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Ohio landlords must return the deposit, with an itemized list of any deductions, within 30 days after the tenant leaves and gives a forwarding address. A tenant can recover wrongfully withheld amounts plus an equal amount in damages and reasonable attorney fees. Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Consumer disputes. Claims under Ohio’s Consumer Sales Practices Act, including its statutory damages, can be brought in small claims.
  • Contractor problems. Bring the contract, payments, photos and a second contractor’s estimate.
  • Personal loans. Written proof, such as a promissory note, texts or bank transfers, makes these cases straightforward.

Common Mistakes in Ohio Small Claims

  • Filing an assigned claim. If you bought the debt or someone assigned it to you, small claims will not hear it. File on the regular civil docket instead.
  • Bringing too few copies. Courts want the original complaint plus copies for each defendant. Franklin County, for example, asks for the original and three copies per defendant for certified mail service, and four when you add bailiff or process server service.
  • Asking for the wrong kind of relief. Small claims divisions award money only. If you need a specific item returned (replevin), or want punitive damages, you are in the wrong division.
  • Ignoring a counterclaim. A counterclaim over $6,000 can move the case to the regular docket, where the rules are more formal. Read anything the court sends you.
  • Missing the 14-day objection window. If you disagree with the magistrate’s decision, objections are due within 14 days. Waiting for the judge’s final entry can cost you the chance to raise the issue.
  • Stopping at the judgment. Find out where the defendant works and banks before the hearing, so you are ready to garnish if they do not pay.

How Ohio Compares

Ohio’s $6,000 limit is below the most common national limit of $10,000 and below neighbors Indiana ($10,000) and Pennsylvania ($12,000), though above Kentucky ($2,500). Its ban on assignees is less common and keeps debt buyers out of the small claims docket. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Ohio?

$6,000, not counting interest and court costs (Ohio Rev. Code §1925.02). Claims must be for money only.

How much does it cost to file small claims in Ohio?

It depends on the court. Franklin County Municipal Court, for example, charges $92 plus service, such as $10 for certified mail. Check your local municipal or county court’s cost schedule.

Can a collection agency sue me in Ohio small claims court?

Generally no. Ohio bars assignees and agents from bringing claims in small claims divisions, apart from narrow exceptions such as tax claims. A collection agency or debt buyer must use the regular civil docket.

Do I need a lawyer for Ohio small claims court?

No. Individuals can represent themselves. A corporation can present its claim through an officer or salaried employee, but without a lawyer it cannot cross-examine or argue.

How do I challenge a small claims magistrate’s decision in Ohio?

File written objections within 14 days of the magistrate’s decision under Civil Rule 53. After the judge enters final judgment, an appeal to the court of appeals must be filed within 30 days.

What interest does an Ohio judgment earn in 2026?

7% a year for judgments in 2026, unless a written contract provides a different rate. The Ohio Tax Commissioner certifies the rate each year.
