The short version (2026): Georgia small claims court is the county magistrate court, which hears claims up to $15,000 (O.C.G.A. §15-10-2). Fees are set locally: DeKalb County lists $104 to file plus $35 per defendant for service. The defendant has 30 days to answer, and if they do, the hearing is set 15 to 30 days after notice. There are no juries, and either side can appeal within 30 days for a brand-new trial in state or superior court, except from a default judgment.

Georgia does not have a separate “small claims court.” Instead, each county’s magistrate court hears civil claims up to $15,000, one of the higher limits in the country. The process is informal and built for people without lawyers, but it has a feature that surprises many plaintiffs: the defendant must file an answer, and there is no hearing unless they do.

This guide covers the Georgia magistrate court limit, fees, the answer-and-default process, hearings, appeals, and collection. Rules link to the Official Code of Georgia Annotated and county magistrate court pages, checked in September 2026.

The Georgia Limit: $15,000

Under O.C.G.A. §15-10-2, magistrate courts have jurisdiction over civil claims where the amount demanded or the value of the property claimed does not exceed $15,000. As the DeKalb County Magistrate Court explains, the limit applies to both the plaintiff’s claim and any counterclaim, and court costs do not count toward it.

You can reduce a larger claim to $15,000 and file in magistrate court, but your recovery stays capped at $15,000 even if the case is later appealed to a court that could award more. Magistrate courts cannot hear divorce and family law cases, or cases that require deciding ownership of real estate.

Filing Fees and Service Costs

Magistrate court costs are set locally within limits in state law, so they differ from county to county. For example, the DeKalb County Magistrate Court’s small claims page lists:

Item (DeKalb County Magistrate Court)Cost
Filing fee$104
Service on one defendant$35
Each additional defendant$35 more

DeKalb’s own FAQ page still shows an older $54 filing fee, which is a good reminder to confirm the current amount with the clerk before you go. Many Georgia magistrate courts accept only cash, money orders or certified funds at the counter, and many now accept e-filing. If you cannot afford the costs, ask the clerk about filing a pauper’s affidavit. If you win, the costs are normally added to the judgment.

Send a demand letter before you file. A clear written demand with a deadline settles many Georgia disputes before the defendant ever has to answer a claim, and it becomes evidence if you go to court. LawDepot’s guided payment demand letter takes about ten minutes.

Where to File, and Who Can Appear

File in the magistrate court of the county where the defendant lives. For a corporation or LLC, that is usually the county of its registered office or where it has an office and transacts business. Filing in the wrong county can get the case dismissed.

Most magistrate court parties represent themselves, and lawyers are allowed. Businesses often appear through an owner or employee who knows the facts, though a party that expects a contested hearing may want counsel. If you need help, many county bar associations offer lawyer referral services.

How to File in Georgia Magistrate Court, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Georgia generally allows six years for written contracts and four years for open accounts; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Complete the Statement of Claim. State the amount you are owed, why, and the defendant’s correct name and address. Attach copies of key documents.
  4. File and pay. File with the magistrate court clerk in the defendant’s county and pay the filing and service costs.
  5. Service. A sheriff’s deputy, marshal, constable or permanent process server serves the Statement of Claim on the defendant.
  6. Wait for the answer. Under O.C.G.A. §15-10-43, the defendant has 30 days after service to file an answer or present one orally to the judge or clerk. The answer must admit or deny your claim.
  7. Hearing notice or default. If the defendant answers, the court notifies both sides within 10 days of a hearing date 15 to 30 days after the notice. If the defendant does not answer, they are in default.
  8. Prepare and attend the hearing. Bring documents, photos and witnesses.

Default Judgments in Georgia

If the defendant does not answer within 30 days of service, the defendant is in default. Under §15-10-43, the defendant can still open the default within the next 15 days by filing an answer and paying the costs. After that, you can ask the court for a default judgment. If your claim is for an amount that is not fixed (for example, repair costs you estimate), the judge may ask you to prove the amount at a short hearing.

Default matters for appeals too: under O.C.G.A. §15-10-41, no appeal lies from a default judgment. A defendant who wants to challenge one has to seek review by certiorari or a motion to set it aside, which is much harder than a regular appeal.

At the Hearing

There are no jury trials in magistrate court (§15-10-41(a)). The magistrate hears both sides and decides the case, often the same day. Tips:

  • Lead with the amount and the reason in one or two sentences.
  • Bring three copies of each document, in date order.
  • Show how you calculated the amount with invoices, receipts or estimates.
  • Bring witnesses in person; the magistrate may give written statements less weight.

Many Georgia magistrate courts offer mediation, sometimes on the day of the hearing. A signed mediation agreement can be made an order of the court.

Appeals: A New Trial in State or Superior Court

Either party can appeal a magistrate court judgment (other than a default judgment) to the state court or superior court of the county. The appeal is de novo, meaning the case is tried again from the start, and a jury may be available there. Appeals follow the general rules in Title 5, Chapter 3, Article 2 of the Georgia Code, including the 30-day deadline after the judgment, and require payment of the appeal costs.

A de novo appeal is a real second chance, so a defendant who loses may use it. If you win in magistrate court, be prepared to present your case again.

Collecting a Georgia Judgment

After judgment, Georgia gives you several tools:

  • Writ of fieri facias (fi. fa.). This writ lets the sheriff levy on the debtor’s non-exempt property; recording it on the county’s general execution docket creates a lien on the debtor’s property in that county.
  • Garnishment. You can garnish wages and bank accounts. For ordinary debts, Georgia follows the federal cap of 25% of disposable earnings (or the amount above 30 times the federal minimum wage, if less).
  • Post-judgment discovery. You can ask the debtor, under oath, about income and assets.

Interest. Unless a written contract sets a different rate, judgments bear interest at the prime rate on the day the judgment is entered plus 3% (O.C.G.A. §7-4-12).

Before You File: Can You Actually Collect?

A magistrate court judgment is only as good as the defendant’s ability to pay. Before spending the filing and service fees, ask a few questions. Does the defendant have a steady job you can garnish? Do you know where they bank? Do they own a business, a vehicle without a loan, or real estate? A defendant with none of these may still pay once a judgment is entered, but you may wait a long time. Writing down what you know about the defendant’s employer, bank and property now will save time later if you need a garnishment or a fi. fa. If the answer is “nothing,” a negotiated payment plan may be worth more than a judgment.

Common Georgia Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Georgia landlords must return a deposit, or list the deductions, within 30 days after the tenant moves out. A landlord who improperly withholds it can owe three times the amount plus reasonable attorney fees, unless the error was a good-faith mistake (O.C.G.A. §44-7-35). Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Contractor disputes. Bring the contract, payments, photos and a second contractor’s estimate.
  • Personal loans. Written proof such as a promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Vehicle damage. Repair estimates, photos and the crash report.

Common Mistakes in Georgia Magistrate Court

  • Filing in the wrong county. Most claims must be filed where the defendant lives.
  • Expecting an automatic hearing. No hearing is set unless the defendant answers; if they do not, you need to ask for a default judgment.
  • Relying on an old fee chart. Fees vary by county and change; confirm with the clerk.
  • Ignoring a counterclaim. A defendant can counterclaim up to $15,000 in the same case. Read everything the court sends you.

How Georgia Compares

Georgia’s $15,000 limit is among the ten highest in the country, well above Florida ($8,000) and South Carolina ($7,500) and equal to Idaho and North Dakota. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Georgia?

$15,000 in magistrate court (O.C.G.A. §15-10-2). The limit also applies to counterclaims, and court costs do not count toward it.

How much does it cost to file in Georgia magistrate court?

It depends on the county. DeKalb County lists a $104 filing fee plus $35 to serve each defendant. Check your county’s magistrate court for current costs.

How long does the defendant have to answer in Georgia?

30 days after service. A defendant who misses the deadline can open the default within 15 more days by filing an answer and paying costs.

Is there a jury in Georgia magistrate court?

No. Magistrate court cases are decided by the magistrate. A jury may be available if the case is appealed to state or superior court.

Can I appeal a Georgia magistrate court decision?

Yes, within 30 days, to the state or superior court, where the case is tried again from the start. There is no appeal from a default judgment.

What interest does a Georgia judgment earn?

The prime rate on the day of judgment plus 3%, unless a written contract sets a different rate.
