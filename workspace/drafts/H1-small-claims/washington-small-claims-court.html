The short version (2026): In Washington small claims court, the small claims department of the district court, an individual can sue for up to $10,000 and a business for up to $5,000 (RCW 12.40.010). The filing fee is $35 plus any local dispute resolution surcharge. Lawyers need the judge’s permission to take part, assignees cannot file, and service must be complete at least 10 days before the hearing. A losing party that doesn’t pay within 30 days faces garnishment and added costs.

Washington’s small claims departments are part of the county district courts. The law describes the goal plainly: hearings should be informal, “with the sole object of dispensing speedy and quick justice between the litigants.” Judges can question witnesses and investigate the dispute themselves, and formal pleadings beyond the claim and notice are not required.

This guide covers Washington’s two-tier limit, fees, service, who can appear, appeals and collection. Rules link to chapter 12.40 of the Revised Code of Washington and related statutes, checked in September 2026.

The Washington Limit: $10,000 or $5,000

RCW 12.40.010 sets two limits for claims for the recovery of money:

Who is suingMaximum claim
A natural person (an individual)$10,000
Any other plaintiff (business, corporation, LLC, organization)$5,000

The individual limit was raised to $10,000 in 2019. If your claim is larger, you can reduce it and give up the difference, or file a regular civil case in district court, where lawyers can appear.

Two other rules shape who can use small claims. A claim must be verified by the real claimant, and an assignee of a claim cannot file or prosecute it (RCW 12.40.070), which keeps debt buyers out. And a defendant sued in regular district court for an amount within the small claims limit can ask to transfer the case to small claims under court rules (RCW 12.40.025).

Filing Fee

Under RCW 12.40.020, the filing fee is $35, plus any surcharge a county has authorized under RCW 7.75.035 to fund local dispute resolution centers. A counterclaim, cross-claim or third-party claim carries the same fee. Service costs are extra. When judgment is entered, the statute adds certain costs to it (RCW 12.40.105), and a prevailing party can recover service fees as court costs up to the amounts allowed by law (RCW 12.40.045).

Send a demand letter before you file. A clear written demand with a deadline settles many Washington disputes, and many district courts encourage you to try resolving the problem first. LawDepot’s guided payment demand letter takes about ten minutes.

Lawyers Only With the Judge’s Permission

RCW 12.40.080(1) provides that no attorney, legal paraprofessional, or anyone other than the plaintiff and defendant may appear or participate in a small claims case without the consent of the judicial officer. A corporation may not be represented by a lawyer or legal paraprofessional, except in the limited situation where a case was transferred into small claims from regular district court and a lawyer was already on record for the plaintiff.

In practice, most parties represent themselves, and businesses appear through an owner, officer or employee.

How to File a Washington Small Claim, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Washington generally allows six years for written contracts and three years for oral agreements; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Find the right district court. Usually the county district court where the defendant lives. Some counties have several district court locations.
  4. Complete the Notice of Small Claim. Verify it under oath as the real claimant, state the amount and the basis for the claim, and pay the $35 fee plus any surcharge.
  5. Get a hearing date. The court sets the case for hearing and issues the notice of claim.
  6. Serve the defendant. Under RCW 12.40.040, serve the notice as a summons would be served (personal or substitute service by a non-party adult, sheriff or process server) or by registered or certified mail with a return receipt signed by the defendant filed with the court. Service must be complete at least 10 calendar days before the first hearing.
  7. Consider mediation. Many Washington counties offer small claims mediation through dispute resolution centers, often before or on the day of the hearing.
  8. Go to the hearing. Bring documents and witnesses; you do not need to subpoena witnesses, but they must appear in person to testify.

At the Hearing

The hearing is informal. The judge may informally consult witnesses or otherwise investigate the controversy and make whatever judgment or orders are right, just and equitable (RCW 12.40.080(3)). Tips:

  • Lead with the amount and the reason.
  • Bring copies of contracts, invoices, receipts, photos and messages for the judge and the other side.
  • Bring witnesses in person; there is no need to summon them formally.
  • Show how you calculated damages, for example with repair estimates.

Appeals: Limited by Amount

Washington restricts small claims appeals by dollar amount (RCW 12.40.120):

  • No appeal is allowed from any small claims judgment where the amount claimed was less than $250.
  • A party who chose small claims (usually the plaintiff) can appeal only if their own claim was at least $1,000.

Appeals go to the superior court under the court rules for appeals from courts of limited jurisdiction. A party who lost by default can ask the district court to set aside the default judgment under the civil court rules.

Collecting a Washington Judgment

Washington has a built-in incentive to pay. Under RCW 12.40.105, once judgment is entered it is certified as a district court civil judgment, and the clerk enters it on the district court judgment docket. If the losing party does not pay within 30 days:

  • the judgment can be increased by post-judgment interest and other costs of enforcing it, including reasonable attorney fees, even above the small claims limit;
  • garnishment, execution and other collection process can issue; and
  • you can file a transcript or certified copy with the superior court for its lien docket, which creates a lien on the debtor’s real estate.

Wage garnishment limits. For consumer debts, the exempt amount each week is the greater of 35 times the state minimum wage or 80% of disposable earnings (RCW 6.27.150), so at most 20% can be taken, and less for lower earners. Other debts use a 75% floor.

Interest. Judgment interest depends on the type of claim (RCW 4.56.110): a written contract rate if the judgment states it, 9% for consumer debt judgments, a prime-rate-based formula for tort judgments, and otherwise the maximum rate under RCW 19.52.020.

Small Claims or Regular District Court?

Washington district courts also hear regular civil cases, with more formal rules and lawyers allowed. Small claims is usually the better choice when your claim is within the limit, you are comfortable explaining it yourself, and you want a fast hearing without paying a lawyer. Regular district court makes more sense when you are a business with a claim above $5,000, when you bought or were assigned the debt, or when you want a lawyer to present the case. If someone sues you in regular district court for an amount within the small claims limit, remember that you may be able to transfer the case into small claims under RCW 12.40.025, which can keep costs down for both sides.

Before You File: Plan for Collection

Washington’s rules make unpaid judgments more expensive for the debtor, but you still have to do the work of collecting. Before the hearing, write down what you know about the defendant’s employer, bank and property. For a consumer debt, the 80% wage exemption means that on $1,000 a week of disposable earnings, no more than $200 a week could be garnished, so a $6,000 judgment would take about 30 weeks to collect from wages alone. A signed payment plan can be worth considering, especially if you can ask the court to enter it as part of the judgment.

Common Washington Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Within 30 days after the rental agreement ends and the tenant moves out, the landlord must give a full and specific statement of reasons for keeping any part of the deposit, with required documentation, and refund the rest (RCW 59.18.280). Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Contractor and repair disputes. Contracts, payments, photos and a second estimate.
  • Personal loans. A promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Vehicle damage. Repair estimates, photos and the collision report.

Common Mistakes in Washington Small Claims

  • Filing as a business for more than $5,000. Only individuals can claim up to $10,000.
  • Serving too late. Service must be complete 10 calendar days before the first hearing.
  • Filing an assigned claim. Only the real claimant can use small claims.
  • Bringing a lawyer without asking. The judge’s consent is required.
  • Waiting to enforce. After 30 days of nonpayment, move quickly to garnishment and the superior court lien docket.

How Washington Compares

Washington’s $10,000 individual limit matches the most common limit nationally and neighboring Oregon, while its $5,000 business limit mirrors California’s approach of a lower cap for companies. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Washington?

$10,000 for an individual and $5,000 for a business or other entity (RCW 12.40.010).

How much does it cost to file small claims in Washington?

$35 plus any local dispute resolution surcharge, and the cost of service. Counterclaims carry the same fee.

Can I have a lawyer in Washington small claims court?

Only with the judge’s consent. Corporations generally cannot be represented by a lawyer in small claims.

How far in advance must the defendant be served?

Service must be complete at least 10 calendar days before the first hearing, by personal or substitute service or by certified or registered mail with a signed return receipt.

Can I appeal a Washington small claims judgment?

Only if the amount claimed was at least $250, and a party who chose small claims can appeal only if their own claim was at least $1,000.

What happens if the other side does not pay?

After 30 days, you can garnish wages and bank accounts, seek execution, and file the judgment with the superior court lien docket. Enforcement costs, including reasonable attorney fees, can be added to the judgment.
