The short version (2026): New Jersey small claims court is the Small Claims Section of the Superior Court’s Special Civil Part, and it hears claims up to $5,000, including security deposit claims. The filing fee is $35 for one defendant plus $5 for each additional defendant. Claims from $5,000.01 to $20,000 go to the regular Special Civil Part. Appeals go to the Appellate Division within 45 days, and judgments entered in 2026 within the Special Civil Part limit earn 4.5% interest.

New Jersey’s small claims court is part of the Special Civil Part of the Superior Court in each county. It is designed for people representing themselves, and it is the one place in the New Jersey courts where a business can appear through an officer or employee instead of a lawyer. With a low filing fee and a court officer who handles service, it is one of the more affordable systems in the Northeast.

This guide covers the New Jersey small claims limit, the Special Civil Part alternative, fees, who can appear, the trial, appeals and collection. Rules link to the New Jersey Courts and the New Jersey statutes, checked in September 2026.

The New Jersey Limit: $5,000

According to the New Jersey Courts, small claims are lawsuits for $5,000 or less, and renters can file for the return of a security deposit of $5,000 or less in small claims court. If your claim is larger, New Jersey offers a middle step before the Law Division:

CourtClaims up toWhat it’s like

You can reduce a larger claim to $5,000 to stay in small claims, but you give up the rest.

Filing Fees

The New Jersey Courts list the small claims filing fee as:

  • $35 for one defendant; and
  • $5 for each additional defendant.

The court arranges service of the summons and complaint on the defendant. After judgment, if you need a court officer to levy on the debtor’s property, you will also be charged mileage for the officer’s travel from the county courthouse to the debtor’s home or business. If you cannot afford the fees, ask the clerk about a fee waiver. If you win, costs are added to the judgment.

Who Can Appear

Individuals represent themselves, and lawyers are allowed. New Jersey normally requires business entities to appear through a lawyer, but small claims is the exception: under New Jersey’s court rules on the practice of law, an authorized officer or employee may prosecute and defend claims on behalf of a business entity in small claims, as long as the claim originated with the business and was not acquired by transfer or assignment, and the representative is not a suspended or disbarred attorney.

How to File a New Jersey Small Claim, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. New Jersey generally allows six years for contract and property damage claims; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Choose the right county. File in the Special Civil Part of the county where the defendant lives or does business, or, in some cases, where the transaction happened.
  4. Complete the small claims complaint. Use the Judiciary’s self-help packet or online filing system. Give the defendant’s full legal name and an address where they can be served.
  5. File and pay. $35 for one defendant, $5 more for each additional defendant.
  6. Service. The court sends the summons and complaint to the defendant and schedules the trial date.
  7. Prepare for trial. Organize contracts, receipts, photos and messages, and line up witnesses.
  8. Attend the trial. Many New Jersey small claims cases are first sent to mediation on the trial date with a trained volunteer mediator.

At Trial

Small claims trials are heard by a Superior Court judge. If mediation does not settle the case, the judge hears both sides, reviews the evidence and decides. Tips:

  • Open with the amount and the reason in one or two sentences.
  • Bring the original documents and copies for the judge and the other side.
  • Show how you calculated the amount, for example with repair estimates or invoices.
  • Bring witnesses in person; written statements may count for less.

If a defendant does not appear, the court may enter a default judgment. If you do not appear, your case may be dismissed.

Appeals: 45 Days to the Appellate Division

A party who disagrees with a small claims judgment can appeal to the Appellate Division of the Superior Court within 45 days of the court’s decision. An appeal reviews the trial record for legal error; it is not a new trial, so you will need the transcript of the hearing. Appeals are more formal and expensive than the small claims case itself.

Collecting a New Jersey Judgment

If the defendant does not pay, the Special Civil Part offers these tools:

  • Information subpoena. The debtor must answer written questions about income, bank accounts and property.
  • Wage execution. Generally limited to 10% of the debtor’s gross income; a court can allow more only when the debtor’s income is above 250% of the federal poverty level (N.J.S.A. 2A:17-56).
  • Bank levy. A court officer can levy non-exempt funds in the debtor’s bank account.
  • Statewide lien. Docketing the judgment with the Superior Court clerk creates a statewide lien on the debtor’s real estate.

Interest. New Jersey sets post-judgment interest each year under Court Rule 4:42-11. For judgments entered in 2026 that do not exceed the Special Civil Part limit, the rate is 4.5%; for larger judgments it is 6.5%.

Small Claims or the Regular Special Civil Part?

If your claim is $5,000 or less, small claims is usually the right choice: the forms are simpler, a business can appear without a lawyer, and the case moves quickly. If your claim is between $5,000.01 and $20,000, you have to use the regular Special Civil Part docket, where the defendant files a written answer and the procedures are more formal, though many people still represent themselves. Think carefully before cutting a $7,000 claim down to $5,000 just to stay in small claims; the extra $2,000 may be worth the more formal process. Either way, the same county courthouse and clerk’s office usually handle both.

Before You File: Can You Collect?

New Jersey’s 10% wage execution limit makes collection from paychecks slow. For example, a debtor earning $1,000 a week gross would generally have about $100 a week withheld, so a $5,000 judgment would take roughly a year to collect from wages alone, plus interest and costs. Before filing, write down what you know about the defendant’s employer, bank and property. A statewide judgment lien can still pay off years later if the debtor sells or refinances real estate, but a written settlement may bring money sooner.

Security Deposit Claims

Security deposit disputes are among the most common New Jersey small claims. Under N.J.S.A. 46:8-21.1, a landlord must return the deposit, plus the tenant’s share of interest, within 30 days after the lease ends, by personal delivery or registered or certified mail, along with an itemized list of any deductions. If the landlord wrongfully withholds it, the court awards the tenant double the amount wrongfully withheld, plus costs. Start with a security deposit demand letter, and bring your lease, move-in and move-out photos, and proof of your forwarding address.

Other Common New Jersey Small Claims Cases

  • Contractor and repair disputes. Contracts, payments, photos and a second estimate.
  • Personal loans. A promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Vehicle damage. Repair estimates, photos and the police report.
  • Consumer purchases. Receipts and messages showing the problem and the seller’s refusal.

Common Mistakes in New Jersey Small Claims

  • Filing a $5,001 claim in small claims. Use the regular Special Civil Part for claims up to $20,000.
  • Sending a lawyer for a business when you don’t need to. In small claims, an officer or employee can appear.
  • Missing the 45-day appeal deadline. It runs from the decision.
  • Using a trade name only. Sue a business by its legal name, which you can confirm through the New Jersey business records search, and add any trade name it uses. A judgment against the wrong name is hard to enforce.
  • Skipping mediation. If the court offers mediation on the trial date, take it seriously; a signed agreement can end the case the same day.
  • Forgetting the deposit interest. In a deposit case, the landlord also owes the tenant’s share of interest.

How New Jersey Compares

New Jersey’s $5,000 small claims limit is among the lowest in the country, but its Special Civil Part fills the gap up to $20,000. Neighboring New York allows $10,000 in New York City, and Pennsylvania allows $12,000. See every state in our small claims court limits by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in New Jersey?

$5,000, including claims for the return of a security deposit. Claims up to $20,000 can be filed in the regular Special Civil Part.

How much does it cost to file small claims in New Jersey?

$35 for one defendant and $5 for each additional defendant. Mileage for a court officer may be charged later if you need to collect.

Can a business represent itself in New Jersey small claims court?

Yes. In small claims, an authorized officer or employee can appear for a business on claims that originated with it, rather than a lawyer.

How do I appeal a New Jersey small claims decision?

File an appeal with the Appellate Division of the Superior Court within 45 days of the decision. The appeal reviews the record; it is not a new trial.

How much of a debtor’s wages can be taken in New Jersey?

Generally up to 10% of gross income. A court can allow more only when the debtor earns more than 250% of the federal poverty level.

What interest does a New Jersey small claims judgment earn?

For judgments entered in 2026 within the Special Civil Part limit, 4.5% a year under Court Rule 4:42-11.
