Illinois small claims cases are heard in the circuit court of each county under a short set of Illinois Supreme Court rules (Rules 281 through 289). The rules keep things simple: no written answer is usually required, discovery is limited, and the judge can hold an informal hearing. Cook County adds a separate self-represented litigant court for the smallest claims.

This guide covers the Illinois small claims limit, fees, who can sue, how the court date and trial work, appeals, and collection. Rules link to the Illinois Supreme Court Rules and the Illinois Compiled Statutes, checked in September 2026.

The Illinois Small Claims Limit: $10,000

Under Illinois Supreme Court Rule 281, a small claim is a civil action based on tort or contract for money not in excess of $10,000, exclusive of interest and costs, or for the collection of taxes not in excess of that amount. The limit rose from $5,000 to $10,000 in 2007 and has not changed since.

If your claim is over $10,000, you can reduce it and give up the difference, or file a regular law division or municipal division case, where the rules are more formal.

In Cook County’s First Municipal District (Chicago), there is also a Self-Represented Litigant (Pro Se) Court for disputes of $3,000 or less, where parties act as their own attorneys. Forms are completed at the Self-Represented Litigant desk (Room 602), according to the Cook County Clerk.

Filing Fees

Each county’s circuit clerk sets small claims fees within limits set by state law, so the cost varies widely across Illinois. Cook County is on the high end. The Cook County Clerk’s civil filing fee schedule (form CCG-0603, dated May 21, 2026) lists:

Cook County civil claim amountNew case filing fee
Up to $2,500$287
$2,500.01 to $15,000$379

A statutory mailing fee plus postage per defendant is added when the clerk serves by mail, and sheriff or special process server fees are extra. Downstate counties generally charge less, so check your county circuit clerk’s fee schedule before you file. If you cannot afford the fees, apply for a fee waiver with the statewide Application for Waiver of Court Fees. If you win, the court can add your costs to the judgment.

Who Can Sue: Individuals and Corporations

Individuals can file and try their own cases, and lawyers are allowed. Corporations are treated differently under Rule 282(b):

  • A corporation cannot appear as a claimant, assignee, subrogee or counterclaimant in small claims unless it is represented by a lawyer.
  • A corporation can defend a small claims case without a lawyer, through an officer, director, manager, department manager or supervisor.

If you run a business as a sole proprietor and sue in your own name, you can represent yourself.

How to File an Illinois Small Claims Case, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Illinois generally allows ten years for written contracts and five years for oral contracts; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Prepare the complaint. Under Rule 282(a), a short, simple complaint states your name, address and phone number, the defendant’s name and residence or place of business, and the nature and amount of the claim with dates. If the claim is based on a written document (a contract, lease or note), attach a copy.
  4. File and pay. File with the circuit clerk in the county where the defendant lives or where the transaction occurred. Most Illinois counties require e-filing, with exemptions available for self-represented people who cannot e-file.
  5. Get the summons. Under Rule 283, the summons requires the defendant to appear on a date not less than 40 or more than 61 days after it is issued.
  6. Serve the defendant. Service can be by the sheriff, a special process server appointed by the court or, in many counties, certified mail by the clerk.
  7. Prepare for trial on the return date. Under Rule 286(a), the case can be tried on the day set for appearance. Bring your evidence and witnesses to the first court date.

The Court Date and Trial

Illinois small claims rules are designed for speed:

  • No answer needed. A defendant who appears does not have to file a written answer unless the court orders one; the allegations are treated as denied (Rule 286(a)).
  • Informal hearing. The court may decide the case at an informal hearing, on its own motion or at a party’s request (Rule 286(b)), where the usual rules of evidence are relaxed.
  • Limited discovery and motions. No depositions, interrogatories or requests to admit, and no motions, without the court’s permission (Rule 287).
  • Jury only on request. A small claim is tried by the judge unless the plaintiff files a jury demand when the case is filed, or the defendant does so by the appearance date. The jury fee is $12.50 for six jurors or $25 for twelve (Rule 285).

If the defendant does not appear, you can ask for a default judgment. If you do not appear, the case may be dismissed.

Many courts also refer small claims to mediation. Be ready to discuss settlement on the first court date.

Installment Payment Orders

Illinois judges can order a small claims judgment to be paid on a set date or in installments, and can pause other collection while the debtor keeps up with payments (Rule 288). Installment plans cannot run longer than three years. If the debtor misses payments, you can ask the court to lift the stay and move to collection.

Appeals

Either side can appeal a final small claims judgment to the Illinois Appellate Court by filing a notice of appeal within 30 days of the judgment. An appeal reviews the trial record for legal errors; it is not a new trial, so you will usually need a transcript or an agreed statement of facts. A party who missed the trial may instead ask the trial court to vacate a default judgment, generally within 30 days.

Collecting an Illinois Judgment

If the defendant does not pay or follow an installment order, Illinois provides these tools:

  • Citation to discover assets. The debtor must appear and answer questions about income and assets, and the citation can freeze assets while it is pending.
  • Wage deduction. The employer withholds the lesser of 15% of gross wages or the amount by which weekly disposable earnings exceed 45 times the minimum wage (735 ILCS 5/12-803).
  • Non-wage garnishment. Reaches bank accounts and other money held by third parties, subject to exemptions.
  • Memorandum of judgment. Recording it with the county recorder creates a lien on the debtor’s real estate in that county.

Interest. Judgments generally draw interest at 9% a year, but consumer-debt judgments of $25,000 or less draw 5%, and judgments against local governments draw 6% (735 ILCS 5/2-1303).

Common Illinois Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Under the Illinois Security Deposit Return Act, a landlord of a building with five or more units must give an itemized statement of deductions within 30 days and return the balance within 45 days; a landlord who does not comply can owe twice the deposit plus costs and attorney fees. Chicago tenants have additional rights under the city’s landlord-tenant ordinance. Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Contractor disputes. Bring the contract, payments, photos and a second estimate.
  • Personal loans. A written promissory note must be attached to the complaint.
  • Vehicle damage. Repair estimates, photos and the crash report.

Common Mistakes in Illinois Small Claims

  • Treating the first court date as a check-in. The case can be tried on the return date. Bring every document and witness the first time.
  • Forgetting to attach the contract. If your claim is based on a written document, Rule 282 requires a copy with the complaint, or an affidavit explaining why it is unavailable.
  • Letting a corporation file without a lawyer. The clerk may accept the papers, but a corporate plaintiff without counsel risks dismissal.
  • Missing the jury deadline. If you want a jury, the plaintiff must demand one when filing and the defendant by the appearance date.

How Illinois Compares

Illinois’s $10,000 limit matches the most common limit nationally and neighboring Indiana, and is higher than Missouri ($5,000) and Kentucky ($2,500). Cook County’s filing fees, however, are several times what most states charge. See every state in our small claims court limits by state table, or compare with Michigan and Ohio.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Illinois?

$10,000, not counting interest and costs (Illinois Supreme Court Rule 281). Cook County’s First Municipal District also runs a self-represented litigant court for claims of $3,000 or less.

How much does it cost to file small claims in Illinois?

It depends on the county. Cook County’s May 2026 schedule lists $287 for claims up to $2,500 and $379 for claims over $2,500, plus mailing or service costs. Other counties generally charge less. Fee waivers are available.

Can a business sue in Illinois small claims court without a lawyer?

Not if it is a corporation. Rule 282 requires a corporation to be represented by counsel to bring a claim, although it may defend a small claims case through an officer or manager. Sole proprietors suing in their own name can represent themselves.

When is the court date in an Illinois small claims case?

The summons sets a date 40 to 61 days after it is issued, and the case can be tried that day, so bring your evidence and witnesses to the first court date.

Can I get a jury in Illinois small claims court?

Yes, if you ask on time: the plaintiff when filing, or the defendant by the appearance date. The fee is $12.50 for six jurors or $25 for twelve.

What interest does an Illinois judgment earn?

9% a year in most cases, 5% for consumer-debt judgments of $25,000 or less, and 6% against local governments.
