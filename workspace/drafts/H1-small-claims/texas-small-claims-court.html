The short version (2026): Texas small claims court cases are heard in justice court, and you can sue for up to $20,000, including any attorney fees but not interest or court costs. Filing costs about $54 in court costs plus a service fee per defendant ($139 total with constable service in Harris County). Lawyers are allowed, either side can ask for a jury for $22, and either side can appeal within 21 days for a brand-new trial in county court.

Texas has one of the highest small claims limits in the country, and its justice courts handle hundreds of thousands of civil cases a year. The process is built for people without lawyers: plain-language rules (Part V of the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure), a short petition, and a judge who is allowed to develop the facts by asking questions. But Texas also has two rules that surprise people who have used small claims elsewhere: juries are available, and wage garnishment is off the table for ordinary debts.

This guide covers the Texas small claims limit, the costs, where to file, each step of a case, appeals, and what you can realistically collect after you win. Rules link to the Texas Rules of Civil Procedure and state statutes, checked in September 2026.

The Texas Small Claims Limit: $20,000

Justice courts have jurisdiction over civil matters where the amount in controversy is $20,000 or less, exclusive of interest (Tex. Gov’t Code §27.031). Rule 500.3(a) defines a small claims case as a lawsuit for money damages, civil penalties, personal property or other relief allowed by law, where the claim is no more than $20,000, excluding statutory interest and court costs but including attorney fees, if any.

That last detail matters. If your contract lets you recover attorney fees and you hire a lawyer, those fees count toward the $20,000. Most people filing their own case will not have attorney fees, so the full amount is available for the debt or damages.

Justice courts in Texas hear four kinds of civil cases under Part V, and it helps to know which one you are filing:

Case typeUsed forRule
Small claims caseMost disputes between people and businesses: unpaid loans, property damage, deposits, bad workTRCP 500.3(a)
Debt claim caseSuits by lenders, debt buyers, collection agencies and assignees to collect a debtTRCP 500.3(b)
Repair and remedy caseA residential tenant asking the court to order a landlord to make repairs (up to $20,000)TRCP 500.3(c)
Eviction caseA landlord seeking possession of the propertyTRCP 500.3(d)

Texas raised the justice court limit from $10,000 to $20,000 on September 1, 2020 (Senate Bill 2342), so older guides and forms still show the lower number. If your claim is larger than $20,000, you can reduce it to $20,000 and give up the rest, or file in county court or district court.

What It Costs to File in Texas

Justice court filing costs are set by statute as consolidated court costs, and service fees are set by each county’s constables and sheriff. Harris County publishes a typical schedule, effective January 1, 2025:

Item (Harris County justice courts)Amount
Original petition court costs (local $33 + state $21)$54
Constable service, per defendant$85
Total to file and serve one defendant$139
Jury fee (if you want a jury)$22

Other counties charge similar court costs but may set different service fees, so check with the justice of the peace precinct where you file. If you cannot afford the fees, file a Statement of Inability to Afford Payment of Court Costs; the court provides the form. If you win, the judgment normally includes your court costs.

Where to File: County and Precinct

Texas justice courts are organized by county and precinct, and filing in the wrong precinct is one of the most common early mistakes. Under Rule 502.4, a defendant in a small claims case is generally entitled to be sued in one of these places:

  • the county and precinct where the defendant lives;
  • the county and precinct where the incident, or most of the incidents, that led to the claim happened;
  • the county and precinct where the contract was to be performed; or
  • the county and precinct where the property is located, in a suit to recover personal property.

The full venue rules are in Chapter 15, Subchapter E of the Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code. Your petition must state why the precinct you picked is correct.

Lawyers and Juries

Unlike California or Michigan, Texas allows lawyers in justice court, and a party may also be represented by an authorized non-attorney in some situations. Most individuals handle their own cases.

Any party can ask for a jury. Under Rule 504.1, the written demand must be filed at least 14 days before trial, with a $22 jury fee (or a Statement of Inability). If nobody asks for a jury on time, the judge decides the case.

How to File a Texas Small Claims Case, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter by a method you can prove, such as certified mail, and keep a copy.
  2. Check the deadline. Most Texas debt and contract claims must be filed within four years; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Complete the Small Claims Petition. The Texas Supreme Court’s approved form asks for the parties’ names and addresses, the amount, the basis of the claim and why venue is proper. Include the defendant’s phone number and the last three digits of their driver’s license and Social Security numbers if you know them.
  4. File and pay. File with the justice court in the correct precinct, in person, by mail, or through eFileTexas where the court accepts it.
  5. Serve the defendant. Under Rule 501.2, the citation can be served by a sheriff or constable, a certified process server, the clerk by registered or certified mail, or a court-authorized adult. You cannot serve it yourself.
  6. Watch for the answer. The defendant has 14 days after service to file a written answer. If they do not, you can ask for a default judgment, but you still need to prove the amount.
  7. Prepare for trial. Organize contracts, receipts, photos, texts and estimates in date order, with copies for the judge and the other side. You can ask the court for subpoenas for witnesses.
  8. Attend the trial. The judge may ask questions of both sides and can allow evidence that would not be admissible in higher courts. Many precincts also offer mediation.

Appeals: A New Trial in County Court

Either side can appeal a Texas justice court judgment, and the appeal is a completely new trial (a trial de novo) in county court. Under Rule 506.1:

  • The appeal must be perfected within 21 days after the judgment is signed, or after a motion to reinstate, set aside or for new trial is denied.
  • A plaintiff appeals by filing a $500 bond. A defendant must file a bond equal to twice the amount of the judgment.
  • A cash deposit can replace the bond, and a party who cannot afford it can file a Statement of Inability to Afford Payment of Court Costs instead.

Because the appeal starts the case over, a defendant who loses in justice court sometimes appeals simply to delay payment. The bond requirement is there to protect you when that happens.

Collecting a Texas Judgment: Know the Exemptions First

Texas is one of the most debtor-friendly states in the country, and that shapes what a small claims win is worth:

  • No wage garnishment for ordinary debts. Current wages for personal services are exempt from garnishment under the Texas Constitution (art. XVI, §28) and Property Code §42.001. Exceptions are limited to things like child support, spousal maintenance, taxes and certain student loans, so a small claims judgment for a loan or repair bill cannot be collected from a paycheck.
  • Homestead and personal property exemptions. A debtor’s homestead is protected, and Property Code §42.001 protects personal property up to $50,000 for an individual or $100,000 for a family.
  • What you can reach. Non-exempt bank accounts (through a writ of garnishment against the bank), non-exempt property (through a writ of execution), and real estate other than the homestead, through an abstract of judgment recorded in the county property records.
  • Interest. Post-judgment interest follows the prime rate, with a floor of 5% and a cap of 15% (Tex. Fin. Code §304.003).

Before you file, ask yourself whether the defendant has a bank account, a business or non-exempt property. If the only source of payment is wages, a negotiated payment plan may be worth more than a judgment. Our guide on how to collect money owed walks through the options.

Common Texas Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. A Texas landlord generally must refund the deposit or give an itemized list of deductions within 30 days after the tenant surrenders the property. A security deposit demand letter is the usual first step.
  • Contractor disputes. Bring the written bid or contract, payment records, photos and a second contractor’s estimate.
  • Vehicle damage. Repair estimates and photos, plus the police report number if there was one.
  • Personal loans. A promissory note, texts or bank transfers showing the loan and any repayments.

How Texas Compares

Texas’s $20,000 limit ties it with Minnesota, Utah and West Virginia for the third-highest in the country, behind only Delaware and Tennessee at $25,000. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Texas?

$20,000, not counting statutory interest and court costs. Attorney fees, if any, count toward the $20,000. Claims are filed in justice court.

How much does it cost to file small claims in Texas?

Court costs are about $54, plus a service fee for each defendant. In Harris County the total with constable service is $139 for one defendant (schedule effective January 1, 2025). A jury costs $22 more. You can file a Statement of Inability if you cannot afford the fees.

How long does the defendant have to answer?

Fourteen days after being served. If the defendant does not file an answer, the plaintiff can ask the judge for a default judgment.

Can I appeal a Texas small claims judgment?

Yes. Either side can appeal within 21 days for a new trial in county court. A plaintiff posts a $500 bond; a defendant posts a bond of twice the judgment, or either can file a Statement of Inability.

Can I garnish wages with a Texas small claims judgment?

Generally no. Texas exempts current wages from garnishment for ordinary debts. You can still pursue non-exempt bank accounts, non-exempt property and real estate other than a homestead.

Do I need a lawyer for Texas small claims court?

No. Justice court rules are written for people representing themselves, though lawyers are allowed. Any attorney fees you claim count toward the $20,000 limit.
