The short version (2026): Colorado small claims court, a division of each county court, hears cases up to $7,500. Filing costs $31 for claims of $500 or less and $55 above that. You may file no more than 2 small claims a month and 18 a year in a county. The defendant must be served at least 15 days before trial, there are no juries, and lawyers are generally barred unless the other side has one. Appeals must be filed within 14 days, and judgments earn 8% interest compounded annually.

Colorado’s small claims courts are designed for people handling their own disputes. The Colorado Judicial Branch publishes a clear step-by-step guide (form JDF 248), the main form (JDF 250) combines the notice, claim and summons, and low-cost mediation is available through the state’s Office of Dispute Resolution.

This guide covers the Colorado small claims limit, fees, filing limits, service, the lawyer rule, trial, appeals and collection. Rules link to Part 4 of Article 6, Title 13 of the Colorado Revised Statutes and the Colorado Judicial Branch, checked in September 2026.

The Colorado Limit: $7,500

Colorado small claims cases can be used for disputes worth up to $7,500 to recover money or property, to enforce or set aside a contract, or to enforce restrictive covenants (C.R.S. §13-6-403; JDF 248). The amount does not include interest or costs. You can file for a larger amount, but you can only collect $7,500 if you win, and you cannot split a claim over $7,500 into two or more smaller cases.

Two other limits matter:

  • Filing frequency: a plaintiff may file no more than 2 small claims a month and 18 a year in each county (C.R.S. §13-6-411; JDF 248).
  • No juries: small claims cases are decided by a judge or magistrate.

For claims over $7,500, Colorado county courts hear civil cases up to $25,000 on the regular county civil docket.

Service by the sheriff or a process server costs extra. If you cannot afford the filing fee, file JDF 205 (Motion to Waive Fees) with JDF 206 (Order); people who receive certain public benefits may qualify automatically. If you win, the court can award your filing, service and witness fees as costs.

Lawyers: Only If the Other Side Has One

Under C.R.S. §13-6-407, attorneys generally may not appear or take part in small claims cases, except when they are themselves a party or act as a full-time officer or employee of a business that is a party. If an attorney does appear as permitted by law or supreme court rule, the other party may also be represented by a lawyer. The case still follows small claims procedure.

Businesses can appear through a full-time officer or employee, a partner or an active member. In security deposit and rental damage cases, a property manager who handled the deposit or signed the lease for the owner may represent the owner.

How to File a Colorado Small Claim, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Colorado generally allows three years for contract claims and six years for a liquidated debt such as a promissory note; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Complete JDF 250 (Notice, Claim and Summons). Do not attach exhibits to the form.
  4. File in the right county. File where the defendant lives or works, or where the property is located, and pay the fee. The clerk fills in the trial date.
  5. Serve the defendant at least 15 days before trial. Use the sheriff or a professional process server, who files the Return of Service. You cannot serve the papers yourself. The clerk can also mail them by certified mail, though the Judicial Branch notes this is not the preferred method and can cause delays.
  6. Serving a business? Look up its registered agent on the Colorado Secretary of State’s business search and serve that person.
  7. Consider mediation. The Office of Dispute Resolution offers low-cost mediation. If you settle, both parties sign JDF 75 (Stipulation).
  8. Prepare for trial. Label your exhibits, make copies for the court and the other party, and line up witnesses or ask for subpoenas.

At Trial

The judge or magistrate hears both sides, decides who wins and the amount, and may award costs to the winner. There is no jury. Tips:

  • Outline the key points you need to prove and match each one to a document or witness.
  • Bring at least one copy of each exhibit for the court and one for the other party.
  • If a witness will not come voluntarily, ask the court for a subpoena in advance.

After judgment, the court may give the winner a Motion and Order for Interrogatories (JDF 252), which requires the debtor to answer written questions about income and assets.

Appeals: 14 Days

Either party can appeal a small claims judgment. According to the Colorado Judicial Branch, the appellant must file the appeal paperwork with the county court within 14 calendar days of the decision or judgment. The appeal goes to the district court, which reviews the record of the small claims trial for legal error; it is not a new trial, so the record (recording or transcript) matters.

Collecting a Colorado Judgment

The court does not collect for you. Tools include:

  • Interrogatories. Written questions the debtor must answer about assets and income (JDF 252).
  • Wage garnishment. Colorado caps garnishment of ordinary debts at the lesser of 20% of disposable earnings or the amount by which weekly disposable earnings exceed 40 times the federal or Colorado minimum wage (C.R.S. §13-54-104). A debtor can also ask the court to reduce it for hardship.
  • Bank garnishment and writs of execution. Reach non-exempt bank funds and property.
  • Transcript of judgment. Recording it with the county clerk and recorder creates a lien on the debtor’s real estate.

Interest. The Colorado Judicial Branch states that the legal rate of interest is 8% compounded annually. The Judicial Branch’s JDF 82 guide explains collection step by step. See also our guide on how to collect money owed.

Small Claims or Regular County Court?

If your claim is $7,500 or less, you can usually choose between small claims and the regular county court civil docket, which hears cases up to $25,000. Small claims is cheaper, faster and built for people without lawyers, and the other side generally cannot bring one unless you do. Regular county court makes more sense if you need formal discovery to get documents from the other side, if you want a lawyer to present your case, or if your claim is above $7,500 and you do not want to give up the difference. Remember that you can only file two small claims a month in a county, so a business with many small debts to collect may need to use the regular docket for some of them.

Before You File: Can You Collect?

Colorado’s 20% garnishment cap affects how quickly a judgment turns into money. Suppose you win $6,000 and the debtor has $900 a week in disposable earnings. At 20%, about $180 a week could be garnished, so collecting from wages alone would take around 34 weeks, plus interest and costs, assuming the minimum wage floor does not reduce the amount further. If the debtor has no job or bank account you can find, consider whether a written payment plan is a better outcome than a judgment. Write down what you know about the debtor’s employer, bank and property before the trial.

Common Colorado Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Colorado landlords must return the deposit, or a written statement of deductions, within one month after the lease ends, or within the longer period the lease allows (up to 60 days). Property managers can represent owners in these cases. Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Contractor and repair disputes. Contracts, payments, photos and a second estimate.
  • Personal loans. A promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Restrictive covenants. Colorado small claims can also enforce restrictive covenants, such as HOA rules, within the $7,500 limit.

Common Mistakes in Colorado Small Claims

  • Serving too late. Service must be completed at least 15 days before trial.
  • Serving the papers yourself. A party cannot serve; use the sheriff or a process server.
  • Exceeding the filing limits. Two cases a month, 18 a year per county.
  • Attaching exhibits to JDF 250. Bring them to trial instead.
  • Missing the 14-day appeal window. It runs from the decision, not from when you receive a copy.

How Colorado Compares

Colorado’s $7,500 limit is below the most common national figure of $10,000 and well below neighbor Utah ($20,000), but above Arizona ($5,000). Its low fees, monthly filing cap and matched-lawyer rule are distinctive. See every state in our small claims court limits by state table, or compare with Arizona.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Colorado?

$7,500, not counting interest and costs. You cannot split a larger claim into several cases.

How much does it cost to file small claims in Colorado?

$31 for claims of $500 or less and $55 for claims over $500, plus service costs. Fee waivers are available.

How many small claims can I file in Colorado?

No more than 2 a month and 18 a year in each county.

Can I bring a lawyer to Colorado small claims court?

Generally no. Lawyers can appear only in limited situations, such as when they are a party; if the other side has a lawyer as permitted by law, you may have one too.

How long do I have to appeal in Colorado small claims?

14 calendar days from the decision or judgment. The district court reviews the record for legal error rather than holding a new trial.

How much of a debtor’s wages can be garnished in Colorado?

For ordinary debts, the lesser of 20% of disposable earnings or the amount above 40 times the federal or Colorado minimum wage per week.
