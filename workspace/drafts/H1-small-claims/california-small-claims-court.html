The short version (2026): In California small claims court an individual can sue for up to $12,500 and a business for up to $6,250. You may file only two claims over $2,500 per calendar year. The filing fee is $30, $50 or $75 depending on the amount. Lawyers cannot appear at the hearing, the hearing is set 20 to 70 days after filing, and the plaintiff cannot appeal a loss, though the defendant can.

California runs one of the busiest small claims systems in the country, and one of the most self-help friendly. The forms are short, every county offers a free small claims advisor, and the whole case is designed to be handled without a lawyer. The trade-off is a set of rules you need to know before you file: separate limits for people and businesses, a cap on how many larger claims you can bring each year, and one-way appeal rights.

This guide walks through the California small claims court limit, the fees, who can sue whom, and each step from the demand letter to collecting your money. Every rule links to the Code of Civil Procedure or the California courts’ self-help site, checked in September 2026.

The California Small Claims Limit: $12,500 or $6,250

California has two limits, and which one applies depends on who is filing:

Who is suingMaximum claimLaw
An individual (a “natural person”)$12,500CCP §116.221
A business, corporation, LLC, partnership or public entity$6,250CCP §116.220(a)
Any filer, for a claim over $2,500No more than 2 such claims per calendar year statewideCCP §116.231

The individual limit rose from $10,000 to $12,500 on January 1, 2024 (Senate Bill 71). A sole proprietor suing in their own name counts as an individual; an LLC or corporation, even a one-person company, is held to $6,250.

The two-per-year rule catches people by surprise. If you have already filed two claims over $2,500 anywhere in California this calendar year, a third one must either be reduced to $2,500 or filed in regular civil court. When your claim is over $2,500, the claim form asks you to confirm this under penalty of perjury.

If your claim is bigger than the limit, you can still use small claims by reducing it to the limit, but the difference is gone for good. You cannot split one debt into two cases. The alternative is a limited civil case in superior court, where the first-paper filing fee is $205 for demands of $10,000 or less and $330 above that (Gov. Code §70613), and where lawyers are allowed.

One recent change: since October 1, 2025, a landlord can no longer bring a small claims case for unpaid COVID-19 rental debt above $12,500, according to the California courts’ self-help center.

Filing Fees and Other Costs

Small claims filing fees are set by CCP §116.230 and are the same in every county:

Amount you are claimingFiling fee
$1,500 or less$30
$1,500.01 to $5,000$50
Over $5,000$75
Any amount, if you filed more than 12 small claims in California in the last 12 months$100

On top of the filing fee, budget for service. The clerk can mail the claim by certified mail for a small fee, or you can pay a sheriff, marshal or registered process server. If you cannot afford the costs, ask for a fee waiver (Form FW-001). If you win, the judge can add your filing and service costs to the judgment.

Send a demand letter before you file. California’s claim form asks whether you asked the defendant to pay before suing, and a clear written demand is the easiest way to prove it. LawDepot’s guided payment demand letter takes about ten minutes.

Who Can Sue, and Who You Can Sue

Anyone 18 or older can file; a minor needs a guardian ad litem. You can sue a person, a business or a California public entity. Before suing a city, county or state agency, though, you generally must file a government claim with that agency first and wait for it to be rejected.

Name the defendant exactly. For a business, look up the legal name on the California Secretary of State’s business search and, if it uses a trade name, include both (for example, “Smith Enterprises LLC, doing business as Smith Roofing”). A judgment against the wrong name can be almost impossible to collect.

Where to file: usually the county where the defendant lives or does business, where the contract was signed or was to be performed, or where the injury or damage happened. The self-help center has a venue chart, and the claim form asks you to pick the reason.

No Lawyers at the Hearing

California is one of the few states that keep lawyers out of small claims hearings entirely. Under CCP §116.530, no attorney may take part in conducting or defending a small claims action, with narrow exceptions (an attorney suing or being sued personally, for example). You can still pay a lawyer for advice before or after the case.

Every county also provides a free small claims advisor under CCP §116.940. Advisors help with forms, procedures, the hearing and collection, by phone, in person or online. It is the single most underused resource in California small claims.

How to File in California, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. The claim form requires you to state that you demanded payment where possible (CCP §116.320). Keep a copy of your demand letter and proof you sent it.
  2. Complete Form SC-100, Plaintiff’s Claim and ORDER to Go to Small Claims Court. Add Form SC-100A if you have more than two plaintiffs or defendants. The self-help center offers a guided online version for many courts.
  3. File and pay the fee. File with the small claims clerk in the correct county, in person, by mail or online where the court accepts e-filing. The clerk sets the hearing at least 20 and no more than 70 days after filing (CCP §116.330).
  4. Serve the defendant. Service can be by the clerk’s certified mail, personal delivery by an adult who is not a party, or substituted service (CCP §116.340). It must be completed at least 15 days before the hearing if the defendant lives in the county, or 20 days if they live outside it.
  5. File proof of service. Whoever served the papers completes Form SC-104, and it must be filed at least 5 days before the hearing.
  6. Prepare for the hearing. Gather contracts, invoices, receipts, photos, texts and repair estimates, in date order, with copies for the judge and the defendant. Bring witnesses or their written statements.
  7. Go to court. The judge or temporary judge hears both sides, usually in a few minutes each. Many courts offer free mediation on the day of the hearing.

If the defendant believes you owe them money, they can file a Defendant’s Claim (Form SC-120) in the same case, and the judge decides both claims at the hearing.

At the Hearing

A California small claims hearing is informal. There is no jury, and the formal rules of evidence do not apply. The judge will usually ask the plaintiff to explain the claim, look at the documents, then hear from the defendant. Tips that make a difference:

  • Open with one sentence: who owes you how much, and why.
  • Bring a simple one-page summary: dates, amounts, and a list of your exhibits.
  • Show how you calculated the amount. A repair estimate or an invoice is much stronger than your own guess.
  • If you need an interpreter, ask the clerk in advance; courts provide them.

The decision may be announced at the hearing or mailed afterward on a Notice of Entry of Judgment (Form SC-130).

Appeals: Only the Defendant Can Appeal

California’s appeal rule is one-sided. Under CCP §116.710, the plaintiff has no right to appeal a judgment on their own claim; by choosing small claims court, you accept the decision as final. The defendant can appeal to the superior court, where a different judge hears the whole case again. A plaintiff who loses on a defendant’s counterclaim can appeal that part.

The notice of appeal (Form SC-140) must be filed within 30 days after the clerk delivers or mails the notice of entry of judgment (CCP §116.750). A party who missed the hearing cannot appeal, but can ask the court to cancel (vacate) the judgment if they had a good reason.

Collecting Your Judgment

The judgment cannot be enforced until the appeal period ends. After that, if the defendant has not paid:

  • Statement of assets. Under CCP §116.830 the losing party must complete Form SC-133, Judgment Debtor’s Statement of Assets, and send it to you within 30 days of the notice of entry of judgment. Refusing can lead to contempt sanctions.
  • Debtor examination. If the SC-133 is not returned or is incomplete, you can ask the court to order the debtor to come in and answer questions under oath.
  • Writ of execution. This court order lets a levying officer garnish wages or levy a bank account, subject to California’s exemptions.
  • Abstract of judgment. Recording it with the county recorder creates a lien on the debtor’s real estate in that county.

Judgments earn interest at 10% a year, but only 5% for judgments entered on or after January 1, 2023 on certain medical debt under $200,000 or personal debt under $50,000 (CCP §685.010). For more ways to get paid, see our guide on how to collect money owed.

Common California Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Landlords must return the deposit or an itemized statement within 21 days after move-out; a tenant can seek the deposit plus a penalty for bad-faith retention. Our security deposit demand letter is a good first step.
  • Unpaid loans and invoices. Bring the note, texts or emails acknowledging the debt, and a payment history.
  • Contractor disputes. Bring the contract, photos, and a second contractor’s written estimate to finish or fix the work.
  • Car accidents. Property damage claims up to the limit, supported by repair estimates and photos.

How California Compares

At $12,500, California sits above the most common national limit of $10,000 but well below Texas ($20,000) and Tennessee ($25,000). Its $6,250 business cap and two-per-year rule are unusual, as is the full ban on lawyers. For every state’s limit, fee and court, see our small claims court limits by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in California in 2026?

$12,500 for an individual and $6,250 for a business or other entity. No one may file more than two claims over $2,500 in a calendar year anywhere in California.

How much does it cost to file small claims in California?

$30 for claims of $1,500 or less, $50 up to $5,000, and $75 above $5,000. Anyone who filed more than 12 small claims in the state in the previous 12 months pays $100. Service costs are extra, and fee waivers are available.

Can I have a lawyer in California small claims court?

Not at the hearing. CCP §116.530 bars attorneys from representing parties in small claims, with narrow exceptions. You can get legal advice before or after, and every county offers a free small claims advisor.

How long does a California small claims case take?

The hearing is scheduled 20 to 70 days after you file. The court’s self-help center says most people have a court date in about one to two months. After judgment, there is a 30-day window for the defendant to appeal before you can start collecting.

Can the plaintiff appeal in California small claims?

No. The plaintiff cannot appeal a judgment on their own claim. Only the defendant can appeal, within 30 days after the notice of entry of judgment is mailed or delivered.

Can a business sue in California small claims court?

Yes, for up to $6,250. The business must appear through an owner, officer or employee who is not a lawyer and who knows the facts, not through an attorney.
