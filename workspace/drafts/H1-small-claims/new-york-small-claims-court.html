The short version (2026): In New York small claims court, the limit depends on the court: $10,000 in New York City Civil Court, $5,000 in city and district courts elsewhere, and $3,000 in town and village courts. The filing fee is just $15 or $20, and the clerk mails the claim for you. Businesses cannot use small claims; they file commercial claims instead. New York has one of the cheapest and most accessible small claims systems in the country. For $15 or $20, the court clerk mails your claim to the defendant, schedules a hearing (evening sessions are common in New York City), and gives you the choice of a judge or a volunteer arbitrator. The catch is that New York has several different local courts, each with its own dollar limit, and you need to file in the right one.

This guide explains the New York small claims limits by court, the fees, who can sue, how the hearing works, the narrow appeal rule, and the unusual tools New York gives you to collect from a business that ignores a judgment. Rules link to the New York court acts and the CPLR, checked in September 2026.

New York Small Claims Limits by Court

Each local court has its own statute, and each sets its own small claims limit:

CourtWhereSmall claims limitLaw
New York City Civil CourtBronx, Brooklyn (Kings), Manhattan (New York), Queens, Staten Island (Richmond)$10,000NYC Civil Court Act §1801
City CourtsCities outside NYC, such as Buffalo, Rochester, Syracuse, Albany, Yonkers$5,000Uniform City Court Act §1801
District CourtsNassau County and western Suffolk County$5,000Uniform District Court Act §1801
Town and Village Justice CourtsTowns and villages statewide$3,000Uniform Justice Court Act §1801

The limits are exclusive of interest and costs and apply to claims for money only. If your claim is higher than the limit for the court where the defendant can be sued, you can reduce it and give up the rest, or file a regular civil case in the same court or in a higher court.

Where to file: generally, the defendant must live, work or have a business office in the area the court covers. In New York City, that means one of the five county branches of Civil Court. Outside the city, it is the city, district, town or village court for the defendant’s location.

Filing Fees: $15 or $20

New York’s small claims fees are among the lowest in the country. Under NYC Civil Court Act §1803, the fee is:

  • $15 for claims of $1,000 or less; and
  • $20 for claims over $1,000.

The fee covers the clerk’s mailing of the notice of claim to the defendant by first-class mail and certified mail, return receipt requested. You usually do not need to hire a process server unless the mailing fails. The same $15/$20 structure applies in city, district, town and village courts. If you cannot afford the fee, ask about a fee waiver.

Who Can Sue: Individuals Only (Businesses Use Commercial Claims)

New York’s small claims part is for individuals. NYC Civil Court Act §1809 says that no corporation (other than certain public entities), partnership, association or assignee of a claim may start a small claims action. A business can be sued in small claims, but it cannot be the plaintiff there.

Businesses use the commercial claims part instead, which has the same dollar limit as small claims in that court. The main differences (NYC Civil Court Act §§1801-A and 1803-A):

  • The claimant must be a corporation, partnership or association with its principal office in New York State.
  • The filing fee is $25 plus the cost of mailing.
  • A business may file no more than five commercial claims in a calendar month.
  • For a claim arising from a consumer transaction, the business must mail a demand letter to the consumer at least 10 and no more than 180 days before filing.

Sole proprietors suing in their own name can generally use regular small claims.

How to File a New York Small Claims Case, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. New York generally allows six years for contract claims and three years for property damage; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Find the right court. Use the table above and the defendant’s home or business address.
  4. Get the defendant’s exact name and address. For a business, use its legal name from the New York Department of State corporation database, and its trade name if different. A wrong name can make a judgment hard to collect.
  5. File the claim. File in person at the small claims clerk’s office or, in many courts, online. Pay the $15 or $20 fee.
  6. Let the clerk mail the notice. The court sends the notice of claim by first-class and certified mail and gives you a hearing date.
  7. Prepare your evidence. Receipts, contracts, photos, texts and estimates, in date order. For damage claims, New York courts commonly accept two itemized estimates or one paid receipt as proof of the amount.
  8. Attend the hearing. Arrive early, answer the calendar call and be ready to choose between a judge and an arbitrator.

At the Hearing: Judge or Arbitrator?

In New York City Civil Court, small claims are often heard in evening sessions, and when your case is called you will usually be offered two options:

  • An arbitrator. A volunteer attorney hears the case, often the same night. It is faster, but the decision is final: there is no appeal from an arbitrator’s award.
  • A judge. You may wait longer or be given a later date, but the decision can be appealed on the limited grounds described below.

Either way, the hearing is informal. Bring three copies of each document and any witnesses. Speak briefly, lead with the amount you are owed, and show how you calculated it.

If the defendant does not appear, the court can grant a default judgment. If you do not appear, your claim can be dismissed.

Appeals: The “Substantial Justice” Rule

Under NYC Civil Court Act §1807, a person who starts a small claims case is deemed to have waived the right to appeal, except that either party may appeal on the sole ground that “substantial justice has not been done” according to the rules of substantive law. In practice, appellate courts rarely overturn small claims judgments.

Appeals go to the Appellate Term and must generally be filed within 30 days. You will need a transcript or recording of the hearing, which has its own cost. A decision by an arbitrator cannot be appealed at all.

Collecting a New York Judgment

If the defendant does not pay, New York offers strong collection tools, including two special rules for business debtors:

  • Information subpoena. Ask the debtor, their bank or employer, under oath, about assets and income.
  • Income execution. Through a sheriff or city marshal, you can collect from wages. Under CPLR §5231, the withholding cannot exceed 10% of gross income or 25% of disposable earnings, and nothing is taken if weekly disposable earnings are below 30 times the minimum wage.
  • Property execution and bank restraint. A sheriff or marshal can levy non-exempt bank funds and property.
  • Treble damages against businesses. Under NYC Civil Court Act §1812, if a business debtor has at least two other unsatisfied small claims judgments from the same trade or business and fails to pay within 30 days of notice, you can sue for three times the unpaid judgment.

Interest. Judgments accrue interest at 9% a year, but only 2% in actions arising from a consumer debt where a natural person is the defendant (CPLR §5004).

Common New York Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. New York landlords must return a deposit or give an itemized statement within 14 days after the tenant leaves. Our security deposit demand letter is a good first step.
  • Contractor disputes. Bring the contract, proof of payment, photos and estimates to finish or repair the work.
  • Unpaid personal loans. Written proof such as a promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Consumer purchases. Receipts and communications showing the defect and the seller’s refusal to refund.

How New York Compares

New York City’s $10,000 limit matches the most common limit nationally, but the $3,000 town and village limit is among the lowest anywhere. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in New York?

$10,000 in New York City Civil Court, $5,000 in city courts outside NYC and in the Nassau and Suffolk district courts, and $3,000 in town and village courts. The limits exclude interest and costs.

How much does it cost to file small claims in New York?

$15 for claims of $1,000 or less and $20 for larger claims. The fee includes the clerk mailing the notice of claim to the defendant by first-class and certified mail.

Can a business sue in New York small claims court?

No. Corporations, partnerships, associations and assignees cannot start small claims cases. A business with a New York office uses the commercial claims part instead, for a $25 fee plus mailing costs, and can file up to five commercial claims a month.

Should I choose a judge or an arbitrator?

An arbitrator usually hears the case faster, often the same evening, but the decision cannot be appealed. A judge may take longer, but the decision can be appealed on the limited ground that substantial justice was not done.

Can I appeal a New York small claims decision?

Only on the ground that substantial justice was not done under the substantive law, and only if a judge (not an arbitrator) decided the case. Appeals are generally due within 30 days.

What interest does a New York small claims judgment earn?

9% a year in most cases, but 2% a year in actions arising from a consumer debt where the defendant is an individual (CPLR §5004).
