Maryland’s District Court handles civil cases up to $30,000, but claims of $5,000 or less get the simplest procedure. Under Maryland Rule 3-701, small claims trials are informal, the rules of evidence are relaxed, and there is no pretrial discovery. The court provides the forms, and the clerk can mail the complaint to the defendant for you.

This guide covers the Maryland small claims limit, the 2026 cost schedule, service, the notice of intention to defend, the trial, appeals and collection. Rules link to the Maryland Courts, the Maryland Rules and the Maryland Code, checked in September 2026.

The Maryland Limit: $5,000

A small claim in Maryland is a District Court civil case where the amount claimed does not exceed $5,000, exclusive of interest, costs and attorney fees if allowed by law. Claims above $5,000 up to the District Court’s general civil limit are “large claims” in the same court, with more formal procedures, including discovery. The Maryland People’s Law Library explains the difference in plain language.

If your claim is slightly over $5,000, you can reduce it to $5,000 to use the small claims procedure, but you give up the difference.

The filing fee includes state surcharges that fund legal services and land records. In Baltimore County, constables serve civil process; elsewhere the sheriff does. If you cannot afford the costs, you can ask the court to waive prepayment. If you win, the costs are added to the judgment.

How to File a Maryland Small Claim, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Maryland generally allows only three years for most civil claims, including contract claims; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Choose the right District Court location. Usually where the defendant lives, works or carries on business, or where the claim arose.
  4. Complete the Complaint/Application and Affidavit in Support of Judgment. This is the District Court’s standard civil complaint form. Attach copies of key documents. Use the defendant’s exact legal name and address.
  5. File and pay. Pay the $44 filing fee and service costs. Many courts accept filings through MDEC (Maryland Electronic Courts).
  6. Service. Choose sheriff or constable service ($60) or certified mail by the clerk ($20), or use a private process server.
  7. Watch for a Notice of Intention to Defend. The defendant is told to file one if they plan to contest the claim. If no notice is filed, you may be able to get a judgment on your affidavit.
  8. Attend the trial. Bring documents and witnesses.

At Trial

Under Maryland Rule 3-701, small claims trials are informal, and the formal rules of evidence do not strictly apply. There is no discovery, so you cannot use interrogatories or depositions to get documents from the other side before trial. That means the evidence you bring on the trial date is what the judge will see. Tips:

  • Open with the amount and the reason in one or two sentences.
  • Bring originals and copies of contracts, invoices, receipts, photos and messages.
  • Bring witnesses in person, or ask the clerk about subpoenas in advance.
  • Show how you calculated the amount.

Lawyers are allowed in Maryland small claims, and many individuals represent themselves. Many District Court locations offer free day-of-trial mediation through the court’s ADR program.

Appeals: A New Trial in Circuit Court

Either party can appeal a small claims judgment to the circuit court by filing a notice of appeal in the District Court within 30 days after the judgment. In small claims appeals, the circuit court hears the case de novo, as a brand-new trial. There is an appeal filing fee, and the circuit court process is more formal than the District Court trial.

Collecting a Maryland Judgment

If the defendant does not pay, you can use the District Court’s post-judgment tools:

  • Oral examination in aid of enforcement. The debtor must appear and answer questions under oath about income and assets.
  • Wage garnishment. The exempt amount is the greater of 75% of disposable wages or 30 times the Maryland minimum hourly wage for each week the wages were earned (Md. Code, Com. Law §15-601.1).
  • Garnishment of bank accounts ($10 request) and writs of execution on non-exempt property ($10 plus service).
  • Judgment lien. A District Court judgment can be recorded in circuit court to create a lien on the debtor’s real estate in that county.

Interest. The legal rate of interest on a judgment is 10% a year; judgments for rent of residential premises carry 6% (Md. Code, Cts. & Jud. Proc. §11-107).

Small Claim or Large Claim?

The same District Court hears both kinds of cases, so the choice is about procedure. A small claim (up to $5,000) is quicker and simpler: no discovery, informal trial, relaxed evidence rules. A large claim allows discovery, which can help if the other side holds records you need, such as a contractor’s invoices or a landlord’s repair receipts, but it adds time and formality. If your claim is modestly above $5,000, weigh whether the extra money is worth a more formal case. If it is well above $5,000, a large claim in District Court is usually the better route.

Suing or Defending as a Business

Businesses file many District Court cases in Maryland, especially landlords, medical providers and contractors. Name the business exactly as registered with the Maryland State Department of Assessments and Taxation (SDAT), and serve its resident agent. If you are defending a claim against your business, file a Notice of Intention to Defend on time and bring the person who handled the transaction, with the contract, invoices and payment records. Maryland rules on who may represent a business in court are stricter than in some states, so check with the clerk or the Maryland Courts Help Centers before sending a non-lawyer employee to argue the case.

Before You File: Can You Collect?

Maryland’s 75% wage exemption means that on $800 a week of disposable wages, about $200 a week could be garnished, so a $4,000 judgment would take roughly five months to collect from wages alone, plus interest and costs. That assumes the debtor has a steady job you can identify. Before filing, write down what you know about the defendant’s employer, bank and property. The Maryland Courts Help Centers offer free help by phone and chat if you have questions about forms or collection.

Common Maryland Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Maryland landlords must return the deposit, with interest, within 45 days after the tenancy ends, or send an itemized list of damages within the same period. A landlord who withholds a deposit without a reasonable basis can owe up to three times the amount withheld plus reasonable attorney fees. Interest accrues at the greater of 1.5% a year or the one-year U.S. Treasury yield rate set at the start of each year (Md. Code, Real Prop. §8-203). Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Medical debt. The cost schedule has a separate small claims category for medical debt suits.
  • Personal loans. A promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Contractor disputes. Contracts, payments, photos and a second estimate.

Common Mistakes in Maryland Small Claims

  • Waiting too long. Maryland’s general three-year limit is shorter than many states.
  • Expecting discovery. There is none in small claims. Gather your evidence before you file.
  • Choosing the wrong service method. Certified mail is cheaper, but if the defendant does not sign for it, you will need sheriff or private service.
  • Using a trade name only. Look up the business’s legal name and resident agent on SDAT before filing.
  • Forgetting interest on a deposit claim. In a security deposit case, the landlord also owes interest at the statutory rate.
  • Leaving witnesses at home. Because there is no discovery, live testimony on the trial date often decides close cases.
  • Missing the 30-day appeal window. It runs from the judgment date.

How Maryland Compares

Maryland’s $5,000 small claims limit is among the lowest in the country, matching Virginia and New Jersey, though its District Court also handles larger claims with slightly more formal rules. Neighbor Pennsylvania allows $12,000. See every state in our small claims court limits by state table.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Maryland?

$5,000, exclusive of interest and costs. Larger claims can be filed in District Court as large claims.

How much does it cost to file small claims in Maryland?

$44 to file, plus $60 per defendant for sheriff service or $20 for certified mail by the clerk, under the District Court cost schedule revised in March 2026.

Is there discovery in Maryland small claims?

No. Maryland Rule 3-701 does not allow discovery in small claims cases, and trials are informal.

How do I appeal a Maryland small claims judgment?

File a notice of appeal in the District Court within 30 days. The circuit court hears the case again from the beginning.

How much of my wages can be garnished in Maryland?

The exempt amount is the greater of 75% of disposable wages or 30 times the Maryland minimum hourly wage per week.

What interest does a Maryland judgment earn?

10% a year, or 6% for judgments for residential rent.
