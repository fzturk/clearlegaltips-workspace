The short version (2026): Michigan small claims court hears claims for money up to $7,000 (since January 1, 2024). Total filing costs are $30, $50 or $70 by claim size, plus mailing. Lawyers and collection agencies cannot take part, and you may file no more than five claims a week in one district. If a magistrate decides your case, either side can appeal to a judge within 7 days; if a judge decides it, there is no appeal.

Michigan’s small claims division is one of the most “do it yourself” systems in the country. By law, lawyers cannot represent either side, juries are not available, and the judgment is usually final. In exchange, the process is quick and cheap, and the clerk mails the claim for you.

Because you give up so many rights by staying in small claims, Michigan also lets either side move the case to the regular civil division before the hearing. This guide explains the Michigan small claims limit, fees, who can file, how removal and appeals work, and how to collect. Rules link to the Revised Judicature Act (MCL chapter 84) and Michigan court sources, checked in September 2026.

The Michigan Small Claims Limit: $7,000

MCL 600.8401 sets the limit in steps. The current figure, $7,000, has applied since January 1, 2024:

Effective dateLimit
September 1, 2012$5,000
January 1, 2015$5,500
January 1, 2018$6,000
January 1, 2021$6,500
January 1, 2024$7,000

The statute does not schedule any further increase, so $7,000 remains the limit until the Legislature acts again. Small claims hears cases for the recovery of money only. If your claim is larger, you can reduce it to $7,000 and give up the difference (staying in small claims waives any right to recover more, under MCL 600.8412), or file in the district court’s general civil division.

Filing Fees

The filing fee under MCL 600.8420 depends on the amount you claim, and courts also collect a $5 electronic filing system fee on small claims actions, according to the State Court Administrative Office’s district court fee table:

Amount claimedStatutory feeWith $5 system fee
$600 or less$25$30
$600.01 to $1,750$45$50
Over $1,750$65$70

You also pay the postage for the clerk to mail the claim to each defendant, and $15 for each writ of garnishment, execution or judgment debtor discovery subpoena after judgment. If you cannot afford the fees, ask the clerk for a fee waiver form. When you win, the judgment normally includes your costs.

Who Can File, and Who Cannot Take Part

Michigan’s rules on participation are strict:

  • No lawyers. Under MCL 600.8408(1), an attorney cannot take part in filing, prosecuting or defending a small claims case except on the attorney’s own behalf.
  • No collection agencies or assignees. Collection agencies and their employees cannot participate, and a claim cannot be filed by an assignee or a third-party beneficiary (MCL 600.8407(1)).
  • Businesses appear through people with personal knowledge. A sole proprietorship, partnership or corporation is represented by an officer or employee with direct and personal knowledge of the facts in dispute (MCL 600.8408(2)).
  • Five claims a week. A person may not file more than five small claims in one week in the same district court district (MCL 600.8407(2)).

Your Right to Leave Small Claims Before the Hearing

Staying in small claims means giving up the right to a lawyer, a jury, any recovery above $7,000 and, usually, any appeal (MCL 600.8412). Because of that, MCL 600.8408(4) gives both sides two choices before the trial starts:

  • demand that the case be heard by a district court judge instead of a magistrate; or
  • remove the case to the district court’s general civil division, where lawyers, juries and appeals are available.

A defendant with a larger claim against the plaintiff can also file a separate lawsuit in the proper court and ask for the small claims case to be transferred and tried together with it (MCL 600.8423). Once the small claims trial begins, these options are gone.

How to File a Michigan Small Claims Case, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Michigan generally allows six years for contract claims; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Find the right district court. File in the district where the defendant lives or does business, or where the claim arose.
  4. Complete the Affidavit and Claim. The small claims form is an affidavit you sign under oath. Give your full and correct name, say whether you are an individual or a business, and list any assumed name used when the claim arose (MCL 600.8402). File one copy for each defendant.
  5. Pay the fee. $30, $50 or $70 including the system fee, plus postage.
  6. Service. The clerk mails the claim to each defendant, and the form tells them when to appear. If mail service fails, the court will tell you your options.
  7. Prepare your evidence. Receipts, contracts, photos, texts and repair estimates in date order, with copies for the other side and the court.
  8. Attend the hearing. Arrive early. Bring witnesses in person if you can.

At the Hearing

A small claims hearing may be conducted by a district court judge or by a district court magistrate who is a licensed Michigan attorney (MCL 600.8427). There is no jury and no lawyer for either side, so the hearing is conversational:

  • Start with the amount and the reason in one or two sentences.
  • Walk through your documents in date order.
  • Show how you calculated the amount with invoices, receipts or estimates.
  • Answer the magistrate’s or judge’s questions directly.

The decision is often announced at the end of the hearing.

Appeals: Seven Days, and Only From a Magistrate

Michigan’s appeal rule is short and strict (MCL 600.8427):

  • If a magistrate decided the case, either party may take an appeal de novo (a new hearing) to the small claims division before a district court judge. The appeal must be taken within 7 days after the magistrate’s decision is entered.
  • If a judge decided the case, whether originally or on appeal from a magistrate, no further appeal is available to either party.

If you want full appeal rights, the time to act is before the hearing, by removing the case to the general civil division.

Collecting a Michigan Judgment

The court’s judgment usually gives the defendant a short time to pay. If they do not:

  • Judgment debtor discovery subpoena. Require the debtor to appear and answer questions about income and assets ($15 fee).
  • Garnishment. A periodic garnishment reaches wages, and a non-periodic garnishment reaches bank accounts or other money owed to the debtor, subject to exemptions ($15 per writ).
  • Writ of execution. Lets a court officer or sheriff seize and sell non-exempt property ($15).
  • Judgment lien. Recording a judgment lien with the county register of deeds reaches the debtor’s real estate.

Interest. Michigan judgment interest resets every six months. For most civil judgments it equals 1 percentage point plus the average yield on five-year U.S. Treasury notes certified by the State Treasurer (MCL 600.6013). The certified figure for the period starting July 1, 2026 is 3.959%, which makes the typical judgment rate about 4.96% a year.

Common Michigan Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. A Michigan landlord must mail an itemized list of damages within 30 days after the tenancy ends, and the tenant must provide a forwarding address within 4 days of moving out. Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Contractor disputes. Bring the contract, payments, photos and a second contractor’s estimate.
  • Personal loans. Written proof such as a promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Vehicle and property damage. Repair estimates, photos and any police report.

Common Mistakes in Michigan Small Claims

  • Waiting until the hearing to think about removal. Once the small claims trial starts, you have waived your right to a lawyer, a jury and (if a judge hears it) any appeal. Decide beforehand whether small claims is the right place.
  • Sending the wrong person for a business. A company must be represented by an officer or employee with direct, personal knowledge of the facts. A manager who only read the file may be turned away.
  • Using the wrong name. The affidavit asks for the full and correct legal name and any assumed name in use when the claim arose. A judgment against a business’s trade name alone can be hard to enforce.
  • Missing the 7-day appeal. The window to appeal a magistrate’s decision is only seven days after the decision is entered. Ask the clerk for the appeal form the same day if you plan to use it.
  • Filing an assigned claim. If you bought the debt or someone assigned it to you, small claims will not accept it; use the general civil division.
  • Forgetting post-judgment costs. Each writ of garnishment or execution costs $15, and the fee can be added to what the debtor owes, so keep records of every payment.

How Michigan Compares

Michigan’s $7,000 limit sits just above neighboring Ohio ($6,000) and below Indiana ($10,000) and Wisconsin ($10,000, rising to $15,000 in 2027). Its combination of no lawyers, no collection agencies and no appeal from a judge’s decision is among the strictest in the country. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Michigan?

$7,000, effective January 1, 2024 (MCL 600.8401). No further increase is currently scheduled in the statute.

How much does it cost to file small claims in Michigan?

$30 for claims up to $600, $50 up to $1,750 and $70 above that, including the $5 electronic filing system fee, plus postage for mailing the claim to each defendant.

Can I bring a lawyer to Michigan small claims court?

No. Attorneys cannot take part except on their own behalf. If you want a lawyer, either side can remove the case to the district court’s general civil division before the hearing starts.

Can I appeal a Michigan small claims decision?

Only if a magistrate heard the case. Either side can then appeal to a district judge within 7 days for a new hearing. A judge’s decision cannot be appealed.

How many small claims can I file in Michigan?

No more than five claims in one week in the same district court district (20 for claims filed on behalf of a local government).

Can a collection agency sue in Michigan small claims court?

No. Collection agencies and assignees of a claim cannot file or take part in small claims cases.
