The short version (2026): Small claims court limits by state run from $2,500 in Kentucky to $25,000 in Delaware and Tennessee, and the most common limit is $10,000. Ten states raised their limit between 2024 and 2026 (Idaho tripled to $15,000 on July 1, 2026), and Wisconsin goes to $15,000 on January 1, 2027. Statewide filing fees range from $10 in Wyoming to $300 for the largest Florida claims, and some county-set fees run higher ($379 in Cook County, Illinois).

Small claims court is where ordinary people collect ordinary debts: an unpaid invoice, a security deposit a landlord kept, a contractor who took the money and left, a friend who never repaid a loan. You do not need a lawyer, the forms are short, and a hearing usually takes minutes. The one rule you cannot work around is the dollar limit. Every state sets its own, and if your claim is bigger, you either give up the difference or file in a more formal court.

This page lists the small claims court limits and filing fees for all 50 states and the District of Columbia, checked against each state’s statute or official court website in September 2026. Use the estimator below for a quick answer, then read the table and the step-by-step guide.

Key Findings From the 2026 Data

Lining up all 51 jurisdictions shows how much the answer depends on where you live. A $12,000 claim is a simple small claims case in Georgia or Pennsylvania and too big for small claims in 39 other jurisdictions.

  • $10,000 is the most common limit. Sixteen jurisdictions use it, and it is also the middle value: half the states allow less and half allow more.
  • Twelve states cap claims at $5,000 or less. Kentucky ($2,500) and Mississippi ($3,500) are the lowest; ten others, including Maryland, New Jersey, Virginia and Missouri, stop at $5,000.
  • Nine states allow $15,000 or more. Delaware and Tennessee lead at $25,000, followed by Minnesota, Texas, Utah and West Virginia at $20,000 and Georgia, Idaho and North Dakota at $15,000.
  • Ten states raised the limit in the last three years. Kansas, Minnesota, California and Michigan in 2024; Utah, Nebraska, Arizona and West Virginia in 2025; Maine and Idaho in 2026. Charts published before these dates are now wrong for those states.
  • Businesses sometimes get a lower cap. In California a business can sue for only $6,250 and in Washington $5,000, half of what an individual may claim.
  • Some claims have their own limit. Hawaii has no cap on residential security-deposit claims, Connecticut allows $15,000 on home-improvement contracts, and Minnesota and Vermont set lower caps for consumer-credit debt.

What Changed in 2024–2026

Small claims limits used to change once a generation. Since 2024 they have been moving quickly, mostly because inflation made the old caps too small for everyday disputes like car repairs and rent deposits. Here is every change we found, with the source.

  • Idaho: $5,000 → $15,000 (July 1, 2026). Senate Bill 1330 amended Idaho Code §1-2301 with an emergency clause. It is the largest jump in the country.
  • Maine: $6,000 → $10,000 (January 1, 2026). Public Law 2025, chapter 261 amended 14 M.R.S. §7482.
  • West Virginia: $10,000 → $20,000 (2025). House Bill 2761 doubled magistrate court civil jurisdiction under W. Va. Code §50-2-1.
  • Arizona: $3,500 → $5,000 (2025). Senate Bill 1022 (Laws 2025, chapter 94) raised the justice court small claims limit in A.R.S. §22-503.
  • Nebraska: $3,900 → $7,500 (July 1, 2025). Nebraska adjusts its limit every five years.
  • Utah: $15,000 → $20,000 (January 1, 2025). Utah Code §78A-8-102 sets the $20,000 figure through December 31, 2029.
  • Minnesota: $15,000 → $20,000 (August 1, 2024). Conciliation court limits in Minn. Stat. §491A.01; consumer-credit claims stay at $4,000.
  • Kansas: $4,000 → $10,000 (July 1, 2024). K.S.A. §61-2703, amended by Laws 2024, chapter 22.
  • California and Michigan (2024). California’s limit for individuals is $12,500, and Michigan’s went from $6,500 to $7,000.
  • Coming next: Wisconsin to $15,000 (January 1, 2027). 2025 Wisconsin Act 105 raises the general limit in Wis. Stat. §799.01 from $10,000; tort and personal-injury claims stay at $5,000.

Proposals that have not become law are just as important, because some websites report them as done. Virginia’s House Bill 364 (2026) would have created a larger small claims warrant, but Va. Code §16.1-122.2 still reads $5,000. South Carolina’s Senate Bill 251 would double magistrate jurisdiction to $15,000, and it is still in committee. Until a bill is signed and in effect, use the figure in the table.

How We Verified This Data

Most small claims charts online are copied from other charts, and old figures travel for years. We started from scratch:

  1. Primary source for every row. Each limit was checked against the state statute or court rule (for example, Ohio Rev. Code §1925.02 or Illinois Supreme Court Rule 281), or against the state judiciary’s own small claims page when the statute site could not be reached.
  2. Recent-change search. For every state we looked for 2024–2026 legislation and confirmed the effective date in the enrolled bill or the statute’s history note. That is how the Idaho, Maine and West Virginia increases were caught.
  3. Fees only from official sources. A fee appears in the table only when a statute, the state court system or the clerk publishes it statewide. Where each county sets its own fee, the table says so rather than guessing.
  4. Dated and linked. Every row carries a verification date (September 2026) and a link to its source, and the CSV download includes both so you can check any figure yourself.

What You Can Sue For in Small Claims Court

Small claims courts decide disputes about money, and in some states the return of specific personal property. Typical cases include:

Case typeExamples
Unpaid debtsPersonal loans, unpaid invoices, bounced checks
Security depositsA landlord kept all or part of a deposit without a valid itemized reason
Breach of contractServices paid for but not performed, goods never delivered
Property damageCar accidents (property damage only), damage by a neighbor or tenant
Poor workmanshipA contractor who left a job unfinished or did it badly
Defective productsA purchase that failed and the seller refused a refund

What these cases share is a definite amount: you can point to a receipt, a contract, an invoice or a repair estimate and say, “this is what I am owed.” A signed promissory note or written agreement makes a debt case much easier to prove.

Small claims courts generally cannot order someone to do something (finish a job, stop a nuisance), cannot grant a divorce or decide custody, and usually do not hear defamation, bankruptcy or claims against the federal government. Many states also exclude evictions from small claims or send them to a separate docket, although a few, such as Tennessee’s general sessions courts, hear evictions with no dollar limit.

How to File a Small Claims Case, Step by Step

The details vary by state, but the process follows the same pattern almost everywhere.

  1. Send a demand letter first. Many disputes end here, and several states expect you to ask for payment before you sue. A clear demand letter for payment states the amount, the reason and a deadline, and it becomes evidence if you go to court.
  2. Check the deadline. Every claim has a statute of limitations, often three to six years for written contracts. Our statute of limitations on debt by state table lists them.
  3. Confirm the limit and the right court. Use the table above. If your claim is over the limit, decide now whether to reduce it (and give up the rest) or file in regular civil court.
  4. Pick the right county. You usually file where the defendant lives or does business, or where the contract was signed or the damage happened. Filing in the wrong place can get your case dismissed.
  5. Name the defendant correctly. Use the person’s full legal name, or a business’s exact registered name from the Secretary of State’s business search. A judgment against the wrong name is hard to collect.
  6. Fill out the claim form and pay the fee. Most states now accept online filing. If you cannot afford the fee, ask for a fee waiver form.
  7. Serve the defendant. The defendant must be formally notified: by certified mail sent by the clerk, a sheriff or constable, or a private process server, depending on the state. You cannot hand the papers over yourself in most states.
  8. Prepare your evidence. Bring three copies of everything: contracts, receipts, photos, texts, emails, repair estimates and your demand letter. Put them in date order.
  9. Go to the hearing. Arrive early, be brief, and let your documents do the work. Many courts offer free mediation the same day, and a settlement there is binding.
  10. Collect the judgment. Winning is not the same as being paid. See the collection section below.

Start with a demand letter. A firm, professional letter that states the amount and a deadline resolves many disputes before anyone files, and it shows the judge you tried. LawDepot’s guided payment demand letter takes about ten minutes.

How Much Does Small Claims Court Really Cost?

The filing fee is only the first cost. A realistic budget has four parts:

  • Filing fee. Statewide fees run from $10 in Wyoming to $300 for a Florida claim over $2,500, and county-set fees can be higher: Cook County, Illinois charges $287 for claims up to $2,500 and $379 above that. Several states tie the fee to the claim size: California charges $30, $50 or $75; New Hampshire $125 or $180; Massachusetts $40 to $150. Iowa charges a flat $115 and Connecticut $95.
  • Service of process. Each defendant must be served. Certified mail through the clerk is usually cheapest; a sheriff, constable or process server costs more. North Carolina’s judicial branch, for example, lists sheriff service at $30 per defendant.
  • Your time. Expect at least one weekday morning at the courthouse, sometimes two if the case is continued.
  • Collection costs. If the other side does not pay voluntarily, writs of execution, wage garnishments and bank levies each carry their own fees.

The good news: in most states, if you win, the judge adds your filing and service fees to the judgment, so the defendant ends up paying them. Keep every receipt.

Can You Bring a Lawyer?

Rules differ more than most people expect. Most states allow lawyers but do not require them. A handful keep them out so the hearing stays informal:

  • No lawyers at the hearing: California, Idaho, Michigan and Nebraska.
  • Only with permission or agreement: Arizona (written consent of all parties), Kansas (leave of court, or if the other side has a lawyer), Oregon and Washington (the judge’s permission).

Even where lawyers are allowed, few people use one; legal fees would often exceed the amount at stake. Businesses are a special case: some states require a corporation to appear through an officer or employee, and others require a lawyer. Check your state’s spoke guide or the court’s self-help page before the hearing.

What Happens at the Hearing

A small claims hearing is short and informal. The judge, magistrate or commissioner will usually ask the plaintiff to explain the claim first, then hear the defendant, then ask questions. Formal rules of evidence are relaxed in most states, so written statements and photos are normally accepted.

Three habits make a strong impression:

  • Lead with the number. Start with what you are owed and why, in two or three sentences, before walking through the documents.
  • Stick to facts you can show. “Here is the signed estimate, here is the canceled check, here are photos of the unfinished deck” is more persuasive than how the dispute made you feel.
  • Be polite to the other side. Judges notice who stays calm.

Some courts announce a decision on the spot; others mail it within a few weeks. If the defendant does not show up, you will usually receive a default judgment, but you still need to prove the amount.

Can You Appeal a Small Claims Decision?

Appeal rights vary widely, and they sometimes depend on which side you were on:

  • No appeal at all. In Arizona, A.R.S. §22-519 makes the small claims judgment final and not appealable; anyone who wants appeal rights has to use the regular justice court civil docket instead. Michigan works the same way when a judge decides the case.
  • Defendant only. In California, the plaintiff gives up the right to appeal by choosing small claims court, but the defendant can appeal for a new hearing in superior court.
  • New trial in a higher court. Many states, including Texas, Pennsylvania and North Carolina, allow either side to appeal to a higher trial court, which hears the case from the beginning.

Appeal deadlines are short, often 10 to 30 days, so ask the clerk about your options the day you receive the decision.

Collecting Your Judgment: The Part Nobody Warns You About

The court does not collect the money for you. If the defendant does not pay after the judgment becomes final, you have to use the collection tools your state provides:

  • Ask first. Send a short letter with a copy of the judgment and a payment deadline. Many people pay once a judgment is on the record.
  • Debtor examination. You can ask the court to order the debtor to answer questions, under oath, about income, bank accounts and property.
  • Wage garnishment. A portion of the debtor’s paycheck is sent to you. Federal law caps ordinary garnishment at 25% of disposable earnings, and several states protect more.
  • Bank levy. The sheriff or marshal can freeze and seize funds in the debtor’s account, subject to exemptions.
  • Property lien. Recording the judgment creates a lien on real estate in many states, so it must be paid when the property is sold or refinanced.

Judgments also earn interest at a rate set by state law, and most can be renewed if they are not paid within the original period. Our guide on how to collect money owed covers the options before and after court.

If your claim is close to or above your state’s limit, compare the two routes:

For a claim slightly over the limit, giving up a few hundred dollars is usually cheaper than hiring a lawyer for regular court. For a claim far over the limit, or one where you need the other side to do something rather than pay, regular court is the right place.

Should You Settle Before the Hearing?

Often, yes. A settlement is guaranteed money, while a judgment still has to be collected. If the defendant offers a reasonable amount, especially paid in full or on a short, written schedule, it can be the better outcome. Put any settlement in writing, signed by both sides, and ask the court to enter it as a judgment or dismiss the case only after payment clears. Many courts offer free mediators on the hearing day for exactly this reason.

Common Small Claims Mistakes

  • Using an outdated limit. Ten states changed their limit since 2024. Check the source link in the table before you file.
  • Splitting a large claim. Filing two cases for $5,000 each to get around a $5,000 limit is not allowed, and the court can dismiss both.
  • Suing the wrong party. Sue the business entity that signed the contract, not just the person who ran it, unless that person also guaranteed the debt.
  • Missing the deadline. The statute of limitations runs from the breach or the last payment, not from when you decided to sue.
  • Bringing no proof of the amount. Repair estimates, invoices and receipts are what turn a complaint into a number the judge can award.
  • Stopping at the judgment. Plan your collection steps before the hearing: where the defendant works, banks and owns property.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I sue for in small claims court?

It depends on the state, from $2,500 in Kentucky to $25,000 in Delaware and Tennessee. The most common limit is $10,000. Some states set different figures for businesses or for particular kinds of claims, so check the “Other limits” column in the table.

Which states raised their small claims limit recently?

Ten states raised their limit between 2024 and 2026: Kansas, Minnesota, California, Michigan, Utah, Nebraska, Arizona, West Virginia, Maine and Idaho. Wisconsin’s limit rises from $10,000 to $15,000 on January 1, 2027.

Can I sue for more than the limit if I give up the rest?

Yes, in most states. You can reduce your claim to the limit and file in small claims court, but you permanently give up the amount over the limit. You cannot split one claim into several smaller cases.

Do I need a lawyer for small claims court?

No. Small claims court is designed for people representing themselves. Some states, including California, Idaho, Michigan and Nebraska, do not allow lawyers at the hearing at all, and others allow them only with permission.

How much does it cost to file a small claims case?

Statewide filing fees run from $10 in Wyoming to $300 for larger Florida claims, and county-set fees can be higher, such as $379 in Cook County, Illinois. Many states charge more for bigger claims. Add the cost of serving each defendant. If you win, the court usually adds these costs to the judgment. Fee waivers are available if you cannot afford to pay.

How long does a small claims case take?

Most cases are heard within one to three months of filing, and the hearing itself often lasts 10 to 20 minutes. Collection can take longer if the other side does not pay voluntarily.

What happens if I win but the other person won’t pay?

You use your state’s collection tools: a debtor examination, wage garnishment, bank levy or a lien on property. The court does not collect for you, but it issues the orders that let a sheriff, marshal or employer take the money.

Can I appeal a small claims decision?

Sometimes. Some states allow either side to appeal for a new trial, some allow only the defendant to appeal, and a few, such as Arizona, allow no appeal from small claims at all. Appeal deadlines are short, often 10 to 30 days.

The Bottom Line

Small claims court is the fastest, cheapest way to recover a modest debt, but only if you file in the right court for the right amount. Confirm your state’s current limit in the table (ten states changed theirs since 2024), send a demand letter first, bring your documents in order, and plan how you will collect before the hearing.
