The short version (2026): Virginia small claims court is a division of the general district court that hears claims up to $5,000 (Va. Code §16.1-122.2). No lawyers may represent either side, though a business can send an owner, officer or employee. The trial happens on the first return date. The defendant can move the case to regular general district court any time before the decision, and either side can appeal within 10 days for a new trial in circuit court. A 2026 bill to raise the limit (HB 364) did not become law.

Virginia’s small claims court is designed for people who want to handle a dispute entirely on their own. Lawyers are not allowed to represent anyone, the judge runs the trial informally, and the case is decided on the first court date. The trade-off is a low $5,000 limit, and a defendant who can move the case into regular court if they want a lawyer.

This guide covers the Virginia small claims limit, who can appear, how to file a small claims civil warrant, the trial, appeals and collection. Rules link to Article 5 of Chapter 6, Title 16.1 of the Code of Virginia and the Virginia Judicial System, checked in September 2026.

The Virginia Small Claims Limit: $5,000

Under Va. Code §16.1-122.2, the small claims court has jurisdiction, concurrent with the general district court, over civil actions for money or specific personal property when the amount claimed does not exceed $5,000, exclusive of interest. The limit has not changed since 2006. Small claims courts cannot hear suits against the Commonwealth under the Virginia Tort Claims Act or claims against state officers or employees for their official duties (§16.1-122.1).

A note on 2026 legislation. House Bill 364 (2026 session) proposed letting plaintiffs file small claims of up to $25,000, twice a year. Some websites report it as passed. As of September 26, 2026, the Code of Virginia still reads $5,000, so use that figure.

If your claim is over $5,000, you can reduce it and give up the difference, or file a regular warrant in debt in general district court, which hears most civil claims up to $25,000 (and up to $50,000 for personal injury and wrongful death). Lawyers can appear there.

No Lawyers: Who Can Appear

Virginia’s rule is strict. Under §16.1-122.4(A), all parties represent themselves, with two exceptions:

  • A corporation, partnership, LLC or other entity may be represented by an owner, general partner, officer, member or employee, who can plead and try the case without a lawyer. An attorney may appear only on their own behalf, not as a representative.
  • A party who, in the judge’s opinion, cannot understand or participate on their own may be represented by a friend or relative who knows the facts and is not a lawyer.

The defendant also has a powerful option: under §16.1-122.4(B), the defendant may remove the case to the general district court at any time before the judge announces a decision, and may hire a lawyer for that purpose. If that happens, the case continues under the regular general district court rules.

Filing Fees

When you file a small claims civil warrant, you pay the clerk a filing fee that is later taxed as costs in the case (§16.1-122.3(B)). The amount is set by statute and includes sheriff service and local add-ons, so it differs slightly by locality; the Virginia Judicial System’s General District Court Civil Filing Fee Calculation tool gives the exact figure for your court. If you cannot afford the fee, ask the clerk about proceeding without payment of fees. If you win, the costs are added to the judgment.

How to File a Virginia Small Claim, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Virginia generally allows five years for written contracts and three years for oral contracts; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Choose the right form. Use a Small Claims Warrant in Debt for money, or a Warrant in Detinue to recover specific personal property worth $5,000 or less.
  4. File in the right general district court. Usually where the defendant lives or does business, or where the claim arose.
  5. Pick the trial date. Under §16.1-122.3(B), the plaintiff selects a hearing time at least five days after service, subject to the clerk’s approval. The chief judge can limit how many cases one person sets on a single day.
  6. Service. The court has the warrant served on the defendant by the same method used in general district court, usually the sheriff, along with the small claims information sheet if applicable.
  7. Prepare and go to trial. The trial is held on the first return date (§16.1-122.3(E)). Continuances are granted only for good cause.

At Trial

Under §16.1-122.5, witnesses are sworn, and the general district court judge conducts the trial in an informal manner so as to do substantial justice between the parties. The Virginia courts’ small claims procedures guide explains that the judge has discretion to admit evidence that helps decide the case. Tips:

  • Prove both that you are owed money and how much: the plaintiff must prove the amount due, not just that the defendant is at fault.
  • Bring contracts, invoices, receipts, photos and texts, with copies for the judge and the other side.
  • Bring witnesses in person; they will be sworn.

The only pleadings allowed are the warrant, and the defendant’s answer, grounds of defense and any counterclaim up to $5,000 (§16.1-122.3(F)).

Appeals: A New Trial in Circuit Court

Under Va. Code §16.1-106, a party can appeal any general district court judgment where the amount in controversy is more than $20. The appeal must be noted within 10 days after the judgment, and the case is heard de novo in circuit court, where formal rules of evidence apply and a jury may be requested.

To perfect the appeal, the appealing party must post a bond within 30 days of the judgment (§16.1-107) and pay the writ tax and costs. A plaintiff whose case involved no counterclaim does not need to post a bond, and an indigent defendant is also excused from the bond.

Collecting a Virginia Judgment

Small claims judgments are collected using the general district court’s procedures (§16.1-122.6):

  • Interrogatories (debtor’s examination). The debtor must answer questions under oath about income and assets.
  • Wage garnishment. The most that can be garnished each week is the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or the amount above 40 times the federal or Virginia minimum wage, whichever minimum is higher (Va. Code §34-29).
  • Bank garnishment and writ of fieri facias. Reach bank accounts and non-exempt personal property.
  • Judgment lien. Docketing the judgment in the circuit court clerk’s office creates a lien on the debtor’s real estate in that jurisdiction.

Interest. The judgment rate is 6% a year, or a higher lawful contract rate if the judgment arises from a contract that sets one (Va. Code §6.2-302).

Small Claims or General District Court?

For claims of $5,000 or less, you can choose either small claims court or the regular general district court. Small claims is usually the better choice when you are confident presenting your own case and want a quick, informal trial without lawyers on the other side. The regular general district court makes more sense when you expect the defendant to hire a lawyer anyway, when you want a lawyer yourself, or when your claim is larger than $5,000. Remember that the defendant can move a small claims case into general district court before the decision, so prepare your evidence as if the hearing could become more formal.

Common Virginia Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Virginia landlords must return the deposit, with an itemized list of any deductions, within 45 days after the tenancy ends or the tenant moves out, whichever is later (Va. Code §55.1-1226). Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Personal property. A warrant in detinue can recover specific items, such as tools or furniture, worth up to $5,000.
  • Personal loans. Written proof such as a promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Contractor and repair disputes. Contracts, payments, photos and a second estimate.

Common Mistakes in Virginia Small Claims

  • Using an outdated or proposed limit. The limit is $5,000 until the Code changes. Filing a $10,000 small claim will not work.
  • Bringing a lawyer. Lawyers cannot represent parties in small claims court; if you want one, file in general district court instead.
  • Being surprised by removal. A defendant can move the case to general district court before the decision. Be ready for a more formal hearing if that happens.
  • Proving fault but not the amount. The judge needs evidence of the exact sum owed.

How Virginia Compares

Virginia’s $5,000 small claims limit is among the lowest in the country, half of North Carolina’s $10,000 and a third of Georgia’s $15,000, although its general district court handles much larger claims with more formal rules. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Virginia?

$5,000, exclusive of interest (Va. Code §16.1-122.2). A 2026 bill to raise it to $25,000 did not change the Code.

Can I have a lawyer in Virginia small claims court?

No. All parties represent themselves, although a business can be represented by an owner, officer, member or employee. A defendant can remove the case to general district court, with a lawyer, before the judge decides.

When is the trial in Virginia small claims court?

On the first return date, which the plaintiff selects at least five days after service, with the clerk’s approval.

How do I appeal a Virginia small claims decision?

Note the appeal within 10 days after the judgment and post any required bond within 30 days. The case is heard again from the beginning in circuit court.

How much of my wages can be garnished in Virginia?

No more than the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or the amount above 40 times the federal or Virginia minimum wage, whichever minimum is higher.

What interest does a Virginia judgment earn?

6% a year, or a higher contract rate if the judgment arises from a contract that sets one.
