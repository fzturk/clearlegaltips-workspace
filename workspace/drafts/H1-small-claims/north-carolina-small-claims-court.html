The short version (2026): North Carolina small claims court is run by magistrates in each county’s district court and hears claims up to $10,000 (G.S. 7A-210). The filing fee is a flat $96, plus about $30 per defendant for sheriff service. Trial is set within 30 days of filing, there is no jury, and either side can appeal within 10 days for a new trial before a district court judge or jury. Judgments earn 8% interest.

North Carolina’s small claims system is fast. A case is usually tried within a month of filing, before a magistrate in the county where the defendant lives. The same courts handle a large volume of landlord evictions (called summary ejectment), so small claims courtrooms are busy and hearings are short.

This guide covers the North Carolina small claims limit, the flat filing fee, service, what happens at trial, the 10-day appeal, and collection, including North Carolina’s strong protection for wages. Rules link to Chapter 7A, Article 19 of the North Carolina General Statutes and the North Carolina Judicial Branch, checked in September 2026.

The North Carolina Limit: $10,000

Under G.S. 7A-210, a small claim action is a civil action where:

  • the amount in controversy does not exceed $10,000;
  • the main relief sought is money, the recovery of specific personal property, or summary ejectment (eviction), or a combination of these; and
  • the plaintiff asks for assignment to a magistrate by marking the complaint “Small Claim.”

The limit was last raised in 2013 (Session Law 2013-159). The chief district judge in each district decides by order or rule which small claims are assigned to magistrates (G.S. 7A-211), so check with the clerk of superior court in your county. If your claim is over $10,000, you can reduce it and give up the difference, or file in district court as a regular civil action.

Filing Fee and Service Costs

The North Carolina Judicial Branch lists a statewide small claims filing fee of $96, the same for every claim amount. To serve the defendant, the sheriff typically charges $30 per defendant, or you can use certified mail. If you cannot afford the costs, you can ask to file as an indigent using the court’s form. If you win, the magistrate can order the defendant to pay your court costs.

Send a demand letter before you file. A clear written demand with a deadline settles many North Carolina disputes, and it gives the magistrate proof you tried to resolve the problem first. LawDepot’s guided payment demand letter takes about ten minutes.

Where to File

Under G.S. 7A-213, you file the complaint with the clerk of superior court in the county where the defendant (or one of the defendants) resides. A magistrate can only be assigned the case if at least one defendant is a genuine resident of that county (G.S. 7A-211). If the defendant lives out of state, the case generally belongs in regular district court.

The Judicial Branch notes that local requirements vary, including how many copies of each form you need, so check with your county clerk’s office before filing.

How to File a North Carolina Small Claim, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. North Carolina generally allows only three years for contract claims and property damage; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Complete the complaint. Use the Judicial Branch’s Complaint for Money Owed or the complaint form that fits your claim, and write “Small Claim” on it. Include the defendant’s full legal name and address.
  4. Complete the magistrate summons. The clerk issues it once the case is assigned.
  5. File and pay. Pay the $96 fee and the service fee for each defendant. If a defendant is on active military duty, the court requires information about their status under the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act.
  6. Service. Under G.S. 7A-217, the defendant can be served by personal delivery, by leaving copies with a suitable adult at the defendant’s home, or by registered or certified mail, signature confirmation or designated delivery service.
  7. Receive the trial date. After service, the clerk sends you written notice naming the magistrate and the time, date and place of trial (G.S. 7A-213).
  8. Prepare and go to trial. Bring your evidence and witnesses.

At Trial

G.S. 7A-214 requires the trial to be set no later than 30 days after the action begins. If the trial date is less than five days after the defendant was served, the magistrate must continue the case. The parties can agree to a different date, and the magistrate can grant continuances for good cause.

Under G.S. 7A-222, trial before a magistrate is without a jury, and, unlike many states, the rules of evidence that apply in regular civil cases are observed. That makes preparation more important:

  • Bring original documents and copies: contracts, invoices, receipts, photos and texts.
  • Bring witnesses in person. A written statement from someone who is not there may be objected to as hearsay.
  • If you need a repair estimate to prove damages, consider bringing the person who prepared it, or ask the clerk how your magistrate handles written estimates.

After the plaintiff’s evidence, the magistrate can dismiss the case if the plaintiff has not made out a basic (prima facie) case. Otherwise the defendant presents evidence, and the magistrate rules at the end or within 10 days.

Appeals: A New Trial in District Court

Either side can appeal a magistrate’s judgment for a trial de novo before a district court judge or a jury (G.S. 7A-228). The deadlines are strict:

  • Give notice of appeal orally in court when the judgment is announced, or file written notice with the clerk of superior court within 10 days after the judgment and serve it on the other parties.
  • Pay the appeal costs within 20 days after the judgment (10 days in an eviction case), or the appeal is automatically dismissed, unless you qualify as indigent.

Because the appeal is a new trial, the district court hears the whole case again. If the defendant appeals, be ready to present your evidence a second time.

Collecting a North Carolina Judgment

A North Carolina small claims judgment is enforced like other district court judgments, but collection has real limits:

  • Wages are hard to reach. Under G.S. 1-362, a debtor’s earnings for personal services within the 60 days before a collection order cannot be taken when the debtor shows they are needed to support a family. In practice, ordinary wage garnishment for consumer debts is largely unavailable in North Carolina.
  • Writ of execution. After the debtor has had a chance to claim exemptions, the sheriff can levy on non-exempt property, including bank funds.
  • Judgment lien. A docketed judgment is a lien on the debtor’s real estate in the county.
  • Interest. Judgments bear interest at the legal rate of 8% a year (G.S. 24-1), unless a contract sets a different rate.

Before You File: Can You Actually Collect?

Because North Carolina protects most wages from creditors, a small claims judgment is worth the most when the defendant has something else to reach: money in a bank account, a business that takes payments, a vehicle without a loan, or real estate. Before paying the filing and service fees, write down what you know about where the defendant banks, works and owns property. If the defendant has nothing collectible today, a judgment can still be useful, because it lasts for years and becomes a lien on real estate the defendant later buys in the county. But a negotiated payment plan, put in writing and signed, may bring in money sooner than a judgment would.

Common North Carolina Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. A landlord must return the deposit or give an itemized accounting within 30 days after the tenancy ends and the tenant gives back possession; if the landlord’s claim cannot be determined in 30 days, an interim accounting is due then and a final one within 60 days (G.S. 42-52). Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Evictions. Landlords use small claims for summary ejectment; the rules have their own timelines.
  • Personal loans. Written proof such as a promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Contractor and repair disputes. Contracts, payments, photos and estimates, plus a witness who can explain the estimate.

Common Mistakes in North Carolina Small Claims

  • Filing in the wrong county. The claim belongs in the county where a defendant lives.
  • Relying on paperwork alone. Magistrates apply the rules of evidence. Bring the people who can testify to key facts.
  • Waiting too long. North Carolina’s three-year limit for most contract claims is shorter than many states.
  • Missing the appeal costs. Giving notice of appeal is not enough; the costs must be paid within 20 days.

How North Carolina Compares

North Carolina’s $10,000 limit matches the most common limit nationally and is above South Carolina ($7,500) and Virginia ($5,000), but below Tennessee ($25,000) and Georgia ($15,000). Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in North Carolina?

$10,000 (G.S. 7A-210). The claim must be mainly for money, personal property or eviction, and marked “Small Claim.”

How much does it cost to file small claims in North Carolina?

$96 for any amount, plus about $30 per defendant for sheriff service. You can ask to file as an indigent if you cannot afford it.

How fast is a North Carolina small claims trial?

The trial must be set no later than 30 days after the case starts, though it is continued if the defendant was served less than five days before trial.

Is there a jury in North Carolina small claims court?

No. A magistrate decides the case. A jury is available if the case is appealed for a new trial in district court.

How do I appeal a North Carolina small claims judgment?

Announce the appeal in court or file written notice within 10 days, serve the other parties, and pay the appeal costs within 20 days (10 days in eviction cases).

Can I garnish wages in North Carolina?

Generally not for ordinary debts. Earnings needed to support the debtor’s family are protected, so collection usually focuses on bank accounts, non-exempt property and real estate liens.
