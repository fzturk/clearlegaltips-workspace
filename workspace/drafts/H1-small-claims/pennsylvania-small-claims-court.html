The short version (2026): Pennsylvania small claims court is the magisterial district court (Philadelphia Municipal Court in Philadelphia), and both hear claims up to $12,000. In 2026, statewide costs to file are $68.50 to $171 depending on the amount, plus service. The hearing is set 12 to 60 days after filing, either side can appeal within 30 days for a new trial in the Court of Common Pleas, and wages generally cannot be garnished to collect an ordinary debt.

Pennsylvania does not use the name “small claims court” for most of the state. Civil claims up to $12,000 go to the local magisterial district judge (MDJ), an elected judge who handles traffic, landlord-tenant and small civil cases in each district. In Philadelphia, the same kinds of cases are heard in the Small Claims Division of the Philadelphia Municipal Court.

This guide covers the Pennsylvania small claims limit, the 2026 cost schedule, how service works, what happens if the defendant does not show up, how appeals work, and what you can realistically collect. Rules link to the Pennsylvania Rules of Civil Procedure Governing Actions Before Magisterial District Judges and the Pennsylvania statutes, checked in September 2026.

The Pennsylvania Limit: $12,000

Magisterial district judges have jurisdiction over civil claims where the sum demanded does not exceed $12,000, exclusive of interest and costs (42 Pa.C.S. §1515(a)(3)). Philadelphia Municipal Court has the same $12,000 limit for its small claims cases. Both limits took effect in January 2011 and have not changed since.

If your claim is larger, you can reduce it to $12,000 and give up the difference, or file in the Court of Common Pleas, where many counties first send cases up to a set amount to compulsory arbitration.

2026 Filing Costs

Unlike many states, Pennsylvania sets magisterial district court costs statewide and adjusts them every year. For calendar year 2026, 42 Pa.C.S. §1725.1 (as published in 204 Pa. Code §29.402) sets these costs for civil cases:

Amount of the claim2026 cost
$500 or less$68.50
$500.01 to $2,000$91.00
$2,000.01 to $4,000$114.00
$4,000.01 to $12,000$171.00
Order of execution (after judgment)$51.50

These costs do not include postage and service. Service is usually made by a sheriff or certified constable, whose fees vary by county, so ask the MDJ office for the total before you file. If you cannot afford the costs, you can ask to proceed in forma pauperis. If you win, the costs are usually added to the judgment.

Where to File

You file with the magisterial district court for the district where the defendant can be served, or where the transaction or occurrence took place, following the venue rules for MDJ cases. Each county has many magisterial districts, so use the Unified Judicial System’s court locator or call the county court administrator to find the right office. In Philadelphia, file with the Philadelphia Municipal Court Civil Division.

Individuals can represent themselves, and lawyers are allowed. Under Rule 207, a corporation or partnership can appear through an officer or partner, or through an employee or agent who has personal knowledge of the facts and written authorization. An individual can even send a representative with personal knowledge and written authorization.

How to File in Pennsylvania, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Pennsylvania generally allows four years for contract claims and two years for injury claims; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Fill out the civil complaint. The MDJ office provides the statewide civil complaint form. Give the defendant’s full legal name and an address where they can be served.
  4. File and pay. File at the magisterial district court and pay the 2026 cost plus service fees.
  5. The court sets the hearing. Under Rule 305, the hearing is set not less than 12 or more than 60 days after filing.
  6. Service. Under Rule 307, the complaint must be served at least 10 days before the hearing, normally by the sheriff or a certified constable. For out-of-county defendants, the MDJ may use certified mail instead.
  7. Watch for a Notice of Intent to Defend. The defendant is told to notify the court in writing if they plan to contest the claim. If they do, the court will let you know.
  8. Attend the hearing. Bring your evidence and witnesses.

At the Hearing (and If Someone Doesn’t Show)

MDJ hearings are informal. The judge hears the plaintiff first, then the defendant, and often decides the case on the spot or shortly after. Bring three copies of each document in date order, and show how you calculated the amount.

Rule 319 explains what happens when someone is missing:

  • Defendant does not appear: the judge enters judgment for the plaintiff or continues the case for cause. If the amount is certain (like an unpaid invoice), damages are assessed from your papers; if not, the judge holds a short hearing on the amount only.
  • Plaintiff does not appear after the defendant gave notice of intent to defend: the judge enters judgment for the defendant or continues the case.

Appeals: A New Trial in the Court of Common Pleas

Either side can appeal an MDJ money judgment to the Court of Common Pleas, where the case is heard again from the beginning. Under Rule 1002, the notice of appeal must be filed with the prothonotary within 30 days after the judgment is entered, together with a copy of the Notice of Judgment. Late appeals are not accepted without leave of court.

Rule 1004 adds a trap for the unwary:

  • If the plaintiff appeals, the plaintiff must file a formal complaint in the Court of Common Pleas within 20 days after filing the notice of appeal.
  • If the defendant appeals, the defendant must file a praecipe with the notice of appeal asking the prothonotary to rule the plaintiff to file a complaint within 20 days. If the plaintiff then fails to file, the case can end in a judgment of non pros against the plaintiff.

If you win at the MDJ level and the defendant appeals, watch your mail closely for that rule to file a complaint.

Collecting a Pennsylvania Judgment

Pennsylvania offers some tools, and one major limitation:

  • No wage garnishment for ordinary debts. Under 42 Pa.C.S. §8127, wages are exempt from attachment except in specific cases: support and divorce proceedings, board for four weeks or less, certain residential landlord judgments (limited to 10% of net wages), Pennsylvania Higher Education Assistance Agency student loans, and criminal restitution. A judgment for a personal loan or repair bill generally cannot be collected from a paycheck.
  • Order of execution. After the appeal period, you can ask the MDJ for an order of execution ($51.50 in 2026) so a constable or sheriff can levy non-exempt personal property.
  • Transfer to Common Pleas. Filing a certified copy of the MDJ judgment with the prothonotary makes it a lien on the debtor’s real estate in that county and opens up bank garnishment through the Court of Common Pleas.
  • Interest. Judgments bear interest at the legal rate of 6% a year (42 Pa.C.S. §8101 and 41 P.S. §202).

Common Pennsylvania Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. A Pennsylvania landlord must return the deposit, or send a written list of damages, within 30 days after the lease ends and the tenant provides a forwarding address. A landlord who fails to do so can owe double the amount wrongfully withheld. Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Contractor disputes. Bring the contract, the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act registration number if applicable, payments, photos and a second estimate.
  • Personal loans. Written proof such as a promissory note, texts or bank transfers.
  • Vehicle damage. Repair estimates, photos and the police report number.

Common Mistakes in Pennsylvania

  • Filing in the wrong district. Pennsylvania has hundreds of magisterial districts. Confirm the right office before paying.
  • Missing the 20-day complaint on appeal. A plaintiff who appeals and forgets to file the complaint in Common Pleas can lose the appeal.
  • Counting on wage garnishment. In most cases it is not available. Think about bank accounts and property before you sue.
  • Ignoring the Notice of Intent to Defend. If the defendant files one, expect a contested hearing and prepare as if the case will be decided on your documents alone.
  • Sending an unauthorized representative. Under Rule 207, a representative who is not a lawyer needs personal knowledge of the facts and written authorization. Bring the authorization to court.
  • Losing track of the defendant’s address. Service must be completed at least 10 days before the hearing. A bad address means a continuance and another round of service fees.
  • Waiting to execute. Judgments do not collect themselves; request the order of execution once the appeal period ends.

How Pennsylvania Compares

Pennsylvania’s $12,000 limit is above the most common national figure of $10,000 and double neighboring Ohio’s $6,000, though below New Jersey’s $20,000 Special Civil Part (New Jersey’s true small claims section stops at $5,000). Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Pennsylvania?

$12,000, not counting interest and costs, in both the magisterial district courts and the Philadelphia Municipal Court.

How much does it cost to file a small claim in Pennsylvania in 2026?

$68.50 for claims of $500 or less, $91 up to $2,000, $114 up to $4,000 and $171 up to $12,000, plus postage and sheriff or constable service fees.

How soon is the hearing?

The magisterial district judge sets it 12 to 60 days after the complaint is filed, and the defendant must be served at least 10 days before the hearing.

Can I appeal a magisterial district judge’s decision?

Yes. Either side can appeal to the Court of Common Pleas within 30 days for a new trial. A plaintiff who appeals must file a complaint there within 20 days.

Can I garnish wages in Pennsylvania after winning?

Usually not. Pennsylvania exempts wages from garnishment except for support, board, certain residential landlord judgments, state-agency student loans and criminal restitution.

What interest does a Pennsylvania judgment earn?

The legal rate of 6% a year, unless another statute or a contract provides otherwise.
