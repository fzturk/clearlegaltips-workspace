The short version (2026): Florida small claims court handles claims up to $8,000, not counting costs, interest and attorney fees. Filing fees are $55, $80, $175 or $300 depending on the amount. Your first court date is a pretrial conference set within 50 days of filing, where many cases settle through mediation. Since July 1, 2026, you must serve the defendant within 120 days or the case can be dismissed.

Florida small claims cases are filed in county court under the Florida Small Claims Rules, a short set of procedures designed for “the simple, speedy, and inexpensive trial” of everyday disputes. The system has one feature that makes it different from most states: before any trial, both sides must attend a pretrial conference, which is where most cases are resolved.

This guide covers the Florida small claims limit, fees, where to file, how service works after the July 2026 rule change, what happens at the pretrial conference and trial, and how collection works in a state with strong protections for heads of household. Rules link to the Florida Small Claims Rules and the Florida Statutes, checked in September 2026.

The Florida Small Claims Limit: $8,000

Under Florida Small Claims Rule 7.010(b), the rules apply to civil actions in county court that demand money or property worth $8,000 or less, exclusive of costs, interest and attorneys’ fees. Florida raised the figure from $5,000 to $8,000 in 2020.

Because attorney fees and interest are excluded, a claim can end in a judgment above $8,000 when a contract or statute allows those extras. What matters is that the underlying demand is $8,000 or less. If it is larger, you can give up the excess and stay in small claims, or file a regular county civil case, where the more formal Florida Rules of Civil Procedure apply.

The Clerk of Court cannot help you prepare a claim above $8,000; Florida’s clerks may assist self-represented filers only within small claims.

Filing Fees and Service Costs

Filing fees are set by Florida Statutes §34.041 and collected by each county clerk. The Miami-Dade Clerk publishes the totals most clerks charge:

Amount of the claimFiling fee
Up to $100$55
$100.01 to $500$80
$500.01 to $2,500$175
$2,500.01 to $8,000$300

Service is extra. For a Florida resident, the clerk can serve by certified mail (Rule 7.070), which is the cheapest route; the mailing cost is added to the filing fee. If certified mail fails or the defendant lives out of state, use the sheriff or a certified process server. If you cannot afford the costs, ask the clerk for an Application for Determination of Civil Indigent Status. If you win, costs are normally added to the judgment.

Where to File

You file with the Clerk of the County Court in the right county. Rule 7.060 and Florida’s venue statutes allow suit where:

  • the contract was entered into;
  • an unsecured promissory note was signed, or where the maker lives;
  • the property is located, in a suit to recover property or foreclose a lien;
  • the event giving rise to the suit occurred;
  • any defendant resides;
  • a contract says suit may be brought; or
  • payment was to be made, in a suit for money due with no venue clause.

The summons tells the defendant about these venue rights in bold type, and a defendant sued in the wrong county can ask to move the case.

Who Can Appear: People, Businesses and Lawyers

Individuals can represent themselves, and lawyers are allowed. A business entity recognized under Florida law can be represented at any stage by a principal with authority to bind it, or by an employee authorized in writing by a principal (Rule 7.050(a)(2)). A court can require a debt-collection business that bought or manages a claim to hire a lawyer.

If you do not have a lawyer, you must sign the statement of claim yourself and include your address and phone number. You also file a Designation of E-mail Address for a Party Not Represented by an Attorney if you want to receive papers by email.

How to File a Florida Small Claims Case, Step by Step

  1. Ask for payment in writing. Send a demand letter and keep proof of delivery.
  2. Check the deadline. Florida generally allows five years for written contracts and four years for oral agreements; see our statute of limitations on debt by state table.
  3. Prepare the Statement of Claim. State who owes what and why, in concise form. If the claim is based on a written document (a contract, lease or promissory note), attach a copy (Rule 7.050(a)(1)).
  4. Prepare the Summons/Notice to Appear. Most clerks provide the form. It sets the date of the pretrial conference.
  5. File and pay. File with the county clerk in person, by mail or through the Florida Courts E-Filing Portal.
  6. Serve the defendant within 120 days. Under Rule 7.070(b), effective July 1, 2026, if service is not made within 120 days of filing, the court must dismiss the case without prejudice or drop that defendant, unless you ask for more time before the deadline.
  7. Attend the pretrial conference. Both sides must appear, in person or by approved remote technology.
  8. Go to trial if the case does not settle. The court sets a trial date, usually within a few weeks.

The Pretrial Conference: Where Most Cases End

Rule 7.090(b) requires the summons to set the first appearance as a pretrial conference, scheduled by the clerk no more than 50 days after the case is filed. It may be run by court staff under a judge’s supervision. At the conference the court will:

  • identify the issues that are actually in dispute;
  • check whether the parties can agree on some facts;
  • refer the case to mediation, often on the spot; and
  • set a trial date if the case does not settle.

Do not skip it. If the plaintiff fails to appear, the case can be dismissed; if the defendant fails to appear, the plaintiff can receive a default judgment. If you settle in mediation, the agreement is written up and signed that day, and it can be enforced like a judgment if the other side does not follow it.

Bring your evidence to the pretrial conference as if it were the trial. Mediators and the other side take a well-documented claim more seriously, and it is the best moment to settle.

At Trial

Small claims trials are before a county judge, usually without a jury (a jury can be demanded under the rules, but it is rare in practice). Tips:

  • Open with the amount and the reason in one or two sentences.
  • Have three copies of each exhibit, in date order: one for you, one for the judge, one for the other side.
  • Bring witnesses in person if you can; written statements may carry less weight.
  • Show how you calculated damages, such as a repair estimate, invoice or ledger.

New Trial Motions and Appeals

A party who believes the judge made a mistake has two options:

  • Motion for new trial within 15 days after the judgment is filed (Rule 7.180), stating the grounds with particularity.
  • Appeal to the circuit court within 30 days of the judgment under the Florida Rules of Appellate Procedure. An appeal reviews the record for legal error; it is not a new trial, so you usually need a record of what was said at the hearing.

Collecting a Florida Judgment

If the defendant does not pay, Florida gives you several tools, but also protects many debtors:

  • Fact information sheet. The court can order the debtor to complete a financial disclosure form listing employers, bank accounts and property.
  • Judgment lien. Recording a certified copy of the judgment in the county official records creates a lien on non-homestead real estate. Filing a judgment lien certificate with the Department of State reaches certain personal property.
  • Writ of execution and bank garnishment. The sheriff can levy non-exempt property, and a writ of garnishment can reach non-exempt bank funds.
  • Wage garnishment, with a big exception. Under Florida Statutes §222.11, all disposable earnings of a head of family earning $750 a week or less are exempt, and a head of family earning more cannot be garnished unless they agreed in writing. A “head of family” is anyone providing more than half the support for a child or other dependent.
  • Homestead. Florida’s constitutional homestead exemption protects a debtor’s primary residence from most judgment creditors.

Interest. Judgments earn interest at a rate the Chief Financial Officer resets every quarter under Florida Statutes §55.03. For the quarter beginning October 1, 2026, the rate is 7.87% a year.

Common Florida Small Claims Cases

  • Security deposits. Florida landlords must return a deposit within 15 days, or send written notice of a claim within 30 days, after the tenant leaves (Fla. Stat. §83.49). Start with a security deposit demand letter.
  • Contractor and repair disputes. Bring the estimate or contract, payment records, photos and a second estimate for correcting the work.
  • Unpaid personal loans. A written promissory note must be attached to the statement of claim.
  • Auto damage. Repair estimates, photos and the crash report number.

How Florida Compares

Florida’s $8,000 limit is below the most common national figure of $10,000 and far below neighbors like Georgia ($15,000). Its tiered filing fees reach $300, the highest statewide small claims fee we found, and its mandatory pretrial conference is unusual. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the small claims limit in Florida?

$8,000, not counting court costs, interest and attorney fees (Florida Small Claims Rule 7.010).

How much does it cost to file small claims in Florida?

$55 for claims up to $100, $80 up to $500, $175 up to $2,500 and $300 up to $8,000, plus the cost of service. Certified mail through the clerk is usually the cheapest service option for Florida residents.

What happens at a Florida small claims pretrial conference?

The court narrows the issues, often sends the parties to mediation, and sets a trial date if there is no settlement. It is scheduled within 50 days of filing, and missing it can lead to dismissal or a default judgment.

How long do I have to serve the defendant in Florida small claims?

120 days from filing, under Rule 7.070 as amended effective July 1, 2026. If you need more time, ask the court before the 120 days run out.

Can I garnish wages in Florida after winning in small claims?

Sometimes. Florida exempts all wages of a head of family earning $750 a week or less, and more than that only with the debtor’s written waiver. Other debtors’ wages can be garnished within federal limits.

Can I appeal a Florida small claims judgment?

Yes. You can move for a new trial within 15 days, or appeal to the circuit court within 30 days. An appeal reviews legal errors in the record; it is not a new trial.
